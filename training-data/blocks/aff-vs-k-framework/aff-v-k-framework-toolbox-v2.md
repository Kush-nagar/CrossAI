The benefits of procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making
Procedural fairness theory is one of the most prominent and extensively documented theoretical frameworks in psychological literature (Colquitt et al., 2023; De Cremer & Tyler, 2007; De Cremer & Van Knippenberg, 2002; Van Dijke & De Cremer, 2008). The central tenet of this influential account is that authorities can increase their <<Table 1 Omitted>> legitimacy as a decision-maker and facilitate acceptance of their decisions when they apply the principles of procedural fairness to their decision processes (Tyler, 2006; Verboon & van Dijke, 2011); that is, when they refrain from showing bias towards one of the stakeholders, grant all those involved the possibility to voice their opinions, use only accurate information and adhere to strictly ethical guidelines to reach a conclusion, allow for evaluation, and if necessary, modification of past decisions (Leventhal, 1980; Thibaut & Walker, 1975). Group authorities can reap further benefits from enacting procedural fairness which go beyond sheer decisional compliance, because fairness perceptions have also been related to, among others, group cohesion (e.g., Lind & Tyler, 1988), commitment to the group as an entity (De Cremer et al., 2010) and cooperation among group members (De Cremer & Tyler, 2005).
Most relevant to the present research, it has also been shown that procedural fairness is a powerful approach to handle disputes and disagreements (Huo & Tyler, 2000, 2001; Huo et al., 1996; Tyler et al., 1998). The fact that procedural fairness has the capacity to defuse potentially conflictual situations seems particularly relevant for societal decision-makers in modern-day diverse societies, which, as we have described above, are characterised by the presence of many ethnic-cultural groups (Coleman, 2013; Pew Research Center, 2008; U.S. Census Bureau, 2008). The sheer fact that each of these minority groups have their own needs, demands and interests makes it particularly tedious for societal decision-makers to deal with ethnic-cultural issues, that is, issues related in some way to ethnic, cultural and linguistic matters and in which ethnic-cultural minority members are thus the stakeholders. Consider, for example, a situation whereby a local political council has to decide whether or not to allow one of their administrative staff members working at the town hall to wear a headscarf. Or think about the recent debates in Western countries about whether or not to remove statues and other monuments reminiscent of colonial times (Carrega & Allen, 2020). Other real-life examples include the organisation of religious slaughter during the Eid festival in Belgium (Wax, 2019), language discrimination at schools (Murillo & Smith, 2011) and allowing prayer during work hours (Rasking et al., 2013). In all the above cases, the interests of entire minority groups are at stake, and authorities must carefully navigate through the decision process surrounding these issues, in order to minimise frustration and discontent among members of the involved minority group and hence, to avoid societal turmoil.
Particularly revealing in this regard is the recent Sanda Dia case in Belgium. Sanda was an undergraduate student with a migration background who died as a result of physical abuse during a thirty-hour student baptism. After this tragic incident had occurred, his fellow students who had been in control of the baptism ceremony were taken to court on charges of unvoluntary manslaughter with suspected racist motives. Despite the seriousness of the offence, all culprits received rather mild sentences, which sparked serious popular anger and civil unrest – particularly among ethnic-cultural minorities (Decré, 2023). In response to these public and online displays of discontent, however, representatives of the court published an open letter wherein they explained the reasons behind the decision and they organised open debate sessions, allowing people to voice their opinions about the case. In the aftermath of these initiatives, a slow but steady decrease in minority moral outrage about the trial was observed (Bauwens, 2023). In this way, the Sanda Dia case illustrates the importance of fair and unbiased procedures – and clear communication about them – for societal actors when they resolve (sensitive) ethnic-cultural issues.
Procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making: proximal outcomes
Seminal work has indeed documented the usefulness of procedural fairness enactment in dealing with such ethnic-cultural issues -see Table 1 (Section 1.1) for a brief overview. Platow et al. (2008), for example, studied the impact of procedurally fair leadership in the context of a real-life intergroup dispute, i.e., a series of mob riots between Anglo-Australians and Australians of Middle Eastern descent in December 2005 in Sydney, Australia. They presented their participants with vignettes describing how an (Anglo-Australian vs Middle Eastern Australian) authority figure had dealt with these large scale physical intergroup confrontations. The results revealed that <mark>participants</mark> themselves were <mark>less likely to engage in</mark> similar <mark>violent</mark> protest <mark>actions</mark> when an ingroup authority had granted (vs denied) their ethnic-cultural group voice in the course of the decision process. In a similar vein, Peate and colleagues (Peate et al., 2008) manipulated Australian citizens’ collective voice perceptions with respect to the settlement of a land claim dispute between the traditional Australian community and non-Indigenous Australians. It was shown that providing voice to Indigenous participants’ ethnic-cultural group increased their acceptance of the outcome of the decision and curbed their willingness to protest against it. In addition to this, further work by Platow et al. (1998, 2013) in laboratory-induced intergroup settings has analogously revealed more positive reactions to procedurally fair versus unfair leaders (in terms of leader trustworthiness, respect, etc.).
Procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making: distal outcomes
Taken together, these seminal studies clearly illustrate the importance of procedural fairness in (ethnic-cultural) intergroup issues and/or disputes. Yet, it should be noted that this research has focused on proximal outcomes or reactions that are directly related to the decision procedure, e.g., evaluations of the decision-making authority and acceptance of his/her decision. Complementing these seminal findings, a more recent line of research – summarised in Table 1 – has extensively shown that members of fairness recipient minority groups can also respond to the perception of ethnic- cultural procedural fairness in ways that are beneficial to social cohesion and social capital in diverse societies (Dierckx et al., 2020, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2023, 2023; Valcke et al., 2020, 2020). In the next subsections, we describe three broad classes of empirical insights that follow from this new body of research findings, and we will argue why they are relevant for decision-makers in diverse societies.
Minority member societal belongingness and identification
A first series of studies has revealed <mark>positive relationships between</mark> ethnic- cultural <mark>procedural fairness</mark> perceptions among minority group members <mark>and</mark> their <mark>feelings of</mark> societal <mark>belongingness</mark> (i.e., feeling accepted and included by society at large) and <mark>societal identification</mark> (i.e., feeling committed to <mark>and</mark> satisfied with one’s society at large). For example, Valcke and colleagues (Valcke et al., 2020) showed that perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by societal actors lead <mark>minority</mark> group members to <mark>feel</mark> that <mark>they belong</mark> to society. In a scenario study (Study 1), conducted in a Black American sample, these scholars manipulated the perceived fairness climate (fair versus unfair) towards minorities in a fictitious society. Specifically, participants read an excerpt of an “interview” in which the interviewer probed a minority member’s perceptions of the institutions installed in this imaginary nation. In the fair climate condition, the interviewee described these institutions as fair, unbiased, and striving to implement ethical and accurate procedures when dealing with ethnic-cultural issues. Conversely, in the unfair climate condition, the interviewee described them as unfair, biased, and uninterested to implement ethical and accurate procedures with respect to the resolution of ethnic-cultural issues. Subsequently, participants were asked to imagine living in this society, and the authors probed their expected sense of belonging. The results of this mental simulation revealed stronger expectations of societal belongingness in the fair (compared to the unfair) climate condition. Importantly, these findings could not be explained in terms of participants’ own procedural fairness perceptions (i.e., with respect to the treatment of African Americans in the U.S.). Further corroboration for these results was provided by a cross-sectional study, wherein African Americans reported on their perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by the main institutions in the U.S. society and their personal feelings of societal belongingness. The results indeed indicated a strong positive relationship between these target variables (i.e., r = .30).
With respect to (societal) identification, Valcke et al. (2020) reported a similar pattern of procedural fairness effects. In a questionnaire study (Study 2), the authors sampled linguistic minorities at a predominantly Dutch Belgian university. These non-Dutch speaking participants completed an online survey which gauged how procedurally fairly they felt that they and their linguistic minority peers within their research group were usually treated by their supervisors. They also completed a measure which probed their feelings of identification with their research group. In a similar vein, Valcke and colleagues (Valcke et al., 2020, Study 3) also sampled Black American minority group members for a correlational study. These participants completed measures tapping into their procedural fairness climate perceptions (with respect to societal actors) and their feelings of identification with society. Across both studies, Valcke et al. (2020) consistently observed strong and positive relations (rs .32–.46) between participants’ self-reported procedural fairness climate perceptions and their identification with the group represented by the relevant, study-specific authority (i.e., one’s research group in the academic linguistic minority sample and the U.S. society in the Black US sample). Taken together, these findings thus indicate that procedural fairness perceptions vis-à-vis one’s ethnic group can bolster feelings of societal belongingness and foster identification with superordinate groups, such as one’s research entity or society at large.
Positive personal outcomes for minority group members
Importantly, these heightened feelings of belongingness and identification among minority group members, in turn, have been shown to yield further downstream beneficial effects. In this section, we delineate a second set of studies which has revealed positive consequences situated at the personal level (i.e., for the involved minority group member). For example, Dierckx and colleagues (Dierckx et al., 2021) reported five studies showing that perceptions of procedurally fair societal institutions can effectively reduce perceived discrimination among minority group members. Of particular interest are the results of their second study, which used a modified version of Crocker and Major’s (1989) attribution-to-discrimination paradigm. In this experiment, Black Americans were invited to participate in a “text comprehension study”. They first read a bogus news article which portrayed the US societal institutions as increasingly procedurally fair (vs unfair). Subsequently, in an ostensibly unrelated task, the authors gauged how this (un)fairness prime affected participants’ interpretation of negative feedback to a fellow Black American. Specifically, they read an article describing the case of a White landlord who had refused a young Black man as a tenant for one of his houses, and the participants were invited to reflect on potential reasons for this rejection. Interestingly, participants in the fair condition were less likely to attribute this negative outcome to discrimination, compared to those in the unfair condition. Dierckx et al. (2021) referred to this remarkable result as a “trickle-down” effect, whereby the perception of procedurally fair societal institutions (situated at the “collective” societal level) culminates into reduced perceptions of group discrimination by majority group members (situated at the “interpersonal” level). Of special relevance to the present literature overview, a recent study of ours,1 conducted among Coloured South Africans, further unravelled that this negative relationship between procedural fairness and perceived group discrimination was fully mediated by societal identification. Given that perceiving discrimination against one’s ethnic-cultural group typically yields negative psychological (Kessler et al., 1999; Priest et al., 2013) and even physical (Anderson, 2013; Williams et al., 2019) consequences, these curbed perceptions of group discrimination can thus indeed be considered a desirable consequence of fair treatment by societal actors.
In corroboration with the latter premise, Valcke et al. (2020, Study 3) further observed positive relationships between Black American participants’ perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by societal institutions and their subjective well-being, subjective happiness, and psychological health (as measured by the General Health Questionnaire; Goldberg & Williams, 2000). Analogous to the findings reported above, it was shown that societal identification mediated these associations. As such, it can be concluded that <mark>minority</mark> group members’ procedural <mark>fairness </mark>perceptions <mark>curb</mark> their <mark>perceived</mark> group <mark>discrimination</mark>, <mark>and</mark> they <mark>foster</mark> their subjective well-being and <mark>psychological health</mark> – findings which can be explained in terms of enhanced societal identification. These studies clearly illustrate that ethnic-cultural procedural fairness can have a positive personal impact on minority group members living in diverse societies.

In the age of Donald Trump, Elon Musk and the rise of the far right, commentators have endlessly opined on the problems of fake news, polarisation and more. Take “debates”. They’re a central part of most election campaigns around the world, seen as so influential that they’re often governed by strict rules around media coverage and balance. There are lots of reasons why debate (and indeed, information-giving and argumentation in general) tends to be ineffective at changing people’s political beliefs. <mark>Cognitive dissonance</mark>, a phenomenon I studied as part of my PhD research, is one. This is the often unconscious psychological discomfort we feel when faced with contradictions in our own beliefs or actions, and it has been well documented. We can see cognitive dissonance and its effects at work when people rapidly “reason” in ways that are really attempts to mitigate their discomfort with new information about strongly held beliefs. “<mark>Humans are fearful of death</mark> and the sense of <mark>finality</mark> of who we are and what we’ve contributed,” explained psychological scientist Morela Hernandez (University of Virginia). “<mark>When mortality is salient</mark>, it <mark>inspires</mark> a very significant <mark>desire to build a positive legacy</mark>.”
<mark>Research suggests that reminding leaders of</mark> their own <mark>mortality</mark> may be one way to <mark>encourage them to make better</mark>, or at least <mark>less selfish, decisions</mark>.
“We are willing to give up benefits now so that others can benefit in the future,” Hernandez said. “This legacy motive is an interesting mechanism that drives stewardship behavior.”
In a series of experiments published in Psychological Science, Hernandez and colleagues predicted that prompting participants to think about death would boost their desire to leave a positive legacy through deeds that benefit others in the future.
In the first experiment, 54 college students read a short news article and then wrote a short statement about the author’s writing style. One group of students read an article titled “Person Killed in Aircraft Brake Failure Accident,” while the control group read an article titled “Has Russian Math Whiz Solved $1M Puzzle?”
Participants then saw a statement thanking them for participating and confirming that they would be entered in a lottery for a $1,000 prize. They were also told that they could proactively set aside a portion of the potential winnings to go to charity. The charity was either described as meeting the immediate needs of people in the community or as creating lasting improvements that would benefit communities in the future.
Those prompted to think of death strongly favored donating to the future-oriented charity (an average of $236 compared to $40 for the present-oriented charity). On the other hand, those in the control group showed the opposite behavior, donating far more money towards people’s immediate needs than their future needs.
In a second online experiment, 71 participants read one of the two articles from the first study. They were then asked to imagine themselves as the vice president of an energy company. The company had discovered a new, highly efficient source of energy. As a leader in the company, it was their job to decide how to distribute this resource. They could keep the new resource within their own branch of the company for future use, or they could share it with other branches of the company to be used either immediately or in the future.
Again, relative to individuals in the control condition, individuals primed with death donated more resources to recipients in the future than to those in the present. Those primed with death also contributed more to the other branches of the company relative to those in the control condition.
“Our work suggests an additional situation in which <mark>death priming</mark> can produce beneficent behavior: Not only does death priming <mark>increase</mark> the <mark>desire to help other people</mark> with whom individuals already feel a connection (e.g., those in our cultural groups), but it also induces a desire to <mark>connect with others</mark> in the future as a mechanism for establishing a legacy, <mark>which produces beneficence to</mark> these <mark>future others</mark>,” Hernandez and colleagues conclude.

I’m a researcher who studies the intersection of psychology and politics, and my work has increasingly led me to believe that our culture’s understanding of how political persuasion works is wrong. In the age of Donald Trump, Elon Musk and the rise of the far right, commentators have endlessly opined on the problems of fake news, polarisation and more. But they’ve mostly been looking in the wrong places – and have focused too much on words.
Take “debates”. They’re a central part of most election campaigns around the world, seen as so influential that they’re often governed by strict rules around media coverage and balance. Yet evidence suggests that watching <mark>debate</mark>s <mark>has no impact on opinions</mark> whatsoever. In 2019 researchers analysed 56 TV debates in 22 elections in the US, Canada, New Zealand and Europe from 1952 to 2016. The study <mark>tracked</mark> nearly <mark>100,000 respondents</mark> to see whether debates helped undecided or decided voters to make up or change their minds. They found no evidence that they did. In 2012, a reporter ran another analysis about whether debates influenced election outcomes. As he put it: “The effects of debates on eventual votes are likely mild, and, in most cases, effectively nil.”
There are lots of reasons why debate (and indeed, information-giving and argumentation in general) tends to be ineffective at changing people’s political beliefs. <mark>Cognitive dissonance</mark>, a phenomenon I studied as part of my PhD research, is one. This is the often unconscious psychological discomfort we feel when faced with contradictions in our own beliefs or actions, and it has been well documented. We can see cognitive dissonance and its effects at work when people rapidly “reason” in ways that are really attempts to mitigate their discomfort with new information about strongly held beliefs. For example, before Trump was convicted of various charges in 2024, only 17% of Republican voters believed felons should be able to be president; directly after his conviction, that number rose to 58%. To reconcile two contradictory beliefs (that presidents shouldn’t do x, and that Trump should be president), an enormous number of Republican voters simply changed their mind about the former. In fact, Republican voters shifted their views on more or less all the things Trump had been convicted of: fewer felt it was immoral to have sex with a porn star, pay someone to stay silent about an affair, or falsify a business record. Nor is this effect limited to Trump voters: research suggests <mark>we all rationalise</mark> in this way, in order <mark>to hold on to</mark> the <mark>beliefs that</mark> let us <mark>keep operating</mark> as we have been. Or, ironically, to change some of our beliefs in response to new information, but often only in order to not have to sacrifice other strongly held beliefs.
But it’s not just psychological phenomena like cognitive dissonance that make debates and arguments relatively ineffective. As I lay out in my book, probably the <mark>most important reason</mark> words don’t change minds <mark>is</mark> that two <mark>other</mark> factors carry far more influence: our social <mark>relations</mark>hips; <mark>and our </mark>own <mark>actions</mark> and experiences.
A sea of evidence demonstrates that our <mark>friends</mark> have the power to <mark>change </mark>our <mark>beliefs</mark> and behaviour – <mark>not by arguing</mark> with us, <mark>but</mark> simply <mark>by being around us</mark> or showing us new ways of living. Studies on social contact theory show that when people are set up in conditions to become friends <mark>and</mark> <mark>collab</mark>orate, they become less prejudiced against the identity groups their new friends belong to. This phenomenon probably explains many of the advances in gay rights over the past few decades: as people came out, their friends changed their views on homosexuality, resulting in one of the fastest ever recorded shifts in public opinion. Similarly, research shows people are most likely to engage in climate-friendly action (such as installing a heat pump), if their friends do – far more likely than if they are given cash rewards or other kinds of incentives. Our friends broaden our field of concern; they get us involved in the world, and they build the trust that human beings appear to require to open up to new ideas. Their indirect influence achieves more than arguments, especially from strangers, ever could. In other words: when it comes to persuasion, it’s not the conversation, it’s the relationship.
Our actions and experiences also have a profound effect, albeit often in counterintuitive ways. For example, research shows that in otherwise similar conditions, women turned away from having an abortion become somewhat less in favour of abortion rights, while women granted an abortion become somewhat more in favour. We might expect those denied would become more passionate about access, but apparently not – probably because, in part due to cognitive dissonance, people tend to bring their beliefs towards their actions, even if those actions are compelled. The influence of actions and experiences on belief systems is also evident when it comes to climate crisis: those who experience intense climate-related weather events are more likely to believe in climate change and in addressing it.
Compared with these influences, arguments alone have comparatively little power. Yet our <mark>institutions</mark> are still <mark>shaped as though words</mark> were <mark>enough</mark>. We’re infrastructurally, collectively trapped in the liberal belief that politics is mostly about talking, then sporadically voting. <mark>What’s needed </mark>by anyone hoping to promote progressive ideas <mark>is</mark> not arguments, but an <mark>infrastructure that fosters</mark> new <mark>relations</mark>hips <mark>and experiences</mark>. This could look like anything from a waiting area for parents to mingle before school lets out, to a workplace union, to a public park. These forms of infrastructure allow people to relate and act in new ways, building friendships and trust, and encouraging the actions that ultimately allow people to change their political views.
