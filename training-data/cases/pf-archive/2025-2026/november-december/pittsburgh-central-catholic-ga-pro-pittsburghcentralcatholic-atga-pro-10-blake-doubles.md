3.2. Special Marine Environmental Risks Posed by Developing FNPPs
<mark>FNPPs</mark>, similar to any other nuclear installation, may also <mark>pose risks to the environment</mark> [19]. Since the risks faced by FNPPs are a combination of nuclear safety and maritime safety risks, constructing, operating, and deploying FNPPs may encounter more safety and environmental issues than encountered by land-based nuclear power plants, posing challenges to the marine environment and marine ecosystem.
First, the <mark>daily</mark> operation, <mark>maintenance</mark>, and refueling of FNPPs may <mark>produce</mark> spent <mark>fuel and</mark> radioactive <mark>wastes</mark> [6,25]. “Radioactive wastes are not biodegradable, nor is there any possibility of removing them from the sea once they have entered it. These substances vary in their effect, but in general, they are <mark>absorbed by</mark> marine <mark>organisms</mark>, often becoming concentrated as they move up the food chain, and <mark>affecting</mark> the growth, reproduction, and <mark>mortality</mark> of marine life” [41]. Most of the environmental damage caused by the nuclear industry comes from the refueling and disposal of spent fuel and radioactive wastes [42]. For example, the incident at Chazhma Bay in August 1985 involved refueling operations. When submarine K-431 was being refueled, an accident occurred that caused the removal of reactor control rods. The accident during refueling destroyed much of the submarine, resulted in crew member casualties, and caused contamination to spread over a significant area due to ejected fresh fuel [19,43]. Moreover, the daily operation of FNPPs may affect the surrounding marine environment due to potential low-level radiation release and heat dissipation. <mark>Thermal pollution</mark> from FNPPs may <mark>affect</mark> marine flora, fauna, and <mark>ecosystems</mark> in nearby waters because water temperature change can fatally damage marine life, <mark>change</mark> the <mark>chemical composition</mark> of seawaters, <mark>and cause ecological imbalance</mark> [16,22,44]. Additionally, even the deployment of FNPPs in the ocean per se may have environmental impacts on the surrounding marine geography and ecology; these impacts include changing sediment deposition patterns in nearby waters and the unusual siltation and erosion of coasts, deltas, and beaches. FNPP accessory devices also have similar problems. For example, underwater power cables could pose disturbances to the seabed, adversely affecting marine flora, fauna, and ecosystems [35,45].
Second, when FNPPs encounter special circumstances (such as extreme weather, collisions, external threats, operational errors, severe machine damage, and fire/explosion) [46], FNPPs’ environmental risks may heighten. <mark>Once a</mark> severe <mark>accident occurs</mark>, for example, colliding with ships navigating with high navigation speed, colliding with rockets, or sinking in shallow water, there are risks of <mark>leaking</mark> radioactive <mark>pollutants</mark> into the marine environment. In the event of accidents or other catastrophic events, “nuclear damage could be of an extreme magnitude” [6]. Leaked radioactive materials from FNPPs could then quickly disperse into the ocean, potentially contaminating large areas of the sea and <mark>caus</mark>ing detrimental <mark>cross-border effects</mark> as well as serious harm to both humans and marine ecosystems [6,47]. Moreover, maritime security threats, such as terrorist attacks, armed intrusions, and the hijacking of ships, may also endanger FNPPs and surrounding waters, making physical protection against external threats vital for the deployment of FNPPs [48]. In addition, without effective training for crew members on board, <mark>human error</mark>-induced accidents are <mark>more likely</mark> to occur <mark>on FNPPs</mark>. Research shows that human errors are responsible for most ship accidents [49]. The offshore working environment on FNPPs is more stressful for humans and, compared with land-based nuclear installations, often lacks enough human resources; consequently, operators of FNPPs are more prone to making errors, <mark>inducing</mark> nuclear <mark>accidents</mark> [48]. Research shows that some radioactive leakage from nuclear-powered ships has resulted in emergency dumping [50].
Third, once the meltdown of nuclear reactors occurs, it may cause more serious maritime environmental disasters than land-based nuclear installations do. If a core meltdown were to occur at a land-based nuclear installation, “the radioactive core debris would melt into the rock below the plant, and the heat would melt the rock into an insulating glaze around the core. The radioactivity would be released slowly into the environment, which would allow for measures to counteract the radioactivity” [22]. However, if a core meltdown were to occur at an FNPP, “the radioactivity would be released immediately into the hydrosphere and would disperse more quickly. The impacts would be larger and more stressful, particularly on those species and organisms which had a low tolerance to radioactivity” [22]. Consequently, in the case of a core meltdown, the overall risks and consequences of FNPPs would be greater than those of land-based nuclear plants, as environmental pollution would become a global instead of a local problem [22,30,35].
Biodiversity loss is existential.
<mark>Torres ’16</mark> [Emile; April 11; Affiliate Scholar at the Institute for Ethics and Emerging Technologies, Founder of the X-Risks Institute, Writer Appearing in Skeptic, Free Inquiry, Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, Salon, Truthout, Erkenntnis, Metaphilosophy, “Biodiversity Loss: An Existential Risk Comparable To Climate Change,” Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, https://thebulletin.org/2016/04/biodiversity-loss-an-existential-risk-comparable-to-climate-change]
Catastrophic consequences for civilization. The consequences of this rapid pruning of the evolutionary tree of life extend beyond the obvious. There could be surprising effects of biodiversity loss that scientists are unable to fully anticipate in advance. For example, prior research has shown that localized <mark>ecosystems can undergo abrupt</mark> and <mark>irreversible shifts</mark> when they reach a tipping point. According to a 2012 paper published in Nature, there are reasons for thinking that <mark>we may</mark> be <mark>approach</mark>ing <mark>a tipping point</mark> of this sort <mark>in</mark> the <mark>global ecosystem</mark>, beyond which the consequences could be <mark>catastrophic for civilization</mark>.
As the authors write, a planetary-scale transition could precipitate “substantial losses of ecosystem services required to sustain the human population.” An ecosystem service is any ecological process that benefits humanity, such as food production and crop pollination. If the global ecosystem were to cross a tipping point and substantial ecosystem services were lost, the <mark>results could be</mark> “widespread social <mark>unrest</mark>,<mark> economic instability</mark>, and <mark>loss of</mark> human <mark>life</mark>.” According to Missouri Botanical Garden ecologist Adam Smith, one of the paper’s co-authors, this could occur in a matter of decades—far more quickly than most of the expected consequences of climate change, yet equally destructive.
<mark>Biod</mark>iversity <mark>loss is a “threat multiplier”</mark> that, by pushing societies to the brink of collapse, will exacerbate existing conflicts and introduce entirely new struggles between state and non-state actors. Indeed, it could even fuel the rise of terrorism. (After all, climate change has been linked to the emergence of ISIS in Syria, and multiple high-ranking US officials, such as former US Defense Secretary Chuck Hagel and CIA director John Brennan, have affirmed that climate change and terrorism are connected.)
The reality is that we are entering the sixth mass extinction in the 3.8-billion-year history of life on Earth, and the impact of this event could be felt by civilization “in as little as three human lifetimes,” as the aforementioned 2012 Nature paper notes. Furthermore, the widespread decline of biological populations could plausibly initiate a dramatic transformation of the global ecosystem on an even faster timescale: perhaps a single human lifetime.
The unavoidable conclusion is that biodiversity loss constitutes an existential threat in its own right. As such, it ought to be considered alongside climate change and nuclear weapons as one of the most significant contemporary risks to human prosperity and survival.

The vast literature on the biodiversity–ecosystem function relationship and the significance thereof in terms of services to humanity has focused its attention on the consequences of changes in the diversity of (mostly) plant species or genetic variants on four major types of ecological processes: (i) provisioning, such as <mark>crop yield</mark>, fodder yield, wood production, medicines and medicine models; (ii) regulating, such as biocontrol, pollination and <mark>nutrient cycling</mark>; (iii) support services such as primary productivity; and (iv) cultural services, such as inspiration, and education (see a classic review in [40]; also [39]). B. Advancing Military Capabilities
Leaving aside uncertainties about how China envisions its long-term global role, it is clear that <mark>Beijing sees control of</mark> its <mark>near seas as essential</mark> to its defence and integral to its continued rise.43 Accordingly, the fruits of <mark>PLA modernisation</mark> are most evident in China’s immediate periphery, within what it calls the “first island chain”.44 Though the U.S. military remains on the whole a superior fighting force, China’s military modernisation is eroding U.S. advantages in several areas, where the PLA now has the capacity to frustrate U.S. intervention.45
First, the PLA has significantly <mark>expanded</mark> and diversified its <mark>missile arsenal</mark>, giving it greater capacity to hit U.S. targets in the region. Between 2005 and 2023, the number of intermediate-range ballistic missiles and medium-range ballistic missiles in China’s possession spiked (from eighteen to 500 and 23 to 1,000, respectively).46 In 2020, the PLA fielded its first hypersonic glide missile, the DF-17; hypersonic missiles have manoeuvrable flight paths and are thus more difficult to intercept than their ballistic counterparts. The PLA also successfully test-fired an anti-ship ballistic missile at a moving ship in 2020, creating a new threat for U.S. surface vessels.47 These developments imply that Chinese <mark>missiles can now hit U.S. targets</mark> on land, sea and air across the region, appearing to give Beijing the ability to shut down U.S. bases in Japan and Guam, if only temporarily, and keep U.S. aircraft carriers and other large ships at bay in a conflict.48
China’s navy has grown significantly in the past two decades, increasing its power projection capability.
Secondly, <mark>China’s navy has grown</mark> significantly in the past two decades, increasing its power projection capability. The Chinese navy surpassed the U.S. navy in number of vessels around 2015 to become the world’s largest.49 Qualitatively, the U.S. still has bigger, more sophisticated warships, with more firepower and the ability to travel greater distances for longer periods.50 The U.S. Pacific Fleet commands five of the U.S. navy’s eleven nuclear-powered aircraft carriers, while China has only three (less advanced) carriers altogether.51 But <mark>trends suggest</mark> that <mark>China will retain</mark> a numerical <mark>edge over the U.S</mark>. as it invests in more modern assets.52 (The U.S. navy’s 2025 plan calls for 381 battle force ships, and Trump has announced a White House-based office to spur faster construction, but <mark>U.S. production</mark> capacity has <mark>atrophied</mark> and ramping it back up will be daunting.53) A modernising PLA navy helps extend the range of Chinese offensive and defensive capabilities beyond that of its land-based systems. Moreover, the Pentagon thinks the PLA navy will likely soon be able to strike land targets outside the Indo-Pacific.54

Fifthly, China’s modernisation efforts extend to auxiliary forces such as the coast guard and maritime militia, improvements to which have expanded the peacetime toolkit that Beijing can use to defend and assert its sovereign claims. 2. Military tactics short of war
Beijing is also using its military might – often in ways short of full-fledged conflict – to advance its sovereign claims in ways that challenge other Asian countries. A more nationalistic <mark>China sees</mark> the exercise of sovereign <mark>control</mark> and the defence of its periphery <mark>through</mark> military <mark>strength as</mark> both a <mark>legitimate</mark> right <mark>and</mark> a political <mark>necessity</mark>.76 Beijing increasingly assesses that using calibrated, escalatory shows of military force as a peacetime tool serves to warn adversaries away from its red lines and create space to achieve its political goals.77 These grey zone tactics have produced mixed results. On one hand, they have limited the ability of other claimants to assert their own sovereign claims and rights. On the other hand, Beijing has been unable to resolve disputes fully through this approach and, in the process, has incurred significant political costs.
For instance, in 2020, China ordered army units to advance along its disputed border with India in an attempt to discipline New Delhi into adopting a more careful approach on the frontier.78 This move resulted in clashes between Indian and Chinese soldiers, the deadliest since 1967, with casualties on both sides. While Beijing gained greater tactical control of stretches of the border as a result, the achievement came at a price: India now sees China as its biggest external security threat, with long-term implications for the bilateral relationship. Even though Beijing and Delhi reached a truce in October 2024 at the BRICS summit in Russia, agreeing to joint patrols along the Line of Actual Control (the de facto border) in Ladakh, analysts doubt that the deal will guarantee lasting stability.
Beijing at times engages in risky behaviour in military encounters with the U.S. to discourage Washington from sending more military assets to its near seas.
Similarly, Beijing at times engages in risky behaviour in military encounters with the U.S. to discourage Washington from sending more military assets to its near seas.79 While Washington is likely more sensitive to Chinese concerns as a result, the U.S. military presence in China’s periphery has stayed constant, if not increased.80
The use of military coercion and signalling is particularly prominent in China’s approach to Taiwan. Since at least late 2020, Chinese military activities around the island have increased in frequency and proximity, both at sea and in the air. In August 2022 and April 2023, in response to high-level exchanges between Taiwanese and U.S. officials, Beijing conducted large military exercises around Taiwan, simulating the beginning stages of an invasion.81 These displays have made China’s threat to use force more credible, thereby advancing Beijing’s goal of deterring Taiwan’s pursuit of formal independence. But they have also put its other objective – peaceful reunification – further out of reach by stiffening the resolve of decision-makers in both Taipei and Washington to shore up Taiwan’s self-defence.82 China’s more threatening posture has also deepened scepticism of its intentions among the Taiwanese population.83
3. An assertive maritime posture
<mark>Increasing</mark> military and paramilitary <mark>capabilities</mark> have also <mark>enabled</mark> China to monitor and <mark>influence</mark> events <mark>in the S</mark>outh <mark>C</mark>hina <mark>S</mark>ea more effectively. Since 2013, Beijing has established two dozen outposts on artificial islands. Facilities on four of them – Woody Island, Fiery Cross Reef, Mischief Reef and Subi Reef – are particularly advanced, with 3km runways, anti-air and anti-ship missiles, hangars for military aircraft, sensors, radar systems and harbours.84 These outposts allow the Chinese navy, coast guard and maritime militia to engage in what Beijing calls “maritime rights protection” in disputed waters.85 Satellite images indicate that during 2023, an average of 195 Chinese militia ships were present at nine features in the South China Sea on any given day.86 Coast guard vessels were present at key features in the waterway for the majority of 2023, ranging from 200 to 338 days, depending on the feature.87
Through its assertive posture, <mark>China</mark> has <mark>limited the extent</mark> to <mark>which other claimants can exploit resources</mark> in waters that fall within Chinese claims. Between 2019 and 2021, it responded to other claimants’ resource exploration and extraction activities by conducting survey operations nearby. While these were purportedly meant to collect data on water and seabed conditions, they were also a means of asserting Chinese control and deterring future activities by other claimants.88 Beijing’s <mark>harassment</mark> has, in effect, <mark>blocked</mark> new <mark>hydrocarbon projects</mark>, including joint ventures of other claimants and multinational companies. Beijing’s heavy-handed <mark>tactics</mark> have also <mark>prevented fishermen</mark> from other claimant countries <mark>from getting to</mark> their <mark>traditional fishing grounds</mark>. Since 2012, China has controlled entry to Scarborough Shoal, which both Beijing and Manila claim, blocking Filipino fishermen from casting nets in the area. Over the last two years, it has also responded more forcefully to the Philippines’ attempts to resupply its outpost on Second Thomas Shoal and fishermen in Scarborough Shoal, leading to a series of dangerous collisions with Philippine vessels.91
Not all of China’s efforts to deter other claimants from asserting their rights in the South China Sea have been successful.
Not all of China’s efforts to deter other claimants from asserting their rights in the South China Sea have been successful. Hanoi, for example, has picked up the pace in constructing outposts in the Spratlys archipelago since 2022.92 Despite regular patrols by Chinese coast guard vessels, the development of oil and gas blocks continues in Vietnam’s Nam Con Son basin, Indonesia’s Tuna block and Malaysia’s Kasawari project.93 Chinese assertiveness has also greatly contributed to both the deepening of the U.S.-Philippine alliance and other claimants’ scepticism of Chinese claims to harbour peaceful intentions. Negotiations between China and South East Asian nations over a Code of Conduct for the South China Sea within the mechanisms of the Association of Southeast Asian Countries (ASEAN), meant to create a set of rules to manage disputes, have languished for two decades in part because of the view that Beijing does not honour such agreements.94
China sees its assertive actions as legitimate and necessary exercises of sovereign control in response to what it considers to be provocations by its neighbours and rivals, whether Taipei, Manila, New Delhi or Washington. The U.S. and a number of its regional allies and partners, however, see Beijing’s emboldened behaviour as an indication that, as China develops its military capabilities and economic power, it would not hesitate to wield the influence that comes with its growing capacity to shape events and impose terms. South East Asians are increasingly apprehensive about China. In a 2025 poll of regional elites, the top security concern in the region was China’s aggressive behaviour in the South China Sea, an increase from the previous year, when it ranked second after the Israel-Hamas conflict.95 A new worry in 2025, ranking third – just after global scam operations – was about the new U.S. leadership.96
