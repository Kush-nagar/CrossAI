This study reveals a complex interplay of geopolitical interests, economic ambitions, and governance challenges surrounding Afghanistan’s rare
earth minerals resources. Three significant findings emerge from the research, each highlighting different dimensions of this multifaceted issue.
First,the study demonstrates China’s strategic dominance in Afghanistan’s REMs sector[25]. Through
state-backed investments and diplomatic maneuvering, China has secured preferential access to key mining sites,
particularly lithium and rare earth deposits in Helmand and Nuristan provinces [26]. Our analysis of
mining contracts shows that Chinese corporation’s control approximately 78 % of current extraction
projects, often through arrangements that exchange infrastructure development for mineral rights [27,28]. This dominance reflects China’s
broader strategy to secure critical minerals supply chains, particularly for its growing electric vehicle and renewable energy industries. However,
the research also identifies significant challenges in these operations, including security risks from local insurgent groups and
growing resentment among Afghan communities who perceive limited benefits from these mining
activities[29]. Second, the study uncovers the limitations of Western engagement strategies. Despite recognizing the
strategic importance of Afghanistan’s REM resources,the United States and the European Union have struggled to
develop effective policies since the Taliban’s return to power. Sanctions intended to pressure the Taliban
regime have in advertently strengthened China’s position by eliminating Western competitors from the
Afghan mining sector[30]. Interviews with industry experts reveal that current U.S. efforts to develop alternative REM sources in Africa
and South America will require at least 5–7 years to offset potential Afghan supplies. This finding suggests a significant gap in Western
critical minerals strategy that could have long-term implications for technical and military
competitiveness. Third, the research presents surprising insights into the Taliban’s governance of mineral resources. Contrary to
expectations of chaotic exploitation, the Taliban has implemented a relatively structured approach to REEs management, establishing a
dedicated Ministry of Mines staffed by both ideological loyalties and retained technical experts from the previous government. The regime has
developed a revenue distribution system that allocates approximately 15 % of mining income to local communities, though enforcement
remains inconsistent across provinces [31]. But there are significant human costs hidden behind this revenue allocation. Artisanal sites provide
evidence of child involvement in risky extraction, forced labor practices, and hazardous working conditions that frequently result in injuries.
Communities also report contaminated water sources and displacement, demonstrating how resource capture is accompanied by human
exploitation. However, the study also identifies serious limitations in the Taliban’s capacity to oversee large-scale mining operations, including a
lack of technical expertise in rare earth processing and vulnerability to corruption among regional commanders [7].
The research also highlights emerging security concerns related to REEs extraction. Approximately 23 % of artisanal mining sites in eastern
Afghanistan show evidence of control by armed groups, including affiliates of Islamic State-Khorasan [32]. These groups reportedly generate
between $15–20 million annually from illegal REEs extraction, creating a new funding stream that could sustain insurgent activities. Satellite
imagery verified the physical growth of uncontrolled extraction areas in eastern Afghanistan, supporting these findings. Illicit REEs trade was
directly linked to insurgent financing networks through parallel blockchain tracking, which revealed recurrent transfers through 17 anonymized
wallets processing an estimated $19 million annually. This combination of digital forensics and remote sensing offers a validated basis for
estimating the scope and methods of smuggling. This finding suggests that the geopolitics of Afghan REEs extends beyond great power
competition to include non-state actors with destabilizing potential. Comparative analysis with other resource-rich conflict zones yields
important insights. While Afghanistan shares characteristics with the Democratic Republic of Congo regarding the “resource curse,” its unique
combination of Islamic governance, geopolitical significance, and international isolation creates distinct challenges [33]. The Taliban’s
ideological constraints, for instance, limit partnerships with Western technical experts who could improve environmental and labor standards,
while the country’s landlocked geography complicates export routes amid regional tensions. These findings collectively suggest that
Afghanistan’s REEs resources are becoming a new focal point of geopolitical competition with far-reaching implications [34]. The research
indicates that current approaches by all major stakeholders – China, the West, and the Taliban – contain significant flaws that may lead to

suboptimal outcomes for both regional stability and global supply chain security [35]. These findings further contribute to broader
discussions about resource governance in fragile states and the geopolitics of energy transition minerals.
4.1. Critical feasibility assessment
Although the rare earth element (REE) reserves in Afghanistan are often referred to as a trillion-dollar opportunity, their practical accessibility is
still severely limited. According to technical specifications, REE extraction calls for sophisticated processing facilities as well as substantial
infrastructure, such as roads, railroads, and power plants, all of which Afghanistan presently lacks. The most promising deposits, like the lithium
reserves in Nuristan and the Khanneshin carbonatite complex, are found in isolated mountainous regions with poor transportation
connections. Afghanistan lacks the capacity to process ores domestically, so even if extraction were to begin, the country would be dependent
on foreign partners and value addition would be limited as raw ores would have to be exported.
Insecurity is a persistent obstacle in politics. Large-scale investment is directly undermined by Taliban factions or
insurgent groups like ISIS-K claiming many mining areas. Up to 40 % of revenues are thought to be
diverted to local commanders or unofficial networks, despite the Taliban's centralization of mineral governance through a
Ministry of Mines. Corruption and revenue leakage are still widespread. Formal contracts are further restricted by the lack of international
recognition, which also discourages respectable foreign companies from investing money out of concern for potential legal and reputational
issues. Economic sanctions prevent Afghanistan from accessing traditional insurance and finance markets, which limits its ability to attract
investment for expensive mining infrastructure. Although Chinese businesses have shown interest, their participation is more a reflection of
geopolitical strategy than of commercial viability, and security and environmental concerns have caused projects like Mes Aynak to stall.
Furthermore, smuggling and illegal trade will probably take center stage in the absence of international
monitoring frameworks, benefiting armed groups rather than advancing national development. These
obstacles have the paradoxical geopolitical effect of making Afghanistan's resources practically inaccessible while still strategically important.
This intensifies internal conflict dynamics and encourages speculative competition among external actors. Afghanistan's REE potential should
therefore be seen more as a contested geopolitical symbol influenced by political, economic, and technical limitations than as an immediate
supply source.
5. Results and discussion
The findings of the paper present a complex tapestry that both confirms and challenges existing theoretical frameworks regarding resource
extraction in conflict zones. The research contributes new empirical evidence to three key debates in the literature while revealing
unexpected dynamics that demand scholarly reconsideration. By contextualizing these findings within the broader landscape of resource
politics, security studies, and development economics, we can better understand Afghanistan’s emerging role in the global (REEs) landscape.
Applying well-established geopolitical theories to these findings reveals their wider significance. In a zero-sum competition, REEs serve as
power assets, according to realism. While regional hegemony explains China, Pakistan, and Russia's strategies to increase their influence in
Afghanistan, resource geopolitics highlights how supply chain control translates into international leverage.
The evidence of China’s strategic entrenchment in Afghanistan’s mining sector largely confirms existing scholarship about Beijing’s Belt and
Road Initiative (BRI) expansion into resource-rich fragile states [[36],[37]]. However, the distinctive “security-for-minerals” bargain with the
Taliban represents a significant evolution beyond traditional BRI models. This hybrid arrangement, where Chinese corporations rely on Taliban
religious
police for site security while avoiding formal state recognition [38], challenges conventional theories of foreign direct investment in conflict
zones. The research further complicates the classic “resource curse” thesis [[39],[40]] by revealing the Taliban’s unexpected institutional
capacity in mineral governance. While traditional theories focus on rentier dynamics, institutional weakness, and corruption (Auty, 1993; [41]),
the Afghan case illustrates how Sharia-based governance both facilitates and hinders mineral exploitation: on the one hand, revenue-sharing
framed as religious obligation can create localized stability, while on the other hand, ideological restrictions on foreign partnerships and
environmental regulation limit institutional capacity. This dichotomy is consistent with research on hybrid governance [[23],[42]], indicating
the need to conceptualize resource politics in Islamic contexts as separate from secular authoritarianism. While the regime’s repurposing of
pre-2021 mining regulations [43] and implementation of Sharia-compliant revenue distribution supports Kemp’s [42] arguments about hybrid
governance adaptability, the 42 % revenue leakage to intermediaries [44] simultaneously validates Humphreys (2022) warnings about
infrastructure constraints. This paradox suggests the need for a modified “Islamic resource curse” framework that accounts for how religious
governance structures simultaneously enable and constrain mineral exploitation in ways distinct from secular authoritarian regimes. The
documented failures of Western containment strategies provide empirical validation for Ali’s [45] concept of “geopolitical myopia” in rare earth
policies. The emergence of cryptocurrency payment systems and 22 new smuggling routes [46] demonstrates how traditional sanctions
regimes become increasingly ineffective in an era of digital financialization and decentralized smuggling networks. The floods in Pakistan in 2022
serve as another example of how fragile states find it difficult to strike a balance between short-term survival needs and long-term resource
governance. Structural investments in sustainable resource management stalled as billions of dollars in foreign aid were diverted to flood
recovery. This case illustrates how environmental and security crises erode resilience in resource-rich but precarious contexts, as seen in
Afghanistan, where conflict pressures similarly reroute mineral revenues. Pakistan's macroeconomic trends highlight a structural conundrum for
fragile states: reliance on agricultural exports makes the economy extremely vulnerable to climate shocks, while industrialization increases

energy demand and carbon emissions. If not properly controlled, Afghanistan's rare earth extraction could replicate this trend by supporting
energy-intensive industries without sufficient environmental protections. This analogy highlights how fragile states frequently have to choose
between sustainable resource governance and industrial growth. These findings demand revisions to existing theories of economic statecraft,
particularly regarding how secondary sanctions affect conflict mineral markets. Theories of resource control show how REEs serve as negotiating
chips, giving the Taliban more clout with China and other regional players. Afghanistan is a prime example of the "resource curse" due to its
corruption, financing of conflicts, and disregard for the environment, but it also has distinct theocratic aspects that call for a different
framework. The security dynamics observed both affirm and challenge Le Billon’s [47] theories of war economies. While REEs smuggling
finances militant groups as predicted ($19 M to ISIS-K), the Taliban’s use of mining revenues for governance (15 % local allocations) reveals a
stabilizing potential unaccounted for in existing models. This duality echoes Maconachie’s [48] findings on Sierra Leone’s post-conflict diamond
sector but with unique ideological dimensions. Afghanistan differs from both: its Taliban governance combines religious legitimacy with
selective bureaucratic continuity, creating a hybrid model; unlike the DRC, Afghanistan lacks strong corporate partnerships; and unlike Myanmar,
its sanctions environment coincides with China's strong engagement. The DRC exhibits a resource-curse trajectory marked by weak central
authority and heavy reliance on multinational corporations, with minimal environmental safeguards. International responses have mostly
focused on certification regimes (e.g., conflict minerals rules), but enforcement has been inconsistent. By situating Afghanistan’s REEs sector
within these theoretical debates, the research advances our understanding of how mineral politics operate under hybrid religious-secular
governance. The human rights component makes Afghanistan's mining industry even more complex, on top of corruption and governance
deficiencies. Afghanistan is located next to conflict mineral zones where resource extraction exacerbates humanitarian crises due to the
exploitation of vulnerable groups, disrespect for labor laws, and community displacement. Beyond governance, Afghanistan's vulnerability is
exacerbated by the environmental factor. Uncontrolled extraction puts biodiversity, agricultural productivity, and
water security at risk. It also creates feedback loops that worsen conflict drivers and impede long-term
sustainability. The findings particularly enrich discussions about how conflict minerals intersect with great power competition in an era of
energy transitions and digital financialization.
6. Conclusion
This study has demonstrated that Afghanistan’s rare earth elements represent both a strategic opportunity and a
source of instability in an era of energy transition and geopolitical competition. The research reveals China’s
successful but controversial consolidation of control over Afghanistan’s mining sector through asymmetric agreements with the Taliban, while
Western sanctions have proven counterproductive, diverting trade to illicit channels rather than curbing Taliban revenues. The Taliban’s hybrid
governance model, blending bureaucratic pragmatism with ideological constraints, presents a unique case of resource management in a
theocratic regime, simultaneously enabling revenue generation while fostering corruption and elite capture. The emergence of rare
earth smuggling as a major funding source for militant groups underscores the unintended
consequences of current policies, with regional security implications surpassing even the narcotics trade in
some areas. These findings challenge conventional theories of the “resource curse” by illustrating how Islamic governance structures both
mitigate and exacerbate mineral-driven conflicts. This study's theory of an "Islamic resource curse" adds to larger discussions by demonstrating
how religion mediates resource politics, leading to results that are not entirely consistent with traditional fragile state dynamics or secular
rentier models. Moving forward, policymakers must reconsider blanket sanctions in favor of targeted approaches that address conflict financing
without ceding the sector entirely to unregulated exploitation. The study highlights the urgent need for international cooperation to establish
monitoring mechanisms and ethical sourcing frameworks, particularly as global demand for critical minerals intensifies. Ultimately,
Afghanistan’s rare earth dilemma encapsulates the broader tensions between energy security, human rights, and geopolitical rivalries in
the 21st century – a dynamic that will only grow more complex as the clean energy transition
accelerates. This study goes beyond description to show how Afghanistan's REEs alter strategic alignments by incorporating realism,
resource geopolitics, and regional hegemony. This establishes a direct connection between local extraction and worldwide power dynamics.

Historical Context
The relationship between Afghanistan and Pakistan has been delicate since Pakistan’s founding in 1947. The Durand Line, the 1,500 mile long
disputed border that separates Afghanistan and Pakistan, was first created by the British in 1893 but has long been disputed by successive
Afghan governments. Kabul has never recognized the Durand line as the border, instead claiming the Pashtun territories in Pakistan as part of
Afghanistan. In addition, Cold War and post-Soviet era conflicts further hindered the relationship between the countries, as Pakistan
supported the mujahideen in Afghanistan during the 1980s. Conversely, Kabul, and even the Taliban regime today, have often backed
anti-Pakistan insurgents in the tribal areas.
In 2001, the Pakistan openly supported the U.S.-led fight against the Taliban-backed insurgency in Afghanistan, while at the same time, Taliban
leaders were enjoying sanctuary in Pakistan with support from the government. Pakistan has a long history of supporting the Afghan Taliban.
Now, Islamabad is accusing the Taliban of giving sanctuary to TTP. The back-and-forth nature of an already fragile relationship has led to
pattern of distrust, cross-border conflict, and competing security priorities that reinforce the precariousness of the current border clashes.
Between 2014 and 2021, changing dynamics between the countries continued as the former Afghan government was replaced by the Taliban.
Regardless of who is at fault for causing the current rise in conflict, the renewed tensions between Afghanistan and Pakistan directly impact
overall security of South and Central Asia. As border clashes and offensive attacks divert the attention of resources
of both governments, militant groups, like the Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), may have the

Strategic and Policy Implications
The escalating conflict and increasing tensions between Afghanistan and Pakistan highlight the need for adaptive security strategies that
account for the historical context and the present-day realities of militant violence and conflict along the Durand line. This should be
incorporated into a broader, formal peace agreement between Afghanistan and Pakistan. Any sustainable resolution must move beyond
temporary ceasefires and reactive operations to establish a structured means for open dialogue, implementation, and mutual accountability.
The future peace agreement should incorporate requirements for joint counterterrorism coordination, intelligence sharing, and localized
stabilization programs that rebuild trust between the two countries.
But first, the countries must resolve a fundamental point of contention: who qualifies as a terrorist actor? This disagreement lies at the heart of
their historical security dilemma. For Islamabad, groups such as the Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP) represent an
existential threat to the state, responsible, especially in 2025, for the some of the deadliest attacks in
modern Pakistan. For Kabul, however, the TTP shares ideological and tribal affinities with elements of the Afghan Taliban, and many
within Afghanistan view the group as an ally rather than an adversary. Further, the Taliban fear that “coercing the TTP to surrender to Pakistan
could incentivize/persuade the group’s leadership to make an alliance with the Islamic State Khorasan, which poses an existential threat” to the
Islamic Emirate of Afghanistan. Additionally, Afghanistan refuses to recognize the legitimacy of the Durand line.
This asymmetry makes consensus on counterterrorism, and therefore a peace agreement, nearly impossible. Pakistan seeks to neutralize groups
challenging its sovereignty and is asking for Afghanistan’s help to do so. However, the Taliban regime shares ties with the same groups Pakistan
is looking to eradicate. At least in some respects, the Taliban’s goal of promoting their version of true form of Islamic law aligns with the TTP’s
vision of reshaping Pakistan’s secular government. As a result, the two governments are unlikely to come to an agreement that satisfies all of
their individual requirements. Addressing this gap must therefore be the first step in any lasting peace agreement. Without this, Pakistan’s
counterterrorism operations will continue to be perceived in Kabul as a violation of their sovereignty while Afghanistan’s inaction against the
TTP will remain intolerable to Islamabad, ensuring the cycle continues.
Risks and Strategic Outlook
The greatest risk now facing Afghanistan and Pakistan is a strategic impasse originating from disagreements on their fundamental beliefs.
Without agreement on these fundamental definitions, neither state can design coherent, enforceable security policies that the other
would agree with.
Islamabad maintains that everything hinges on Afghanistan controlling terrorists operating within Afghanistan and that anything coming from
Afghanistan will be a direct violation of this agreement. While both sides agree that there needs to be an immediate

eradication of terrorism, and while Afghanistan acknowledges terrorism as the main cause of tensions in
their current relations, it remains to be seen whether the two countries will actually agree on what the path forward entails. Taliban
leaders insist that the TTP is a domestic challenge that Pakistan needs to address. With this dichotomy, it seems any peace agreement will be
quickly violated.
The longer this ambiguity persists, the more entrenched groups like TTP, ISKP, and the Baloch insurgency will become, exploiting divisions
between Islamabad and Kabul to expand their depth and breadth. Without a reliable agreement that establish shared desires, even a more
permanent ceasefire will only offer temporary relief. The enduring risk is not merely renewed conflict, but the institutionalization of
instability along one of the world’s most unstable, and disputed, geographic borders.
Policy Options If Afghanistan and Pakistan Agree on a Path Forward
To prevent further destabilization, Afghanistan and Pakistan must first pursue a structured process aimed at definitional alignment and mutual
recognition of threats. Any such agreement should be moderated by a group, such as the Organization of Islamic Cooperation, or facilitated by
a neutral state such as Qatar. Through either avenue, there are three priorities to establishing a long-term peace agreement:
Establishing a joint definition of the threat, clarifying which groups are subject to collective counterterrorism action and those that do
not qualify.
Creating a cross-border accountability mechanism to monitor alleged militant activity to reduce the risk of retaliatory
strikes. Developing a localized framework that incorporates tribal elders and community leaders in border provinces.
Ultimately, stability in the region will not emerge from military dominance but from political alignment and mutual agreement. A sustainable
peace agreement must integrate adaptive security policies that both countries support, ones that explicitly recognize the diverse ideological
and operational profiles of current terrorist and insurgent organizations and those still to come. Only through such a comprehensive approach
can both counties reclaim control over their respective border regions and deny transnational militants the opportunity to exploit their
divisions.

Nearly five decades later, self-styled Field Marshal Asim Munir appears determined to finish what Zia started. But where Zia seized power
through a coup, Munir has achieved something more surgical and far more enduring. He has engineered a constitutional overhaul that
formalises the military’s supremacy with the veneer of parliamentary approval. The 27th Constitutional Amendment, rushed
through Pakistan’s National Assembly on November 12, 2025, amid opposition boycotts and sporadic nationwide
protests, marks an unprecedented consolidation of military power.
In a country long governed under the shadows of military generals, this amendment brings the shadows of military control into
the broad daylight. It erodes the last pretences of civilian authority, dismantles judicial independence,
and most alarmingly, it places Pakistan’s nuclear strategy firmly into the hands of one man.
The result is a disturbing evolution of what many military analysts call Pakistan’s “nuclear terror fusion”
doctrine that is a volatile blend of tactical nuclear signalling and state-sponsored terrorism aimed at coercing New Delhi into resumption of
bilateral dialogue by internationalising the Kashmir issue.
By engineering the latest constitutional amendment, Asim Munir has not just fortified military supremacy, but he has become the architect of
a nuclearstrategy that blurs deterrence, brinkmanship, and proxy warfare and binds them into a single
highly combustible doctrine.
Converting a Hybrid State into a Uniformed Autocracy
Pakistan’s democracy has always been a brittle compromise between the civil government and the
military. It has been more of a hybrid construct in which civilians occupied the office while the military held the power. The 27th Amendment
strikes at the heart of even this fragile arrangement.

The amendment has rewritten the legislation’s key constitutional articles, most notably Articles 175 and 248, which grant lifetime immunity to
a sweeping category of officeholders, in particular to Asim Munir himself. It shields him completely from the civil and criminal proceedings,
effectively granting him perpetual protection for the past abuses and carte blanche for the future ones.
The amendment also reengineers the judicial architecture by dismantling the fragile buffer that once allowed courts to occasionally challenge
military overreach. The Supreme Judicial Council is recast to give military-aligned judges expanded influence over appointments and
dismissals, while a new “national security clause” empowers the military leadership to intervene in cases deemed sensitive. Two Supreme
Court justices resigned in protest, calling the changes “an annihilation of judicial independence”.
In earlier eras, the military operated from behind the curtain, quietly ushering out disobedient prime ministers, engineering fragmentations
in political parties, or leaning on courts to preserve its primacy. With the 27th Amendment, those sources of leverage no longer require
subterfuge. They are now constitutional powers largely in the hand of one man, Munir himself.
Asim Munir’s Political Coronation
Asim Munir’s meteoric rise has been carefully self-choreographed, just like General Zia’s. His self-appointment as the second Field Marshal in
Pakistan’s history, next to Ayub Khan, followed the defeat of Pakistan in Operation Sindoor earlier this year. Despite the glaring weaknesses
in Pakistan’s defence infrastructure that stood totally exposed after India knocked out most of its airfields and air defence sites during the
operation, the skirmish was spun by the Pakistan army as a triumph that showcased Munir as a “strategic genius”.
The 27th Amendment completes his transformation from army chief to Pakistan’s uncontested supreme authority. It does so by abolishing the
office of the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff Committee and merging its authority with that of the Chief of Army Staff, thereby creating the
new and vastly more powerful role of Chief of Defence Forces (CDF). As a result, all defence, intelligence, and nuclear decisions now flow directly
through Munir.
Civilian oversight, already feeble, has become essentially irrelevant. Parliament has been reduced to a
ceremonial echo chamber, and provincial governments exist at the mercy of the military’s intelligence apparatus. The media,
placed under aggressive censorship regimes, is unable to report freely on the implications of this transformation.
Opposition leaders are either imprisoned or silenced, and protests across major cities such as Lahore, Karachi, and
Quetta have been met with harsh crackdowns and sweeping sedition charges. For the first time since General Zia, Pakistan is effectively a
one-man military state, but now with a constitutional framework designed to make this centralisation of power permanent.
The Darkest Chapter
The most consequential aspect of the 27th Amendment lies not in the domestic politics but in its perceived regional security. Under Munir’s
CDF office, the reforms grant its military virtually total control of the National Command Authority (NCA), the body responsible for Pakistan’s
nuclear policy. This centralisation accelerates a dangerous trend within Pakistan’s strategic thinking by bringing the fusion of tactical nuclear
signalling with proxy warfare.
For years, the military’s doctrine of “full-spectrum deterrence” has been interpreted by analysts as a euphemism for using the threat of
low-yield nuclear weapons to shield the state-sponsored terror activities against India. Under Munir,this doctrine mutates into
something much sharper, riskier, and more personalised.
First, the amendment empowers the CDF to “pre-emptively deploy strategic assets in scenarios posing
existential threats,” effectively granting Munir unilateral authority to mobilise battlefield nuclear
weapons, such as the Nasr/Haft-IX, without any civilian consultation or oversight. This institutionalises a first-use nuclear posture
and formalises the blurring of conventional and nuclear boundaries.

The risk of China spiralling into an unprecedentedly prolonged recession is increasing. Its economy is
experiencing deflation, with the price level falling for a second consecutive year in 2024, according to recent data from the
National Bureau of Statistics of China. It’s on track for the longest period of economy-wide price declines since the
1960s. Coupled with the collapse of the property sector, a looming trade war with the United States and
demographic and debt overhang challenges, much of the Chinese public has lost confidence in the economy and its
leadership. The country has the ingredients for a recession, and not a short one. It has spent too much on
investment and needs to turn to consumption as a source of demand, but people are unwilling to spend.
They have long had high savings rates, and now deflation is further discouraging spending. So do falling property
values, ageing of the population and excessive corporate and government debt. Getting out of such a recession will be hard, because of the
challenge of restoring confidence and getting households and businesses to spend more. Since local government debt is high,
expanding public expenditure to stoke demand would worsen economic imbalances. Current deflation is a
result of the Chinese government’s long-standing adherence to the China model, which consists of extensive state control
and ownership of resources, limited free-market activity, and authoritarian CCP leadership. The model fuelled both the
country’s economic miracle and its most intractable problem: a structural imbalance between investment and domestic
consumption. To sustain fast growth and cushion economic downturns, China has long relied mainly on investment in infrastructure,
property and manufacturing. Household consumption is seriously constrained through unfair policies and a discriminatory social security
system. These include strictly limited rights to move for work, weak human rights protections and relatively low benefits for migrant workers. In
the 30 years to 2012, investment gradually rose from 32 percent to 46 percent of GDP, while the share of final consumption declined from 66.6
percent to 51.1 percent. The high rate of investment financed necessary infrastructure upgrades and modernised China’s production technology,
helping the country become a global manufacturing powerhouse. However, over time, high rates of investment led to severe
overcapacity in key industrial sectors, particularly after the 2008 financial crisis.
