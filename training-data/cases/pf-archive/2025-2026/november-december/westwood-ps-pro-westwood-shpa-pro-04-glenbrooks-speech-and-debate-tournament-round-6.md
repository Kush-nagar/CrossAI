Digital Platforms and Platform Economics
A digital platform is an online entity that facilitates interaction between two or more distinct economic entity², allowing them to create, communicate, exchange value, or collaborate. A platform economy is a business model where digital platforms enable multiple economic entities to come together and interact in a way that “benefits” all the entities involved. Examples include Uber, Swiggy, Amazon, Instagram, and WhatsApp. Unlike traditional markets, platform economies are shaped by:
Network effects
Positive feedback loops
Switching costs
Lock-in
Together, these forces can propel one platform to dominance and erect high barriers to entry for challengers.
Network Effects
Network effects are force multipliers that allow a product/platform to become stronger when more people join or use it. For example, if most of your friends are on Instagram, it makes sense to join the same network. The larger the groups of people on a network, the stronger the network becomes. There are different types of network effects — Direct Network Effects, Indirect Network Effects, and Data Network Effects.
Direct Network Effects occur when more users join a one-sided platform (like a messaging app). As the user base grows, so do the opportunities to communicate and connect, driving both higher adoption rates and an ever-larger network.
Indirect Network Effects occur on two-sided platforms that connect distinct user groups — typically buyers and sellers. An increase in users on one side of the platform enhances trade and business opportunities for users on the other side. For example, on ride-hailing platforms like Uber, which connect drivers and riders, more drivers on the road improve the experience for riders, and vice versa.
Network Effects as the Engine for WhatsApp’s Growth
Launched by Jan Koum in 2009, WhatsApp 1.0 began as a service for updating address-book statuses, but quickly evolved into a way for people to message friends via their status updates. Koum — known for his ethos of “No Ads! No Games! No Gimmicks!” — then transformed WhatsApp 2.0 into a clean, ad-free messaging platform at a time when most phones were internet-connected³ and the only alternatives were SMS, MMS, or BlackBerry’s BBM. This initial critical mass of users created the perfect conditions for WhatsApp to corner the market: with most of a person’s contacts already on WhatsApp, new users automatically adopted it as their default messaging app, driving direct network effects.
WhatsApp’s integration with businesses further reinforced indirect network effects: a larger user base attracted more companies, which in turn reached more users and expanded the platform’s ecosystem.
Positive Feedback Loops
In systems thinking, complex networks usually have feedback loops, which can either be positive or negative. Positive feedback loops reinforce a particular feature or behaviour of the network, thereby leading to exponential changes in the system. Negative feedback loops, on the other hand, produce effects that run counter to the changes observed in the system. Both these loops act so that the system eventually achieves its version of homeostasis.
Positive Feedback and Tipping: Achieving Market Dominance
WhatsApp’s growing user base, combined with its intuitive interface and strong UX features, created a positive feedback loop that drove further adoption. Rapid expansion in key markets such as India and Brazil accelerated this viral growth. Together, network effects and the feedback loop tipped the market in WhatsApp’s favor, building a wide moat around its user base and producing a winner-take-all dynamic — evident in monthly active users rising from 10 million in October 2010 to 100 million in October 2012.⁴
Switching Costs
<mark>Switching costs</mark> expenses a user incurs when moving from one platform to another. They are common in the information-technology space because data is stored on servers, and transferring it- or adopting new hardware or software — adds expense. Switching costs may be social (losing established connections) or pecuniary/transactional. When these costs are high, they <mark>effectively lock users into a platform, even if</mark> <mark>its features</mark> or pricing <mark>become</mark> economically <mark>unattractive</mark>⁵.

Self-escrow
Self-escrow refers to the idea that when the user sets the device passcode, this value is escrowed on the device itself rather than with any external third-party. Third-party secret escrow poses a range of challenges, including the need to trust a new third party, the complexity of dynamic escrow protocols themselves and the difficulty in knowing if the escrowed information has been used. Moreover, to enforce this invariant, our design mandates that the escrow agent is a write-only hardware component (i.e., one not readable via software) and the only means of querying this component is via a direct physical connection (e.g., via dedicated pins on the hardware package). 3. No impact to quantum warfare.
Matisek ’25 [Jahara; Command Pilot, U.S. Air Force; Nonresident Research Fellow, U.S. Naval War College and Payne Institute for Public Policy; and Visiting Scholar at CSIS; October 9; "Quantum Sensing and the Future of Warfare: Five Essential Reforms to Stay Competitive"; CSIS; DOA: 11/21/2025, https://defensescoop.com/2024/10/24/dod-cryptographic-modernization-effort-challenges // iris🌻]
While advances in quantum technology are showing promise, there are still many challenges to be overcome before those advantages can be realized on the battlefield. These challenges do not negate quantum’s military value but serve as a reminder of the contested nature of current quantum terrain. Every revolutionary technology, from radar to GPS to stealth, matured through complex engineering phases before transforming combat. Quantum sensing is now in that phase: fragile but advancing quickly, with each iteration bringing it closer to rugged, deployable systems. Countries investing and experimenting the most will own the decisive edge in the post-stealth battlespace.
Extreme sensitivity is both an advantage and a liability. Sensors that can detect the faintest magnetic or gravitational changes also absorb vibration, thermal drift, electromagnetic clutter, and the noise of their own host platforms. On moving steel hulls and fast jets, background interference overwhelms weak signals. Magnetometers throw spurious tracks near power systems, gravimeters lose stability in turbulence, and inertial packages drift without constant recalibration.
This <mark>fragility has slowed the transition from lab</mark>oratory <mark>to</mark> the <mark>battle</mark>field. Devices that perform well in controlled conditions will malfunction when exposed to the motion, temperature swings, and background noise of real-world military operations. Size, weight, power, and cost constraints add another layer of difficulty, as <mark>few systems can meet the</mark> rugged <mark>demands of combat</mark>.
Other oft-touted applications face similar limits. “Unhackable” quantum communications remain vulnerable to hardware exploits and require major infrastructure investments in repeaters and memory. Claims that <mark>quantum radar will</mark> “expose advanced stealth aircraft and missiles” or be a “submarine killer” should be treated with skepticism, because it will <mark>have limited range and be susceptible to photon loss, atmospheric interference, and noise</mark>.
More likely, quantum radar will evolve into hybrid systems, paired with conventional radar and signal processing, to improve detection in certain niche environments rather than revolutionize air defense outright.
The gap between promise and practice is that quantum devices can detect what traditional sensors miss, but most <mark>prototypes are too fragile for combat</mark> conditions. Until engineers solve ruggedization and error correction at scale, <mark>quantum</mark> sensing will remain more of <mark>a vulnerability</mark> in theory <mark>than a military advantage</mark>.

8. Preemptive war is a lie.
Massa ’21 [Mark J. and Matthew Kroenig; June; MA, Security Studies, Georgetown University, associate director, Forward Defense practice, Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Security; PhD, Berkeley, Professor of Government at the Edmund A. Walsh School of Foreign Service at Georgetown University; Atlantic Council, Scowcroft Center for Strategy and Stability, “Are Dual-Capable Weapon Systems Destabilizing? Questioning Nuclear-Conventional Entanglement and Inadvertent Escalation,” https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/in-depth-research-reports/issue-brief/are-dual-capable-weapon-system s-destabilizing/]
Reading between the lines of most nuclear entanglement theory—and the direct arguments of others—it is clear that the theory rests almost entirely on the “use it or lose it” theory of nuclear escalation.13 “Use it or lose it” is the idea that, if a state fears that its nuclear arsenal might be wiped out, then the target state would prefer to use its nuclear weapons early in a crisis before it loses them. Attacks on a target’s nuclear forces or command and control creates pressures for the targeted state to use nuclear weapons because the target fears that, if it does not use them, then its nuclear arsenal could be degraded or eliminated. The use of dual-capable offensive platforms could cause the target to choose nuclear escalation, because the target state might fear the dual-capable system is on the verge of conducting a nuclear attack, and the target state would prefer to employ its nuclear forces first before the attacking state can conduct a potentially disarming or decapitating nuclear attack.
Although often not stated clearly by proponents,this is the strongest underlying theoretical rationale for theories of nuclear entanglement and inadvertent escalation.
Talmadge, for example, worries that “the target state might fear that the opponent was seeking to attrite the target’s nuclear force through conventional counterforce.” She fears that “Chinese leaders could reasonably come to believe that the United States was seeking to pursue conventional counterforce...or even nuclear counterforce.”14
Sometimes, theorists enunciate this logic explicitly. Ankit Panda and James Acton argue that US attacks could “in- advertently degrade Chinese nuclear [command and con- trol (C2)] and generate nuclear use-or-lose pressures.”15 Wu Riqiant writes that “Chinese leaders would face high use-it- or-lose-it pressure, and might lose confidence, leading to a decision to escalate.”16 John Speed Meyers argues that US strikes on Chinese co-located missiles risk “putting Chinese leaders in a use-it-or-lose-it situation.”17 Joshua Rovner writes that “inadvertent escalation may occur when conventional attacks put the adversary’s nuclear force at risk...Fearing the destruction or incapacitation of its nuclear deterrent, the tar-get state might face a ‘<mark>use it or lose it’ </mark>dilemma.”18
While superficially plausible, closer examination reveals that “use it or lose it” rests on a weak logical foundation and does not, in fact, provide rational incentives to use nuclear weapons for three reasons.19
First, “use it or lose it” <mark>is a false dilemma</mark>. A false dilemma is a common logical fallacy that occurs when one is presented with a choice between two unattractive options when, in fact, there are more than two options available. The false dilemma presented by “use it or lose it” is that leaders with vulnerable nuclear forces have a choice between either using their nuclear weapons or losing them. Rarely, if ever, however, in international politics, do <mark>leaders</mark> face a choice between suffering a disarming attack and launching an intentional nuclear attack of their own. They <mark>have many other options. They can</mark> surrender. They can <mark>conduct diplomacy</mark>. They can <mark>retaliate with conventional</mark> military <mark>force</mark>. They can <mark>launch</mark> a <mark>nonnuclear</mark> strategic <mark>attack</mark>, such as <mark>in space or cyber</mark>space. They could take other steps to ensure the survivability of their forces, such as to <mark>flush sub</mark>marine<mark>s to sea</mark>, place nuclear-armed bombers on alert, or <mark>activate</mark> <mark>mobile</mark> missile <mark>forces</mark>. They can <mark>engage in</mark> nuclear <mark>brinkmanship</mark>, raising the risk of nuclear war through nuclear alerts or veiled nuclear threats in the hope that the other side will back down. Intentionally launching a nuclear first strike is not the only, or even the most obvious, response for a state that fears it might become the victim of a disarming attack.
