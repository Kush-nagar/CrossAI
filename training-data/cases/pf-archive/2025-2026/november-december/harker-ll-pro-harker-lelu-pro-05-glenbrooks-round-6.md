<mark>The FBI is secretly breaking</mark> the <mark>encryption</mark> that secures our cell phones and laptops from identity thieves, hackers, and abusive governments, and it refuses to even acknowledge that it has information about these efforts — even though some details have been filed publicly in federal court. We’re suing to get some answers.
Between our emails, text messages, location information, social media activity, and more, our cell phones hold almost our entire lives. In recent years, <mark>governments have stepped up efforts</mark> to gain access to the information on our cell phones and personal computers. The federal government has been <mark>pressuring companies</mark> to build encryption backdoors that would severely undermine our digital privacy and security, <mark>and</mark> both federal and state governments have regularly paid <mark>third-party vendors</mark> to break into people’s encrypted devices.
Now, it appears <mark>the FBI has</mark> built an <mark>in-house capability</mark> to break into these devices. Publicly available information indicates that the Electronic Device Analysis Unit (EDAU), a team within the FBI, has acquired or is in the process of acquiring software that allows the government to unlock and decrypt information that is otherwise securely stored on cell phones. Public court records also describe instances where the EDAU appeared capable of accessing encrypted information off of a locked iPhone. And beyond that, the EDAU even sought to hire an electronics engineer whose major responsibilities would include “perform[ing] forensic extractions and advanced data recovery on locked and damaged devices.”
To learn more about the EDAU and its capabilities, we filed a request under the Freedom of Information Act asking that the Department of Justice and the FBI disclose records relating to the EDAU and its technological capabilities for retrieving information from locked electronic devices. The FBI responded in part by issuing what’s known as “Glomar” responses to two of our requests — which means <mark>that the agency refuses to</mark> even <mark>confirm or deny</mark> the existence of any records pertaining to the EDAU.
A valid Glomar response is rare, as there are only extremely limited instances where its invocation is appropriate — that is, only where the existence or nonexistence of records is itself exempt under FOIA. The problem with the FBI’s Glomar response is that, as detailed above, we already know records pertaining to the EDAU exist because information about the unit is already public. The fact that all of this information is already publicly known deeply undercuts the FBI’s Glomar theory. The FBI itself has made clear that it is attempting to access and decrypt personal electronic devices, so the claim that it can’t even acknowledge whether these records exist is implausible.
Seeking some much-needed transparency, today we asked a federal court to intervene and order the DOJ and the FBI to turn over all responsive documents pertaining to the EDAU. We’re demanding the government release records concerning any policies applicable to the EDAU, its technological capabilities to unlock or access electronic devices, and its requests for, purchases of, or uses of software that could enable it to bypass encryption.
By invoking the Glomar response, the federal government is sending a clear message: <mark>It aims to keep</mark> <mark>the</mark> American <mark>public in the dark</mark> about its ability to gain access to information stored on our personal mobile devices. But it’s not that the FBI has just shut the door on this information — they’ve shut the door, closed the windows, drawn the shades, and refused to acknowledge whether the house that we’re looking at even exists. It’s imperative that the public gets meaningful access to these records regarding the federal government’s capabilities to access our phones and computers. Our privacy and security are at stake.

Chapter 1 - The Threat from China A quarter of a century ago, there was a widespread belief that China could be ‘Westernised’ and steered towards liberalism. President Clinton, speaking in 2000 about China’s accession to the World Trade Organisation, outlined that while membership in the WTO would not make China liberal overnight, “it will move China faster and further in the right direction.”20 Today, that optimism has vanished. A rare bipartisan consensus has emerged in Washington: China is the United States’ most formidable competitor and the primary threat to its national security and interests. The 2017 National Security Strategy, issued under President Trump’s first administration, stated unequivocally that “China and Russia want to shape a world antithetical to U.S. values and interests. China seeks to displace the United States in the Indo-Pacific region, expand the reaches of its state-driven economic model, and reorder the region in its favor.” It also underscored American fears that Beijing was building the most capable and well-funded military in the world after the U.S., with that gap closing by the day.21 Trump’s successor, Joe Biden, largely continued the firm stance taken on China. Rather than reversing Trump-era policies, his administration expanded them by introducing a variety of economic measures, such as the Inflation Reduction Act. Likewise, a diplomatic strategy was undertaken to solidify U.S.-led alliances in the Indo-Pacific to push back against China’s increasingly aggressive posture. In 2022, President Biden’s National Security Strategy described that China “is the only competitor with both the intent to reshape the international order and, increasingly, the economic, diplomatic, military, and technological power to advance that objective.”22 It was this growing power that the administration saw as harming America’s interests and tilting the global order for the Chinese Communist Party’s (CCP) benefit. The current administration has appointed known China hawks to key positions: Marco Rubio as Secretary of State and Mike Waltz, formerly National Security Advisor and now nominee for Ambassador to the United Nations. Meanwhile, influential think tanks close to the Trump administration, such as the Heritage Foundation, have stated that “a totalitarian Communist dictatorship is engaged in a strategic, cultural, and economic Cold War against America’s interests, values and people.”23 This view has become embedded in the U.S. foreign policy establishment. Both parties agree that the goal is no longer to manage the competition with China but to win it. China, for its part, has undergone a similar transformation in perspective. It previously saw areas of cooperation with Washington, mainly in the realms of counter-terrorism and climate change, but now believes that the two countries are in a protracted competition with each other. Its approach to the competition with the U.S. is holistic, incorporating not just decision-makers in the Politburo but a range of different stakeholders and actors that are being mobilised in this competition, from the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) to businesses and academic institutions.24 Its objective is clear: displace the U.S. at the top of the international system through a combination of economic, technological, military, and diplomatic means. The Science and Tech Competition The science and technological dimensions of America’s competition with China run far deeper than its competition with the Soviet Union during the Cold War. President Biden’s National Security Advisor, Jake Sullivan, argued that the U.S. would pursue a policy of maintaining “as large of a lead as possible” over its adversaries in technology, attempting to keep China behind in strategic technologies with defence applications.25 Meanwhile, President Xi outlined in 2024 that while China had made breakthroughs in strategic technology fields, even becoming the world leader in some, there are weak spots. Therefore, he stated that China “must further enhance its sense of urgency and intensify its efforts … so as to secure a leading position in sci-tech competition and future development.”26 Xi’s remarks are the latest expression of a long-standing ambition of the Chinese Communist Party. Announced in 2015, the ‘Made in China 2025’ plan highlighted his goal of becoming the global leader in critical technology manufacturing and development, including semiconductors, electric vehicles and biotechnology. By 2049, it aims to dominate a broader set of strategic sectors, including AI, quantum, and robotics.27 As one former official at China’s Ministry of Science and Technology stated, “[t]he core of the U.S.-China rivalry is the technology war … Whether the U.S. can defeat China or whether China can rise from adversity will depend on the technology war.”28 In pursuit of this goal, China has rapidly increased its investment in the research and development of new technologies. By 2022, China was reportedly spending over 2.5% of its GDP on research and development (R&D), representing just shy of $650 billion, while the U.S. spent just over $700 billion.29 Due to the opacity of Chinese financial reporting, there is a possibility that its spending is higher than this. Notably, China’s R&D expenditure is mainly state-led, whereas the U.S.’s is mostly driven by the private sector. In quantum technologies, for instance, the Chinese government announced spending of $15.3 billion in 2022, compared to the U.S. government’s $3.7 billion and the UK’s $1.3 billion.30 This centralised, state-led model enables the CCP to more directly align technological investment with its broader strategic competition with the United States and do so faster. Beijing’s investments are paying dividends. According to the Australian Strategic Policy Institute’s (ASPI) Critical Technology Tracker, which assesses global research performance, strategic intent, and potential future science and technology capability across 64 critical technologies, including in defence, computing, cyber, and AI, China has made remarkable progress. Between 2003 and 2007, the U.S. led the world’s innovative research in 60 of the 64 tracked technologies. By contrast, China only led in three. Fast forward to 2019-2023 and the landscape had shifted dramatically. China led in 57 of the 64 technologies, while the U.S. led in just seven.31 This trend underscores the need for the U.S. and allies to expand its innovation efforts – presenting opportunities for deeper collaboration with key allies like the UK. Concerns about the development of Chinese innovation and technology are not localised in the U.S. Sir Alex Younger, the former Chief of MI6, recently stated that China’s mastery of key technologies is a “pacing threat” to the UK and one that is just as important as the threat currently facing the UK from Russia. He warned that the UK needed to stay at the forefront of the development of key emerging technologies, without which the UK’s “security prospects will be pretty dim.”32

A greater number of PLAN overseas military installations – which in many ways depends on its diplomatic and development strategy – whether in Asia, Africa or Latin America and the Caribbean, can be used to collect vast amounts of intelligence, disrupt U.S. shipments in times of crisis and resupply its navy with weapons, ammunition, and equipment to challenge America’s control of the sea.44

Following a series of cyber attacks on UK companies, government departments, critical infrastructure, and politicians, the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre named China as the “pacing threat in the cyber realm.”45 Second is the development of global power projection capabilities as outlined above. The NATO strategic concept in 2022 outlined that China poses a challenge not simply to the security of the Indo-Pacific but also the Euro-Atlantic due to the use of its political, economic, and military tools.46 Already, Beijing has been accused of conducting sub-threshold activities against European NATO members. The damage to undersea infrastructure in the Baltic Sea by Chinese vessels has demonstrated that the UK and European NATO members should not only increase capabilities and spending to counter Russia, but also China.

Adversaries in the Arctic Another matter of concern is Russia’s role in the Arctic. There is another rare bipartisan consensus in Washington that America needs to be paying closer attention to the region, both due to the opportunities and the strategic challenges it presents. The U.S. Coast Guard during the first Trump administration argued “[t]he Arctic’s role in geostrategic competition is growing,” warning that U.S. adversaries are using the Arctic to conduct their activities “under a cloud of ambiguity that makes it difficult to mount an effective or timely response.”67 In 2024, the Biden administration released an Arctic strategy, which outlined that the region “is critical to the defense of our homeland, the protection of U.S. national sovereignty, and our defense treaty commitments.”68 Interest in the Arctic spiked further following President Trump’s suggestion that the U.S. acquire Greenland for national security reasons. A Mineral-Dominated Economy China’s hold on critical minerals poses significant challenges for Western economies. In his first administration, President Trump declared the reliance on China for critical minerals a “national emergency.”101 More recently, the Secretary of State, Marco Rubio, claimed in his Senate confirmation hearing that China is “scooping up” mineral rights from its economic engagement across the world.102 Should the U.S. fail to diversify its critical mineral supply chain away from China, there is a fear that much of what America depends on for its economic growth will be dictated by Beijing. Critical minerals are the lifeblood of modern economies. From defence and electric vehicles to computing, renewable energy, and medical technology, a wide range of industries depend on reliable access to these resources. As global demand accelerates, competition to secure these resources will shape the behaviour of many global players, not least the U.S. and China. The International Energy Agency predicts that demand for critical minerals will increase by up to 350% by the middle of this century. In defence alone, critical minerals are used in a wide array of applications, including weapons systems, ammunition, and aerospace technologies. NATO has listed twelve critical minerals and materials that are essential for its defence capabilities: aluminium, beryllium, cobalt, gallium, germanium, graphite, lithium, manganese, platinum, rare earth elements, titanium, and tungsten.104 China dominates the U.S. import supply for several of these, serving as the primary import source for germanium, graphite, rare earths, and tungsten, and is a major provider of gallium.105 With almost complete Chinese dominance over mineral processing, Western supply chains are over-exposed to China, and in times of tension, Beijing has been able to weaponise minerals to undermine adversarial industries. In 2010, China put an export ban on rare earth elements to Japan following a territorial dispute, forcing Tokyo to radically change its approach to supplying key mineraldependent industries.106 More recently, the Biden administration faced Chinese export restrictions on minerals with military applications such as gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite, and tungsten.107 Likewise, as the trade war between China and the U.S. intensified under President Trump’s recent tariff regime, China again ordered restrictions on the exports of rare earths to the U.S., undermining America’s ability to build fighter jets, missiles, and unmanned vehicles.108 As the economic, technological, and military competition between the two superpowers intensifies, Beijing has an advantage due to its dominance in this field. America’s industry will struggle to revive itself and its military weakened relative to China as supplies needed for its industries stall. Concerns have been voiced by the UK’s Defence Select Committee which argued that reliance on China for critical minerals used in the defence supply chains presents a significant risk to weapons and ammunition production in the UK and, therefore, the country’s defence.109 Similarly, the National Security Strategy, released in June 2025, stated that advances in science and technology would mean “greater competition for sources of energy and critical minerals. The frontrunner in this competition could achieve technological predominance,” which would have profound implications for the UK’s safety and security.110 It is not simply the UK’s dependence on China that poses a threat to UK security, but broader allied dependence, particularly America’s. The UK is a large importer of military equipment from the U.S. – which, as highlighted, relies on critical minerals. The UK currently has almost 40 operational F-35B Lightning II fighter jets, built by Lockheed Martin in Texas, and seeks to expand that number over the next decade.111 There is a wide variety of critical minerals needed to successfully support the production of F-35s, as well as other defence imports. For instance, each F-35 jet requires upwards of 400 kilograms of rare earths,112 cobalt supplies for engines, and beryllium, lithium, and gallium for a variety of other applications. Likewise, UK government attempts to promote green energy are vulnerable as a result of high levels of Chinese domination. With lithium, graphite, cobalt, and rare earths crucial in the make up of batteries, wind turbines, solar panels, electric vehicles, and more, the UK’s energy and economic security will be under threat due to China’s dominance in the processing of these minerals. Without sure and diversified supply chains, the UK could find itself in a dangerous economic knot, with long-term consequences for national resilience. Russia and its paramilitary groups have gained access to mining rights through its role in supporting authoritarian regimes. With Africa home to upwards of 30% of the world’s mineral reserves,114 the need for the U.S., UK, and allies to mount a serious competitive response has never been more urgent. Many developing countries are now seeing the consequences of China’s economic statecraft, undermining the supposed ‘win-win’ nature of its financing. Beijing’s investments in critical minerals are having disastrous human and economic consequences. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), it is estimated that 40,000 children work in cobalt, lithium, and rare earth mining, and China-led mining activities have only increased child labour issues. Indeed, Congolese lawyers have indicated that in China-owned or -operated mines in the DRC, children often work seven days a week, for more than 12 hours a day, and are exposed to injuries and diseases. Meanwhile, environmental safeguards are overlooked, accelerating environmental degradation.115 The troubling practices are not limited to the DRC. In Zimbabwe, trade unions have pleaded to the Chinese embassy for its companies to abide by local laws. Workers there have accused Chinese mining companies of undermining health and safety and physical abuse.116 Consequently, many developing nations are eager to diversify their mining partnerships. China’s continuous disregard for environmental, social, and governance (ESG) standards and local regulations has made Western companies – which have higher ethical and operational standards – more attractive alternatives. However, the U.S., UK, and allies have so far failed to offer a compelling alternative and have not created the conditions for greater private sector involvement. Failures to adequately tackle development challenges like political instability alongside weak economic and judicial systems have meant that Western companies have been reluctant to invest and operate in mineral extraction due to risk.117 Some of the most mineral-rich nations are the most fragile and hardest to conduct business in. To challenge China’s control effectively and ensure secure supplies for U.S., UK, and other NATO allies’ defence and technologies industries, a more coordinated approach is essential – one that actively looks to reduce risk and open doors for Western companies to operate in these markets with confidence – and uses an effective diplomatic and development strategy to boost partnerships with mineral-rich partners. That said, there have been some steps to address challenges. In 2022, a group of fourteen countries: Australia, Canada, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Norway, South Korea, Sweden, the UK, and the U.S., as well as the EU, announced the creation of the Minerals Security Partnership (MSP). The partnership aims to create more diversified and resilient supply chains for critical minerals, promote ESG standards in mining and processing, and increase the recycling of minerals.118 The MSP, however, has largely failed to engage effectively with mineral-rich nations. PART 2 - OPPORTUNITIES AND CHALLENGES FOR UK-U.S. COOPERATION The closeness of the relationship between the UK and the U.S. is fundamentally driven by each other’s ability to contribute to the other’s foreign policy objectives. Taking steps to keep the U.S. on top in its competition with the new axis of autocracies, mainly China and Russia but also Iran, must be a priority for UK foreign policy. Making practical policies to support Washington in tackling shared threats and challenges in the areas outlined in the previous chapter would strengthen the transatlantic alliance in an era of potential vulnerability and support UK defence, security, economic prosperity, and values over the coming decades. Most importantly, it provides a pathway to keep Washington involved and committed to the relationship with the UK, as well as the wider transatlantic relationship. It is equally important for the UK to avoid overcommitting and underperforming, which could be just as harmful as not committing in some areas at all. Overextension would not only cast doubt on the UK’s value but would also weaken UK security by leaving gaps ripe for exploitation by Russia and China. There remains, nevertheless, a plethora of things that the UK can be doing to counter China, deter Russia and support U.S. interests. And there remain many challenges that the UK will have to confront. The UK’s approach to China, AUKUS, and burden sharing in European security, as well as rethinking international development and derisking critical supply chains all, if done well, will support the goal of stabilising and securing the strength of UK-U.S. relations over the coming decades. Chapter 5 - The UK’s China Approach The UK’s approach to China has long been inconsistent, distorted, and reactive. From the so-called ‘Golden Era’ of UK-China relations that envisaged close investment and trade ties between Beijing and London, to the downturn in relations post-Cameron, all the way through to current attempts to court economic opportunities, the UK has lacked a coherent and enduring China approach. Successive leaders have likewise struggled to maintain consistency. Official rhetoric has shifted regularly from describing China as a systemic challenge, as described in the Integrated Reviews, to labelling Beijing as a threat, a term used by Rishi Sunak and echoed by other senior figures in the last Conservative government. Most recently, the 2025 Strategic Defence Review called China a “sophisticated and persistent challenge.”119 Both parties’ China approaches have been widely criticised for a lack of clarity, coherence, and specifics. Experts at the Royal United Services Institute accused the previous Conservative approach as one that “smacks of ‘cakeism’, promising to secure all the benefits and address all the negatives that China presents, while ducking value judgements and tough choices on priorities.”122 Meanwhile, the current Labour approach is far too vague for practical policy-making. Neither party has clearly defined red lines or articulated what the top priority is – be it security, growth, or values This lack of coherence in language and policy could have consequences for the UK’s future relationship with the United States. Washington expects allies to take a firm and clear stance. A tendency towards reactive, short-term policy-making risks damaging the UK’s standing in the eyes of American leaders, putting a strain on the military, intelligence, and diplomatic ties with the country’s closest ally. Trump’s chief trade advisor, Pete Navarro, recently accused the UK of being a “compliant servant of communist China.”123 While this is an exaggerated and inflammatory claim, it highlights the pressing need for consistency in how the UK articulates and enacts its China policy. While it is right to recognise China as a threat – and do so consistently – this should not lead to total disengagement. Cutting off dialogue or cooperation entirely would be counterproductive, especially in addressing global challenges like health security. Meanwhile, with the likelihood of rising tensions between Washington and Beijing, complete breakdowns in dialogue between the UK and China could increase global instability. China is becoming more important to the global health ecosystem, particularly in disease management and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). It has funded antibiotic research significantly and, given its high rates of AMR in China, will be central to the development of new products, technologies, and medicines to tackle the challenge.124 Greater collaboration on pandemic preparedness and AMR will be essential to curbing future infectious disease outbreaks. Likewise, as global volatility and unpredictability increase, it becomes more important for the UK to have high-level dialogue with Chinese officials. As the closest partner to Washington, the UK is uniquely positioned to serve as a mediator between Washington and Beijing to de-escalate tensions and reduce the risks of miscalculation on both parts. As chances of conflict by misunderstanding, miscalculation, and an asymmetry of information become more likely, these meetings between military leaders are invaluable to avoid conflict, serving as important intelligence-gathering exercises for both the West and China to understand foreign policy and military intentions and capabilities. Nevertheless, a trip by the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS), Admiral Sir Tony Radakin, to China in April 2025 was the first official visit by a UK CDS in a decade.125 Sparse meetings between military leaders from adversarial nations are likely to increase tensions, distrust, and chances of conflict. Consequently, to maintain peace in the IndoPacific, the UK should engage more directly and more regularly with the Chinese military leadership.126 In recent years, UK politicians have been strong and consistent when exposing and speaking out against the CCP’s repression and abuses in Xinjiang, Tibet, and Hong Kong. Previous Conservative governments regularly condemned the CCP’s targeting of Uyghur Muslims, forced labour, restrictions on religion, and the persecution of minorities across China, especially in Xinjiang and Tibet. Parliament has been equally vocal. In 2021, the House of Commons passed a motion that declared that crimes against humanity and genocide were occurring in Xinjiang.127 Similarly, the erosion of the Sino-British Joint Declaration, in particular through the National Security Law, led to the new Hong Kong BN(O) visa route which allows Hong Kongers to live, work, and study in the UK.128 When there has been Chinese repression against pro-democracy protests in the UK, politicians have also spoken up. However, when actions like this occur across the rest of the world, silence has been palpable. Due to the role that Chinese discourse, economic statecraft, and security activities play in bolstering its antiAmerican and anti-Western movements, firmer approaches to tackling Chinese soft power and influence are needed. This includes using platforms at the UN and other international forums to spotlight the damaging impacts of Chinese global activity on human rights, sovereignty, and socio-economic development. The UK has been failing to counter China’s soft power and its claims of SouthSouth cooperation – a pillar of its anti-American movement. The success of this movement hinges on developing nations believing that China is a more reliable partner than the West, and better understands and supports their interests. The narrative promoted from Beijing conceals a darker reality: human rights abuses, repressive practices, and extraterritorial arrangements are embedded in its overseas activities. Publicly challenging China’s practices on the global stage would not only reinforce the UK’s commitment to transatlantic values but also signal to the White House that the UK is taking China’s anti-Western movement and its broader ambitions seriously. Such a move would reaffirm the UK’s alignment with the U.S. and demonstrate a willingness to lead in defending a global order against an autocratic movement. Chapter 6 – AUKUS AUKUS is a trilateral partnership between the UK, U.S., and Australia that intends “to support a free and open Indo-Pacific that is peaceful, secure and stable,” as well as deepen information and technology sharing between the three partners.129 It is structured around two pillars. Pillar 1 focuses on equipping Australia with conventionally-armed nuclear-powered submarines. This first involves the sale of Virginia class submarines, followed by the joint development of a next-generation submarine – SSN-AUKUS – by the UK and Australia in the 2030s and early 2040, integrating technologies from all three partners. Pillar 2 is much broader in scope, focusing on developing capabilities, technology sharing and interoperability in new defence technologies such as AI, quantum technology, cyber, undersea, hypersonic, counter-hypersonic, and electronic warfare.130 For the UK, AUKUS presents significant industrial and strategic opportunities. It offers a boost to the defence sector, promising some 21,000 jobs and technological advancement. Similarly, the Royal Navy stands to benefit from acquiring the best nuclear-powered submarines in the world – an increasingly vital asset in light of Russia and China’s investments in undersea capabilities. Likewise, the success of Pillar 2 can bolster the UK’s technological prowess and ensure the upper hand against adversaries. However, the UK is particularly exposed should AUKUS falter. Not only would the UK lose out on industrial opportunities, but it could lead to an overreliance on the U.S., potentially diminishing the UK’s standing in the relationship and calling into question Westminster’s value as a partner.132 For the U.S., the importance of AUKUS’s success cannot be overstated. It greatly enhances the deterrence capabilities of the U.S., UK, and Australia in the face of China. Currently, the Virginia class submarines and the UK’s Astute class stand almost unrivalled in undersea warfare – only Russia comes close to competing. Nevertheless, Beijing’s industrial output and its investments in research and development could mean that it soon catches up.133 Despite a review being ordered in Washington on the future of the deal, AUKUS has received immense bipartisan support on Capitol Hill due to its strategic benefits. Marco Rubio recently stated that AUKUS “is something that I think you’re going to find very strong support for in this administration”, with the partnership being “almost a blueprint” for how the U.S. should work with other allies. AUKUS is, in his opinion, “one example of how we can leverage the power of these partnerships with allies … to reach outcomes and objectives such as creating a geopolitical and strategic balance in the Indo-Pacific region and beyond.”134 This shows that the ability of the UK to drive AUKUS forward will be one of the central considerations for Washington to put time, money, and resources into the relationship AUKUS Pillar 2, as argued by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, offers “game-winning potential in the strategic competition with China”, helping to secure both military and economic advantage for AUKUS nations long into the future.135 In 2023, China was ahead of the U.S. in 19 of the 23 technologies relevant to AUKUS Pillar 2.136 As highlighted in Chapter 1, China’s rapid technological progress threatens to outpace Western efforts unless addressed collectively. A coordinated AUKUS approach to R&D, alongside other Indo-Pacific partners like Japan and South Korea, can help to close the gap in research performance to China.137 Exercising interoperability is a core American priority in working with allies, and AUKUS is beginning to make progress here. In 2024, the three partners conducted Maritime Big Play, a joint experiment in Australia aimed at testing new defence technologies and improving interoperability. Innovators participated alongside militaries, allowing the evaluation of new technologies.138 Likewise, Japan joined as an observer following consultations about closer collaboration under Pillar 2.139 Improving interoperability through AUKUS will strengthen the ability to act in concert during joint operations and reduce friction in achieving shared objectives. Despite the game-changing potential of both AUKUS pillars, there remain significant hurdles to overcome. One concern is whether the U.S. industrial base can cope with ramping up production to make up for the Virginia Class submarine sales to Australia while maintaining its own needs. The U.S. will need to simultaneously construct up to five new Virginia class submarines to maintain its strength.140 America’s ageing shipbuilding capacity and underinvestment in industrial infrastructure could place the early phases of Pillar 1 at risk if the U.S. determines it cannot divert submarines to Australia. The success of Pillar 2 is indispensable. In September last year, the U.S. eased export controls on defence technology transfer by exempting AUKUS nations from its International Traffic in Arms Regulations, allowing the UK and Australia favoured access to the U.S. defence industrial base.141 However, beyond the removal of export controls, there lacks a clear direction and strategy for Pillar 2. Questions remain about its goals, the intended outcomes and how the three nations will coordinate effectively to develop next-generation capabilities. The Strategic Defence Review outlined that the UK should ensure that AUKUS is an exemplar of co-innovation and industrial collaboration between allies,” and that the UK should use “Pillar 2 to test and develop a template for future technology partnerships.”142 Despite an £86 billion commitment for defence- and securityrelated research and development from the Government in the National Security Strategy, there has been a worrying lack of coordination of efforts between AUKUS partners on Pillar 2.143 Greater collaboration in this space, as outlined by Dr Emma Salisbury, “allows for cost-sharing and reduces the financial burden on any single nation. This can accelerate the development and deployment of cutting-edge technologies, benefiting all parties involved.”144 With better R&D comes greater progress but there has failed to be an environment between the three nations, and potentially with others – notably Japan and South Korea – to support joint innovation. With joint R&D efforts being viewed positively in Washington, the UK should actively seek to shape this ecosystem, ensuring that innovation can occur at scale and pace. This will not just deter Chinese activities but also allow the UK to increase capabilities in the face of future Russian aggression. Chapter 7 - Demonstrating European Burden Sharing As outlined in Chapter 2, Northern Europe and the Arctic are set to be key areas of focus for the White House over the next decades, driven by its strategic challenges and its economic opportunities. Likewise, Europe, despite the potential drawdown of U.S. forces, will continue to face security challenges that impact American interests. The UK’s position in the High North is unique. Despite not being an Arctic power – the Shetland Islands lie roughly 400 miles south of the Arctic Circle – the UK has historically played an important role. The UK is one of the only non-Arctic nations that invests in its capability in the region and has a regular military presence there.145 For over half a century, the UK has conducted cold-weather training in Norway, and every year Royal Marines train U.S. Marines in Arctic warfare, enhancing their operational, tactical, and survival skills.146 Given the high turnover rate among U.S. Marines, with only around a third reenlisting, British experience and leadership in the region is highly valuable.147 Likewise, the UK has consistently contributed to major Arctic exercises. Since 2006, Norway has hosted Exercise Cold Response, giving allies military opportunities to train and operate cohesively in the toughest circumstances. In 2022, the UK contributed six Royal Navy ships – including one aircraft carrier – and 2,000 personnel to the 27-country exercise.148 It is under the Joint Expeditionary Force (JEF) that the UK can truly prove to the U.S. the value of UK and European partners in tackling threats spanning from Europe and the High North. The JEF is a UK-led military partnership focused on the High North, North Atlantic and Baltic Sea. Consisting of the UK, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Latvia, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway, and Sweden, it enables rapid deployment of forces across multiple environments. The JEF is not a duplicate of NATO and falls outside of its command structure. However, all JEF members are in NATO and the group enhances NATO activities by ensuring a more flexible and rapidly deployable response to security crises and threats, even those below the Article Five threshold where NATO is unable or unwilling to respond.149 With Washington not just fearing conventional threats from Russia, but also subthreshold activities, such as sabotage to critical infrastructure like undersea cables, the role of the JEF becomes even more important to the U.S. Russian vessels seeking to disrupt transatlantic infrastructure pass through JEF waters, making regional surveillance, defence, and partnerships paramount. In recent leaders meetings of the JEF, the group has remarked that one of its aims is to "constrain, contest, and counter Russia’s aggression and threats to regional security” by increasing efforts to deter, defend against, and counter Russian sabotage, acts of violence, cyber and electronic interference, and disinformation.150 In response to the growing sub-threshold threat, the JEF has intensified efforts to safeguard critical underwater infrastructure. In 2023, a JEF Response Option was activated for the first time. This saw the deployment of 29 vessels and 11 aircraft to patrol a million square miles of sea. In 2024, Nordic Warden was launched whereby all 10 members tracked and monitored the Russian shadow fleet as it was looking to damage undersea infrastructure.151 U.S. frustration with European defence spending is long-standing and demonstrating concrete action to share the burden is key to maintaining U.S. engagement in European security. The JEF is a great vehicle for this. First, all JEF members have consistently spent over 2% of their GDP on defence and have seen some of the fastest rises in defence spending out of all NATO countries. Indeed, the Royal United Services Institute outlines that since 2014, JEF members have seen an average real-term defence spending increase of 150%, compared to 108% for the rest of NATO’s European members. Lithuania, for example, has seen a 327% increase. Likewise, all JEF members have pulled their weight when assisting Ukraine. Of the top 10 European NATO members providing assistance to Ukraine (as a percentage of GDP), eight are in the JEF.153 Yet, a key challenge in demonstrating the JEF’s value is the lack of awareness and understanding of the group in Washington. Outside specialised circles, knowledge of the JEF’s mission, operations, and structure remains limited – even within the Pentagon and State Department. As the leader of the JEF, the UK must do more to promote the group’s role in European and transatlantic security to key audiences in the White House, Pentagon, State Department, and on Capitol Hill. Failure to do so risks allowing U.S. perceptions to be shaped by underperforming allies, potentially justifying a reduced American commitment to European defence. At the same time, the UK can and should be doing more than simply demonstrating the JEF’s role in burden sharing. As discussed in Chapter Two, a resurgent Russia after the war in Ukraine will continue to be a defining threat for the UK, U.S., and NATO allies for years to come. Beyond the war, the Kremlin’s objectives are unlikely to change and it is likely to undertake large investments in advanced technologies and force size. In this context, calls to halt increases or even reduce defence spending once the war ends will be dangerously short-sighted. Some may argue that the crisis has passed and that ‘peace’ allows for cutbacks. If the UK is serious about deterring future Russian aggression, it must continue to increase defence spending, capabilities, and innovation. American frustration over European defence spending is completely justified. The lack of spending from NATO allies, allowing militaries to be hollowed out, undermines Article 3 of the North Atlantic Treaty which insists that each member “develop their individual and collective capability to resist armed attack.” Over the past two decades, defence has been outsourced to the U.S. While Pete Hegseth recently called the UK’s plans to increase defence spending to 2.5% of GDP by 2027 “a strong step from an enduring partner,” there remains impatience and higher expectations in Washington for what the UK and other European allies should be spending. Numbers are an obsession. Indeed, there have been signals from Washington that if Europe does not go further and faster, the U.S. will reduce its commitments to European security." Importantly, this pressure from both Democrats and Republicans is not simply about freeing up American resources to shift to the Indo-Pacific. It stems from a recognition that European security remains important to American interests. A less secure Europe would severely constrain the U.S.’s ability to project power globally. As one interviewee described, Europe serves as America’s ‘strategic rear’ as the partnership and collaboration between Russia and China intensifies. This places a particular responsibility on the UK, which has traditionally been the bridge between the U.S. and Europe. It will have to be UK leadership in NATO that helps to persuade future administrations that the continent remains worthy of investment and attention. France and Germany have embraced an idea of independence from the U.S. – a position that gives Washington an excuse to pull back commitments to NATO’s Article 5. Meanwhile, the UK knows that no European power or collection of powers can replace U.S. strengths and capabilities.156 The prominence of the role that Britain plays in NATO has the potential to determine whether the U.S. is in or out. The current Labour government has already outlined its intention to be the leading European nation in NATO. The UK currently has the second-largest defence budget out of European NATO members, is one of just two European NATO nuclear powers, and is the only one to assign its nuclear deterrent to NATO. Nevertheless, in the decade following the annexation of Crimea in 2014, the UK has had one of the lowest real increases in defence spending in the alliance.157 With Poland spending 4% of GDP on defence, and other European partners ramping up spending quicker than Westminster, the UK’s position as the leading European NATO member is up in the air. As such, the UK needs to go further and faster with spending and innovation to ensure a position as the leading European NATO member, which is of vital importance to keep the U.S. in. Equally, by improving capabilities, there is a better division of labour, whereby the U.S. can focus on its largest threat all while ensuring a continued commitment to NATO, even as it has less of a presence. Chapter 8 - Rethinking International Development Partnerships Since the Second World War, the White House and Capitol Hill have consistently seen that defence, diplomacy, and development are interlinked pillars of American foreign policy. Together, they have served to advance American strategic interests abroad. The current administration has taken a drastically different approach by dismantling the agency set up by Kennedy in 1961 to provide development assistance across the world: USAID. Under the first Trump administration, development assistance was largely framed as a tool to advance American interests. The 2017 National Security Strategy outlined that through development spending “the United States will also assist fragile states to prevent threats to the U.S. homeland. Transnational threat organizations, such as jihadist terrorists and organized crime, often operate freely from fragile states and undermine sovereign governments.”158 In line with this view, the Trump administration passed the Global Fragility Act that coordinated efforts by USAID, the State Department, and the Pentagon to help nations, predominantly in West Africa, to move from fragility to stability for U.S. national security reasons, though it also served an economic rationale.159 The 2017 National Security Strategy also set out a so-called ‘America First’ approach to development, not as a rejection of global engagement but as a means of aligning development spending more closely with American economic and geopolitical interests. Indeed, it outlined that the United States would “modernize its development finance tools so that U.S. companies have incentives to capitalize on opportunities in developing countries.” This also served a geopolitical rationale, for by leveraging the private sector, the U.S. could compete with states that “use investment and project finance to extend their influence,” namely China.160 This more transactional approach to development underlined the belief that development spending could, when spent well, achieve three goals: open up economic opportunities, compete against China, and support development. Importantly, efforts by the first Trump administration to achieve these goals, notably the launch of the U.S. International Development Finance Corporation (DFC), relied on USAID efforts to create a pathway towards stability. Within 24 hours of his second inauguration, President Trump signed an executive order suspending all foreign aid programs for 90 days, with reviews taking place to see if spending was aligned with policy goals. They were analysed by a simple criterion: does it make the U.S. safer, stronger, and more prosperous? As such, the future of American development spending, regardless of party, is likely to follow two central considerations that are not too dissimilar from those of Trump’s first administration. First, we can expect a more transactional approach to development, which focuses on promoting U.S. economic prosperity. Future administrations are likely to look to free up American businesses, removing barriers for them, encouraging investment in developing markets, and fostering trade relationships that benefit American industries and workers, whether through access to resources, the export of goods, or ensuring returns on investment. When outlining the priorities of U.S. international assistance at the start of July 2025, Secretary Rubio stated that moving forward, American assistance will target resources to areas “where they can have a multiplier effect and catalyze durable private sector, including American companies, and global investment.”161 Second, development spending will be far more aligned with the competition with China. The agency and role of developing countries will help to shape the order that comes next, whether it reflects Western values or those of authoritarian powers. With American politicians believing that the book of the 21st century will be about the U.S. competition with China, and huge concerns over Chinese infrastructure investment across Africa and in the Western hemisphere, the future of development assistance is very likely to align tightly with the competition with Beijing and broader security concerns. Rubio explicitly argued that by putting development assistance under the remit of the State Department, it would place the U.S. “in a stronger position to counter China’s exploitative aid model and further our strategic interests.”162 At the same time, there is uncertainty about the future of America’s commitment to multilateral development institutions. They are increasingly criticised as being too slow, inflexible and incapable of achieving broader American foreign policy objectives. In some cases, multilateral institutions have been looking to ‘Americanise’ their operations so as to ensure continued U.S. commitment. For instance, the World Bank is looking to orient itself around jobs, expanding economic opportunities, and greater partnerships with the private sector.163 Across foreign policy, the U.S. has been looking at new mechanisms outside bilateral and multilateral spaces that are quick, flexible, and facilitate national goals better. This has been most common in defence. Minilaterals, such as AUKUS and the Quad, are key pillars for deterring Chinese aggression in the Indo-Pacific, while groups like the Five Eyes continue to underpin intelligence cooperation. Minilaterals offer distinct advantages: they bring together small coalitions of like-minded states with shared objectives and demand active contributions from members.164 They epitomise burden-sharing. Free-riding is harder in minilateral settings due to the smaller number of participants. Each member has to pull their weight otherwise the ambitions of the group have the potential to fall through. However, unlike in defence where minilateral cooperation is well-established, there has been little progress in shaping minilateralism in international development. At a time when the traditional development architecture is under immense pressure and the strategic environment is becoming more volatile, there is clear scope for new and innovative thinking for development cooperation. The UK, with its historical leadership in both diplomacy and development, is well-positioned to take a leading role in shaping this new approach which brings together like-minded partners with shared interests to deliver impact where it matters most. The U.S. has valued British expertise in some areas of development, as well as the information, intelligence, and local knowledge that has arisen through the deep historical ties between the UK and many developing nations. As such, the UK has always had a lot to offer in this space. It is no wonder Kennedy was so keen to ensure British support in expanding development and technical assistance programmes across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. Today, the UK’s contributions continue to make a significant and lasting impact, from global health initiatives to institution-building and democracy promotion. The U.S. Global Leadership Coalition – an organisation that champions American global leadership in defence, diplomacy, and development – has argued that to ensure that development spending supports American interests, security, and prosperity, a strategy that promotes burden sharing in development between allies is imperative. A collaborative approach ensures that every dollar spent overseas is maximised, creates the greatest returns, and supports American and allied interests.165 As America’s closest ally, the UK should look to champion burden sharing in development and support initiatives that promote Western values and security in a minilateral format.

Chapter 9 - Diversifying Mineral Supply Chains As Chapter 4 showed, critical minerals are the lifeblood of modern economies, defence, and industry. Across the U.S., UK, and other allied nations, dependence on China poses significant vulnerabilities. Should there fail to be diversification of critical mineral supply chains, Beijing will have a greater ability to direct the trajectory and opportunities for Western economies and militaries. The U.S. feels acutely vulnerable and has used this fear to justify the current administration’s ambitions with Greenland and also agreements with Ukraine, Rwanda, and the DRC for critical minerals. The UK, meanwhile, has started to realise the importance of creating more resilient supply chains for critical minerals. The Russian invasion of Ukraine, as well as Chinese dominance in the area, sparked a desire for a holistic approach to derisking critical mineral supply chains. At the UK’s Critical Minerals Strategy’s heart was a desire to collaborate more deeply with international partners. The Conservative Government stated that it would encourage and support UK companies to participate overseas in the mining sector. Likewise, it envisaged a diplomatic and development approach to support diversification and resilience in a way that supported sustainability, transparency, human rights, and environmentalism.167 The use of Official Development Assistance (ODA) and engagement of the private sector, as outlined in the Strategy, was to be used to “de-risk investments” and ensure global standards for safe and responsible mining – something ignored by Chinese companies.168 Ultimately, however, the Critical Minerals Strategy failed to outline anything beyond surface-level recommendations for de-risking investments and building resilient supply chains. The Foreign Affairs Committee in the House of Commons – which argued that critical minerals need to be understood through the lens of geopolitical competition – described that the Strategy “does not provide a set of targets with a clear plan of implementation or indicate an order of prioritisation.”169 Fundamentally, it failed to outline concrete policies that the UK could make. The International Development White Paper, released in 2023, also highlighted the importance of critical minerals. It regarded that the UK needed to support developing nations “to retain more of the value of their critical mineral resources.”170 This currently is not happening as China continues to dominate and expand its role in the processing of minerals, as shown in the tables in Chapter 4. Beijing, as such, captures the value of critical minerals and gains an advantage over the West, whereby it can shut off supplies, just as it has done in the past and currently to Japan and the U.S. Whilst not explicitly mentioning the dominance of the CCP in critical mineral processing, the White Paper outlined a pathway to address it. It said that the UK would work with private finance to increase investments in value chains to support developing nations in processing their minerals.171 In doing so, there would be greater support for sustainable development with higher ESG standards and stronger resilience of supply chains. Since both the Critical Minerals Strategy and the International Development White Paper, there has failed to be a stronger application of private finance. Of the $17 billion of private equity capital for global mining, only 2.8% focuses on Africa, according to financiers, showing the large discrepancy between the continent’s resource richness and private capital supporting extraction and in-country processing.172 This should not be too surprising. Mining companies seeking capital have struggled to raise funds for their activities in developing countries as investors see the risk as too high. As a result, many Western companies have had to cease operations in some African nations.173 China has been the main beneficiary of the retreat of Western investment in the minerals sector. Between 2019 and 2024, China expanded its mine ownership in Africa by 21%, while Western countries have seen mine ownership lessen. Despite China representing a smaller share of total mining output than its Western counterparts, it has substantial stakes in critical minerals with significant industrial and defence applications like lithium, copper, and cobalt, particularly in the DRC, South Africa, Zambia, and Zimbabwe.174 The BRI and financial ties with African nations have ensured a steady flow of raw materials to China for processing. In the African mining sector, China is the largest buyer. 71% of African exports to China originate from mineral-rich nations in Africa’s copper belt, as well as South Africa and Angola, all of which have received significant investment from Beijing.175 In the long term, a growing role for Chinese companies in Africa’s mineral sector will carry three major consequences. First, it will harm the development of African countries, primarily as minerals are exported to China for processing, meaning that value cannot be captured. Second, it will undermine human rights and local laws and regulations, as has been demonstrated in previous chapters. Third, it threatens Western industries. Tackling this requires a more collaborative development and diplomatic strategy that can create pathways for this to happen. On a development strategy, the Government should follow recommendations set out in previous Coalition for Global Prosperity reports, which encourage a greater portion of the UK’s ODA budget spent on governance and institution building to de-risk investments and unleash opportunities for the Western private sector.176 Diplomatically, the UK is in an influential position to better coordinate and build partnerships with many developing nations. The City of London is the global centre for metals trading, which sets standards for safe and responsible mining.177 Furthermore, all 21 African members of the Commonwealth, as well as some Asian members, are actively engaged in the mining sector.178 Nevertheless, there has currently been a lack of engagement with many on this issue. The Minerals Security Partnership (MSP), for instance, has consulted with non-members to support diversification, including Argentina, the DRC, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Kazakhstan, Mexico, Namibia, Peru, Philippines, Serbia, Turkey, Ukraine, Uzbekistan, and Zambia.191 Yet, there are several crucial countries with critical minerals, including many in the Commonwealth, that have not been consulted with. Mozambique could provide 15% of the world’s graphite by the end of this decade.192 Similarly, Gabon is the largest producer of manganese globally and accounts for 63% of U.S. imports of the mineral.193 With many nations having a desire to diversify partnerships away from China due to the negative consequences of Beijing’s investments, the UK’s role within the Commonwealth can play a crucial role in engaging MSP members better with Commonwealth partners to create more diversified supply chains and encourage Western private companies to invest sustainably, whilst also ensuring that economic engagement follows the high ESG standards and values of MSP members. This sort of diplomatic engagement can, as has been regarded by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, create a “platform for solving the coordination problems between investors, developers, and consumers.”194 This would support the aims of both the Critical Minerals Strategy and the International Development White Paper by strengthening diplomatic relationships and engaging better with producers to provide a clearer pathway for engaging the private sector, improving development outcomes by allowing developing nations to capture more value from mining activities, and diversifying critical mineral supply chains for the UK, U.S., and other MSP members. PART 3 - POLICY RECOMMENDATIONS This report has made the case that in order to preserve the strength of the UK’s relationship with the United States, and reinvigorate it in a volatile and unpredictable global environment, the UK has to take a more substantial role in tackling the threats and challenges facing both countries: China’s economic, technological, and military capabilities; the threat from Russia as its military modernisation continues and it looks to undermine the strength and resilience of NATO allies; the anti-Western movement that threatens to create a world order antithetical to UK and U.S. interests, whilst serving the interests of adversaries; and economic insecurity which poses fundamental challenges to defence, technology, and energy industries across both nations. A weakened UK-U.S. relationship harms each nation’s ability to tackle these threats and challenges, undermining the strength, resilience, and security of both. As the National Security Strategy notes, there is much more that the UK can do to strengthen and deepen the alliance in a way that reflects the changing nature of power in the world. As such, this report recommends a unified series of practical steps and policies, across defence, development, and diplomacy, to boost the UK’s strategic value to Washington over the next two decades so that the relationship is fit for a pre-war era. As the threats and challenges facing the UK and U.S. are broad, encompassing hard, soft, and economic power, any set of policies must acknowledge that defence, development, or diplomacy alone would be unsuited to managing the modern world. Therefore, a concerted approach is needed that engages with each to achieve the most impact. The UK should convene an AUKUS Pillar 2 forum biannually to lay out a clear strategy and priorities for Pillar 2 and create an effective ecosystem for research and development collaboration for new defence technologies. AUKUS Pillar 2 offers a game changing opportunity for the UK, U.S., and Australia to deter Chinese aggression in the Indo-Pacific. If done well, it will help to secure a long-term military advantage for allies in the pre-war era. It will also help to translate into greater capabilities for the UK in Europe when facing Russian aggression. However, China has surpassed the U.S. and her allies in terms of research output for new technologies, especially military technologies, which will support its assertive ambitions by complementing its rapid build up in force size and spending. The UK offers strengths to the U.S. and Australia in this area, with unique research and development for some areas of defence technology, such as electronic warfare. It has some of the best academic institutions in the world for new technologies, a vibrant culture for innovative start-ups in the defence space, and strong international collaboration. With a review being ordered by the Trump administration on the future of AUKUS, it is essential to show the potential behind the agreement. However, Pillar 2 currently lacks a direction and strategy. There needs to be answers given about Pillar 2’s goals and pathways created for effective coordination by the three allies to develop next-generation military capabilities. Most notably, there has been a paucity of suggestions for collaborative research and development efforts for Pillar 2 technologies, providing China with a great opportunity to surpass the West in military power over the next decades. This will contribute to a less secure and more unstable Indo-Pacific. More regular forums that focus on AUKUS Pillar 2 can encourage discussions between governments, industries, innovators, and universities about how to build an effective environment for research and development collaboration between the UK, U.S., and Australia to effectively counter China’s dominance in R&D output for military technologies. This will complement steps taken by the Government through the Strategic Defence Review, particularly the creation of UK Defence Innovation and its £400 million annual budget to deliver innovative technology to the UK’s armed forces.195 Importantly, Japan has been involved as an observer for some aspects of AUKUS, notably Maritime Big Play. With Japan and South Korea’s technological prowess, this forum should encourage participation from Tokyo and Seoul to ensure greater levels of research, development, and innovation amongst like-minded partners to deter Chinese activities and also allow the UK to access modern capabilities in the face of Russian aggression in Europe and the High North. The UK should do more to expose in international institutions, like the UN, human rights abuses and a neglect of local laws inherent to Chinese development projects as a pathway to counter Beijing’s soft power. Part of China’s strategy to displace the U.S. at the top of the international hierarchy is to undermine Western influence and encourage greater alignment with China. Ultimately, the growth of Chinese influence creates a permissive environment for aggression in the Indo-Pacific, allows greater intelligence gathering for Beijing and threatens to disrupt global supply chains, whilst also shaping new norms, rules, and values that underpin the future international order. One of the key pathways for China to do this is to present itself as a more equitable alternative to Washington, that rejects interference in other countries affairs and promotes a world order of equality, justice, shared prosperity, and global solidarity. A U.S. led order is argued by the CCP as being one of greed, exploitation, subversion, and suppression – wording that Beijing also used to describe British influence globally. Despite this rhetoric, China’s influence in many developing nations undermines human rights, freedoms, and sovereignty. Its claims of South-South cooperation and much of China’s soft power conceals a darker reality of human rights abuses, repression, and extraterritorial arrangements. Whereas British politicians and governments have done well to speak out against repression and human rights abuses within China, such as in Xinjiang and Tibet, as well as in Hong Kong, there has been a lack of attempts to counter China’s global discourse and the soft power. The UK should look to counter this anti-Western narrative by exposing in international forums, like the UN, human rights abuses, extraterritorial arrangements, repression, and the neglect of local laws and norms that are inherent to Chinese financing and security cooperation. For example, speaking out against Chinese mining practices in the DRC and Zimbabwe, as outlined in Chapter 4, would expose the reality of China’s anti-Western movement and demonstrate that a China-led order would not represent the principles that it purports to. The UK should set a pathway to increase core defence spending to 3.5% of GDP by 2030. In February 2025, the Prime Minister, Sir Keir Starmer, announced to the House of Commons that the UK would spend 2.5% of GDP on defence by 2027 and intended to spend 3% in the next Parliament. After years of successive American administrations’ frustration with European defence spending, this has been seen by the current administration as a positive step. More recently, at the NATO Summit at The Hague, the alliance’s leaders agreed to increase defence spending to 5% of GDP by 2035, with 3.5% of GDP on core defence expenditure and 1.5% on investments in resilience and security. However, the ambiguity over when, how, and indeed if, the UK gets to 5% raises questions over the UK’s leadership in defence. Without a concrete plan of getting to the target, the Government risks undermining the effect of the ambitions laid out in the 2025 Strategic Defence Review, or whether recommendations come into effect at all. Across Europe, there needs to be a drastic rise in defence spending, not just to justify American involvement in NATO, but to increase capabilities, support Ukraine, and deter future Russian aggression on the continent. The new Chancellor of Germany, Friedrich Merz, has recently stated that Americans “do not care much about the fate of Europe."196 Such arguments, simply give the U.S. an excuse to pull back commitments from NATO. It, therefore, becomes essential that the UK reestablishes its role as the bridge between the U.S. and Europe, especially in defence terms. A quicker and more substantial increase in defence spending can support that. The UK should play the leading role in driving European increases in defence spending. This means being one of the first to reach the 3.5% target on core defence expenditure; five years before the deadline. This commitment would serve as a much needed bridge between North America and Europe and stabiliser to the NATO alliance. Crucially, it would be true burdensharing, allowing Europe to secure America’s ‘strategic rear’ while the Pentagon focuses most of its efforts on China, all while ensuring that there remains an American commitment to Article 5. The UK should invite a U.S. delegation to JEF summits to showcase the role of Northern European countries in burden sharing. There has, of course, been a huge problem with burden sharing on defence, and too many NATO members have failed to reach the 2% target. In 2024, Croatia, Portugal, Italy, Canada, Belgium, Luxembourg, Slovenia, and Spain failed to spend 2% of GDP on defence, while several countries like Slovakia, Montenegro, Albania, the Netherlands, and France had only just reached the 2% target. It is no wonder, therefore, that the U.S. across several administrations have been so critical of its NATO allies’ defence spending. Washington has often honed in on members who are failing to spend enough on defence. For the modern Republican Party, and for many Democrats, the view of Europe is that it has not been pulling its weight as a whole, due to the attention that is given to those barely spending 2%. This, however, does not show the whole picture of burden sharing. Northern European nations have excelled at increasing their defence spending. As stated in Chapter 7, JEF members have rapidly increased their spending, and in doing so, have contributed vital capabilities to European and High North defence. Likewise, with Washington being concerned over Arctic security and sub-threshold threats, the role of the JEF in boosting U.S. security and countering threats from the Arctic becomes vital. Nevertheless, there is a lack of understanding about the JEF and the role of JEF members in sharing the security burden in Europe with the United States, meaning that the attention given to underperformers is not being countered. The UK, as the leader of the JEF, should therefore do more to showcase to the U.S. the role of the JEF in burden sharing, highlighting the importance of the group and its individual members in boosting American security and interests. Equally, JEF nations are some of the foremost Atlanticists and are the greatest supporters of an American presence in Europe. The Lithuanian Defence Minister, Dovilė Šakalienė, has recently stated that the country supports U.S. demands for defence spending increases, argued that U.S. support is a “life-or-death issue,” and has indicated a willingness to strengthen cooperation with American defence companies. Statements like these reflect a common view across JEF members. The JEF holds annual summits with country and defence leaders, which allow members to discuss defence cooperation, regional security, and strategic priorities, including the war in Ukraine. It is not unprecedented for non-JEF members to attend summits. Indeed, in 2024, Volodymyr Zelenskyy attended the JEF summit in Tallinn virtually. As such, the UK should look to invite a U.S. delegation to attend JEF summits to demonstrate the group’s and members’ importance to the U.S. The UK should seek to establish a UK-U.S.-led minilateral development partnership with like-minded allies for a more flexible and faster method to align development spending to mutual strategic priorities. The world is more precarious and volatile than any time in the past 80 years. Both Russia and China are looking to developing countries to shape the global order that comes next. The role of development is, therefore, entirely strategic in the eyes of the UK’s and U.S.’s adversaries. At the same time, the Western development model is in turmoil. Cuts in the UK and Europe, the dismantling of USAID, alongside growing questions about the strategic gain of multilateral development initiatives means that there needs to be a radical rethink about priorities and mechanisms of development. All while the West has been dismantling the traditional development model, Beijing has been ramping up its efforts across the world. The Belt and Road is picking up steam again after a lull during the Covid-19 pandemic. The Global Development Initiative, Global Security Initiative and Global Civilisation Initiative are seen by President Xi as the pathway for a China-led global order that mirrors its domestic image. An approach to development is needed that can align spending with strategic priorities in a world between orders. There ought to be an approach that combines the expertise of allies, shares the burden, and is flexible enough to adapt to changing situations. Minilaterals have become a staple of defence policy in recent years. AUKUS, the Quad, the U.S.-Japan-Australia trilateral strategic dialogue, the Global Combat Air Programme, and the JEF have all become central to security to the Indo-Pacific and Euro-Atlantic. As they bring together small coalitions of like-minded states with shared objectives, they offer more adaptable cooperation, higher levels of interoperability, shared practices, and a common strategic vision. They accumulate the power of each member and form an effective deterrent. Whereas minilaterals have become commonplace in defence policy, there has been little progress in shaping minilateral approaches to international development. A minilateral development partnership that is issue-based and aligns closely with strategic challenges – whether that is tackling Chinese influence, countering weak governance which is exploited by Russia, or preventing disinformation – can help to more flexibly and quickly counter the impact of adversarial nations that look to take the world down a darker path. Importantly, a UK-U.S.-led minilateral development partnership should be open to like-minded allies who can offer value in addressing key strategic issues through effective development assistance, whether that be Japan in democracy promotion and infrastructure development, Canada for engaging the private sector, or Australia for regional priorities in the South Pacific. The UK should push to give Commonwealth nations observer status in the Minerals Security Partnership to enable discussions on how Western nations and investors can better support sustainable, responsible, and high-quality mineral extraction and in-country processing across the Commonwealth. Critical minerals are the lifeblood of Western defence and industry. A shortage of minerals, or the choking of supply chains, would have disastrous economic and security consequences for the UK, U.S., and allied nations. As such, an ambition has arisen across the Western geopolitical bloc to diversify supply chains for critical minerals away from China. Should the diversification fail to arise, Beijing will have a greater ability to undermine the trajectory, opportunities, and resilience of the West. Not only this, but many African nations will see the dark side of Chinese mining operations, as the DRC and Zimbabwe already have. However, there has been a failure to diversify as private companies have either not invested in mining activities due to risk, or in some cases left some countries due to a deterioration in political and economic stability. Due to the dismantling of USAID and cuts to the UK’s and European development budgets, it will be significantly harder for the West to de-risk investments in mining, support in-country processing, and diversify supply chains. Any attempt to secure a steady supply of critical minerals that cannot be exploited by Beijing requires an effective diplomatic and development strategy. The UK is in a unique position to support the diversification of critical mineral supply chains. Many Commonwealth nations have significant supplies of minerals essential to technology and defence sectors. With many looking to diversify partnerships away from China due to egregious abuses of human rights and local standards, the UK’s role in the Commonwealth and continued influence can play a crucial role. By pressuring the White House to give Minerals Security Partnership observer status to Commonwealth countries – three Commonwealth nations are already members: the UK, Australia, and India – a forum can be created to engage better with countries holding significant reserves. Observer status is not new for the MSP. Indeed, in a MSP meeting held in Toronto in March 2025, Belgium, Poland, and New Zealand participated as observers.198 Ultimately, providing observer status can ensure greater and deeper conversations about how UK, U.S., and other Western private sector companies can engage in mining activities and improve development outcomes due to higher ESG standards. It would also help to indicate ways to overcome barriers to Western private sector participation in the mining sector, expand projects, align with strategic priorities for both MSP members and Commonwealth observers, enhance financial support, and collaborate on policy coordination.

This fall’s voter forecast for next fall
Midterm elections to the United States Congress are commonly held to be referenda on the performance of the party in the White House. Indeed, they have given rise to an “<mark>iron law</mark>” in these contests, <mark>foretell</mark>ing “the <mark>ruling party</mark> would <mark>experience</mark> a <mark>net loss</mark>” of seats. With few exceptions, this “law” has been followed. The present political makeup of the federal government, with a Republican president and Republican seat majority in both chambers, strongly implies noteworthy congressional losses for that party in 2026. Thus, the live question becomes, “Will the incumbent party be able to exercise majority control?”
The recent gubernatorial and state-level election results were considered by many to be an early measure of whether the “<mark>iron law</mark>” might be <mark>showing rust</mark>. While these results may be more anecdotal than predictive, much political punditry focused on them as the first test for 2026. Though polling indicated some uncertainty in the lead up to these races, and there were reasons to suspect a Republican upset in at least one major gubernatorial contest, we saw instead handy Democratic victories. To some observers, this was a surprise. But when we systematically compare changes in voter expectations earlier in the Trump term to now, we see voters have been updating their expectations for who will win in the 2026 midterm congressional elections. Indeed, from April 2025 to September 2025, voter attitudes shifted.
Our support for this claim comes from potential voters who were interviewed in national scientific surveys fielded by Verasight. The findings on citizen forecasting (CF) suggest voters have studied opinions about which party will control the House and Senate. Furthermore, the forecasts show corrosion of the “iron law.” Expectations empirically favor the possibility of a Republican retention of rule. However, those expectations are diminishing. In what follows, we assess two relevant surveys, one from April 2025 and then a later one from September 2025. As we shall see, expectations of a Republican majority have lessened over time. Still, the public gives the incumbent party the nod. By way of conclusion, we assess the accuracy of these predictions.
Survey I: April 2025
The polling firm Verasight, in its annual Midwest Political Science Omnibus Survey (April 2025), drew three sample modules of high scientific quality, each with 1,000 U.S. adults as the sample size. Respondents were randomly recruited by addresses, text messages, and online, with multi-step authentication. Data were weighted to match key socio-demographics: age, race/ethnicity, sex, income, education, region, metropolitan status, and partisanship. The margin of error, employing the classical random sampling formula, equals +/- 3.5% for the Elections module we used.
The citizen forecasting items utilized ask respondents their expectations about which party will win control of the chamber. As an example, the wording for the House is as follows:
“Thinking about the midterm elections in 2026, which party do you think will win control of the US House of Representatives?” (Response options: Democrats will win a large majority, Democrats will win a slim majority, Republicans will win a slim majority, Republicans will win a large majority). The same item format was employed for the Senate.
As can be seen, rather than using voter intentions to forecast, we focus on voter expectations, which ask “Who do you think will win?” This approach, called <mark>citizen forecasting</mark>, has a <mark>history of successfully predicting</mark> presidential election <mark>outcomes</mark>. At the legislative level, its application is more scarce, but has been used to predict legislative elections <mark>across the globe</mark>. For the upcoming 2026 US midterm elections, we utilize CF to project the aggregate outcome.

Further, placing a 95% confidence around these point estimates, and using the margin of error of +/- 3.5 points, we see that the Republican expectation never drops below 50% [54.5%, 61.5%], while the Democratic expectation never climbs to 50% [38.5%, 45.5%].
