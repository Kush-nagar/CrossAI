<mark>Effort‐reward imbalance</mark> at work <mark>is</mark> <mark>a</mark>n established <mark>psychosocial risk factor</mark> but there are also newer conceptualisations, such as <mark>procedural</mark> <mark>injustice</mark> (decisions at work lack consistency, openness and input from all affected parties) <mark>and</mark> relational injustice (<mark>problems in</mark> considerate and <mark>fair</mark> <mark>treatment</mark> of employees by supervisors). The authors examined whether procedural injustice and relational injustice are associated with employee health in addition to, and in combination with, effort‐reward imbalance.
Methods
Prospective survey data from two cohorts related to public‐sector employees: the 10‐Town study (n = 18 066 (78% women, age range 19–62) and the Finnish Hospital Personnel study (n = 4833, 89% women, age range 20–60). Self‐rated poor health, minor psychiatric morbidity and doctor‐diagnosed depression were assessed at baseline (2000–2) and at follow‐up (2004). To determine incident morbidity, baseline cases were excluded.

In multivariate models including age, sex, occupational status and all three psychosocial factors as predictors, high effort‐reward imbalance and either high procedural injustice or high relational injustice were associated with increased morbidity at follow‐up in both cohorts. After combining procedural and relational injustice into a single measure of organisational injustice, high effort‐reward imbalance and high injustice <mark>were</mark> both independently <mark>associated with</mark> health. For all outcome measures, a combination of high effort‐reward imbalance and high organisational injustice was related to <mark>a greater</mark> <mark>health risk</mark> than high effort‐reward imbalance or injustice alone.

Evidence from two independent occupational cohorts suggests that procedural and relational components of <mark>injustice</mark>, as a combined index, <mark>and</mark> effort‐reward <mark>imbalance</mark> <mark>are complementary risk factors</mark>.
A number of theoretical conceptualisations of stressful experience at work have been described, but only a few have proven their ability to predict the incidence of disease in prospective epidemiological studies.1 The effort‐reward <mark>imbalance</mark> model is one such concept that was repeatedly found to be associated with significantly <mark>increased risks of physical</mark> and mental <mark>disorders</mark>, such as<mark> cardiovascular disease</mark>, <mark>depression</mark>, <mark>alcohol</mark> <mark>dependence </mark>or <mark>type 2 diabetes</mark>.2,3 This model is based on the fundamental sociological notion of <mark>contractual reciprocity</mark> where any normative action provided by person A to person B that has some utility to B is expected to elicit an equivalent return from person B to A.4 As is the case with other types of relevant social exchange, this notion lies at the core of the employment contract which <mark>defines</mark> <mark>distinct obligations</mark> or tasks <mark>to be performed</mark> in exchange <mark>with</mark> equitable <mark>rewards</mark>.
The model of effort‐reward imbalance at work claims that <mark>lack of reciprocity</mark> between costs and gains (that is, high‐cost low‐gain conditions) occurs frequently under specific circumstances and <mark>elicits strong negative emotions</mark>, <mark>with</mark> particular propensity to sustained <mark>autonomic and neuroendocrine activation</mark> <mark>and</mark> their adverse long‐term <mark>consequences for health</mark>. In this model, three types of reward are distinguished: money, esteem and promotion prospects including job security. The specific <mark>circumstances</mark> in which effort‐reward imbalance is frequent <mark>are</mark> when employees have no alternative choice in the labour market, when they strive for <mark>highly competitive</mark> jobs, or when they exhibit a <mark>motivational</mark> pattern of excessive work‐related “<mark>overcommitment</mark>”.
In the framework of justice theory, effort‐reward <mark>imbalance</mark> <mark>concerns injustice of</mark> exchange or, if <mark>scarce rewards</mark> are to be assigned to several people, distributive injustice.5 It is of interest to learn how this type of injustice relates to other types of injustice at work, such as procedural and relational injustice.6,7,8 Moreover, it is unknown whether each type of injustice elicits similar experiences of psychosocial stress at work and, thus, may be associated with comparable health outcomes. <mark>Procedural</mark> justice concerns the <mark>conformity to agreed‐upon </mark>formal <mark>procedures</mark> within an organisation that <mark>guarantee fair</mark> and consistent <mark>decision‐making</mark>, whereas relational justice describes the extent to which employees are treated with respect and fairness by their supervisors. In several recent epidemiological investigations, procedural and/or relational injustice were <mark>related to adverse</mark> <mark>health</mark>.9,10,11,12,13 However, these reports raised some critical comments as the conceptual and operational similarities with and differences from earlier established concepts of psychosocial stress at work remain unclear.14 To our knowledge, no previous study has examined whether procedural injustice and relational injustice are complementary or redundant to effort‐reward imbalance as health risk factors.
In this paper, we compare the notions of injustice of exchange (effort‐reward imbalance), procedural and relational injustice at the operational level, and we examine their independent contribution to employee health in two prospective epidemiological investigations. Moreover, in the Discussion section, conceptual similarities and differences between the three notions of injustice are elaborated.

Introduction Political science professors have been using simulations to enhance student learn- ing for years. Simulations are interesting, a nice change of pace from the traditional lecture, and allow for more student participation in class. Simulations are widely studied in the political science literature and there are a number of significant benefits related to engagement that scholars have suggested simulations provide. Simulations create situations in which students can practice skills they might use outside the classroom, can increase participation and engagement, create opportu- nities for critical thinking, analysis, and appreciation of diverse viewpoints, and may allow risk-taking in a safe environment (McIntosh 2001; DeLeon 2008; Dack, van Hover, and Hicks 2016). There is also evidence that they increase the civic engage- ment of students and fulfill the public engagement mission of higher education, which has received increased attention across the last several decades and especially in our current moment of political polarization (McCartney, Bennion, and Simp- son 2013). However, much of the literature on simulations is practitioner-focused, centers around pedagogical strategies of implementation, and rests upon author ex- periences with a single case or two (Asal 2005; Asal and Blake 2006; Shellman and Turan 2006; Raymond 2010; Pettenger, West, and Niki Young 2014; Wright-Maley 2015b; Sears 2018; Battaglini et al. 2019; Deejay, Rublee, and Zech 2019; Kartal 2020). Thus, while simulations appear in the literature as examples of experiential pedagogy, more research is needed to uncover their effects on students beyond the classroom. As a tool with not only the potential to deepen student learning, but also the ca- pacity to impact student civic engagement more broadly, political simulations play a role in shaping a wide variety of student outcomes. While the consensus among scholars in this area is generally that simulations are not the most effective strat- egy for promoting rote learning of content knowledge, they are effective tools for fostering engagement, complex thinking and problem-solving, and soft skills devel- opment (Haack 2008; Wright-Maley 2015a; Hammond and Albert 2020; Toomey, Zhou, and Yan 2020). Rather than focusing on simulation as a means of improv- ing knowledge development in the classroom, in this paper we ask whether and to what extent simulations are related to non-academic student outcomes, including outward-facing civic engagement as well as private civic behaviors and dispositions. First, we review the literature on the relationship between simulations and each of these outcomes. Then, we present new evidence from a pre-post, comparison group study, which builds on the existing literature by offering a more methodologically robust set of findings. Specifically, we focus on Model UN-style simulations, which seem likely to be a good candidate for fostering civic engagement due to their fo- cus on current events and their multilateral consensus-based dialogue, in addition to the perspective taking and problem-solving common across simulation formats. We find that simulations are related to measures of civic engagement among under- graduate students in our sample. Finally, we discuss the implications of these results for political science education and higher education more broadly. Civic Engagement For the purposes of this study, we define civic engagement as the public-facing behaviors one undertakes as part of democratic participation and citizenship in a broad sense. Political scientists have typically defined civic engagement in the context of three categories of engagement, including political engagement, com- munity participation, and public expression. Political engagement includes activ- ities such as voting, campaign involvement, and running for office; community participation refers to involvement with non-profit organizations, volunteering and fundraising, and community problem-solving or committee membership; public ex- pression refers to actions such as protesting, boycotting, or signing petitions, con- tacting elected officials, or expressing opinions in the media through mechanisms such as op-eds or blog posts (Putnam 2000; Zukin et al. 2006; Levine 2007). One way simulations might increase civic engagement is by increasing aware- ness of and interest in global challenges. Zappile, Beers, and Raymond (2017) ran simulations in four courses across three campuses related to the situation in post-earthquake Haiti and found that students reported gains in global engage- ment and increased feelings of global citizenship. In addition, political simula- tions can increase student interest in the political process. For example, Caruson (2005) ran a long-term simulation of a campaign in her course that included aspects such as interest groups and web-based campaigning. During the evalua- tion of the simulation, 97.6 percent of the students indicated that the simulation caused them to be more interested in the election process and 71.4 percent in- dicated that they would seriously consider working for a political campaign in the future. Mariani (2007) also created a multi-course campaign simulation and found that students who participated reported that they would follow campaigns more closely. The author concludes that the simulations gave students the tools to understand how campaigns work and encouraged them to participate in politics more. The opportunity to engage with complex ideas and to take on the role of a polit- ical actor through simulation may also be an effective means of fostering civic en- gagement by developing students’ civic interest and political efficacy (Kartal 2020). Niemi and Junn (1998) found that participation in social studies simulations among high school students led to more frequent discussion of current events, which have been associated with a wide variety of positive civic measures including interest in current events outside of school, as well as political empowerment and efficacy (Joe Bellon 2000; Hess and Posselt 2002; Diana Mutz 2006; Campbell 2008; Hess 2009; Mirra et al. 2016). Simulations may also improve students’ civic engagement out- comes by altering their beliefs and perceptions about the world, their role in it, and their sense that they can be active participants (Bredemeier and Greenblat 1981; Gehlbach et al. 2008; Ioannou et al. 2009; Ganzler 2010; Gehlbach 2011; Yukhymenko 2011; Kaftan and Linantud 2020). Lo (2017) used semi-structured in- terviews, classroom observation, and questionnaires to conduct a qualitative cross- case analysis of the experience of two students participating in a series of in-class political simulations. She found that role play “informed the development of their civic identities” and that authentic simulations helped them link their behavior dur- ing class to “how they might participate in politics in real life” (Lo 2017, 200–201). Civic Dispositions From a more personal perspective, simulations may be an effective way of improving civic dispositions and private activities such as keeping up with the news and current events, discussing politics with friends and family, and civic practices such as seek- ing multiple perspectives and being open to alternative viewpoints on social and political issues. The vibrant literature in this area suggests that simulations are ef- fective tools for fostering soft-skill development because they can provide a sense of accessibility to complex moral or policy problems and allow students to explore the interests or experiences of others, which can develop more nuanced understand- ing of complicated phenomena (Preston and Cottam 1997; Colella 2000; Marsh and Bucy 2002; Williams and Williams 2007; Parker et al. 2011; Stephens, Feinberg, and Zack 2013; Wright-Maley 2015a; Meschoulam et al. 2019; Butcher and Njonguo 2020; Leib and Ruppel 2020).

Existing research on simulations also suggests that they may improve students’ ability to understand different points of view by fostering perspective recognition, empathy, and care for others (Byrnes and Kiger 1990; Ben-Peretz 2003; Schweber 2003; Else 2006; Ganzler 2010; Maitles and McKelvie 2010; Head 2020; Shannon 2020). Bachen, Hernández-Ramos, and Raphael (2012) look specifically at empathy, which they define as a connectedness across ethnic, racial, linguistic, and nation-state lines. Their study found that <mark>students</mark> who took part in their simulation <mark>scored higher in</mark> posttest global <mark>empathy</mark>. In addition, they found that students identifying with the characters they played had increased global empathy. This suggests that taking part in a simulation does encourage students to meaningfully step into the experiences of very different actors. Simulations provide an opportunity for students to gain an empathetic view of others and help them understand historical and contemporary realities outside of their own lives in a deeper way (Pellegrino, Lee, and d’Erizans 2012). West and Halvorson (2021) found that the emotional reactions elicited by the difficult topics addressed in a simulation are important for sparking reflection. Similarly, Krain and Lantis (2006) used a comparison group design and found that after participating in a simulation on torture and war crimes, students who took part in that simulation reported a better understanding both of country preferences and of the overall complexity of the issue. The authors conclude that the simulations indeed helped students understand different perspectives.

In addition to gaining deeper understanding of the roles they take on in a simulation, students are able to engage in perspective taking and deepen their understanding of a variety of viewpoints. For example, Andrew and Meligrana (2012) carried out a series of simulations that focused on various methods of negotiation and gathered data from two sets of questionnaires. Students highlighted the importance of discovering the interests of the parties that lay behind their stated positions, and they reported that understanding the other party’s viewpoints was a key component of their learning process. The authors found that differences in responses between the pre- and post-simulation questionnaires were due to the simulations’ ability to change student knowledge, perceptions, and opinions. These effects may even help students to explore perspectives that run strongly counter to their own viewpoints. Cuhadar and Kampf (2014) carried out a cross-national study of Turkish, Israeli, American, and Palestinian students. American and Turkish students reported that their original perceptions were altered after the simulation, but Jewish and Palestinian students did not change their perspectives regarding the rightness of each side after playing the game. The authors conclude that considering these mixed results, they still find that game simulations contribute to perspective taking, even if in a limited manner. Thus, based on evidence which suggests that simulations are positively associated with students’ broad civic development, we consider whether and to what extent participation in simulations is associated with college students’ civic engagement and civic dispositions. We study whether participation in political and international affairs simulations leads to increases in civic attitudes and behaviors by comparing pre-post student survey responses between groups who did and did not take part in a simulation. This contributes to the existing literature in two ways: first, by moving away from the focus on learning in an academic sense in order to specifically consider the broader civic effects of simulations, and secondly by offering more rigorous evidence for these effects by including a comparison group in our analysis and drawing evidence from a variety of simulation experiences, rather than a single pre-post classroom group. Method Sample This study uses a comparison group, difference-in-difference design to estimate the effects of participating in a simulation on student civic outcomes. Participants were students at a university in the Southwestern United States who were enrolled in at least one of four political science classes or an academic Model United Nations (MUN) club during at least one of two semesters during which data collection took place. All of these groups had a political science focus and some of them involved political simulation. Students in the simulation or “treatment” group participated in MUN or international affairs simulations either as part of a MUN club or a class. Students in the MUN class prepared for and participated in MUN-style simulations during class time. Students in the club undertook more serious preparation for a conference simulation, as well as participating in a number of smaller practice simulations. During the second semester of data collection, students in an introductory-level World Politics class participated in a series of international affairs simulations focused on illustrating major issues and actors in global affairs. These simulations were similar to the MUN simulations in that they were multilateral, represented a broad range of stakeholders, and focused on generating shared solutions over simulating the effects of unilateral actions. Students in the comparison group took political science classes but did not participate in political simulations. These students took either a 200-level political ideologies course or a Senior capstone course for Politics and International Affairs majors. A total of 264 students were surveyed as part of the study, but only those who took both the pre- and post-semester survey were included in the analytical sample of 184 students. Of these, 125 students (or about 68 percent of respondents) participated in one of the simulation groups. Twelve of the students in the MUN club also took one of the non-simulation courses. Observable characteristics of the sample are displayed in table 1. Overall, students who participated in the simulations were slightly younger and earlier in their college careers when compared to the rest of the sample, and much less likely to be political science or international relations majors. However, it is worth noting that if anything, we would expect these characteristics to lead the simulation group to perform lower overall on measures related to civic engagement, since we typically consider political experience and interest to have positive associations with engagement. Nevertheless, in order to further account for these differences, we include these covariates as controls in the model, as well as including fixed effects for each class/club group. Procedure Students were administered pre-test surveys in the second week of class in hopes of being able to survey a stable set of students after the add/drop period at the beginning of the semester had died down. Surveys were about 28 questions long and divided into five sections: (1) personal information, (2) political engagement and interest, (3) factual test questions about current events (both domestic and international), (4) political alignment within their community, and (5) civic engagement and participation during the semester. This study uses data from the first, second, and fifth sections. Students were administered the same survey at the end of the semester. Responses were matched for individual respondents at both time points in order to identify changes in students’ responses. Measures We estimate the relationship between participating in simulations and measures of positive civic engagement outcomes that the literature suggests simulations might improve. We use two student self-reports of involvement in activities with a social or political focus as measures of civic engagement. The first measure is an absolute count of the number of different types of social/political activities students report taking part in and the second is a measure of how much time they spent on those activities each week, ranging from 0 “none” to 3, “more than 10 hours per week.” At time 2, the mean activity count was 1.92 with a minimum of 0, maximum of 8, and standard deviation of 1.77. The mean of the time spent scale was 0.89 with a range of 0–3 and a standard deviation of 0.85. We also measured students’ civic dispositions thorough their engagement in political conversations, efficacy, and ability to understand different points of view, using a series of lichert-type questions. Students were asked “How frequently do you discuss political issues with people whose opinions are different from yours?” and given response items ranging from (0) “never” to (4) “daily.” Questions about efficacy and perspective taking asked students how confident they were defending their opinions, how willing they were to change their minds when learning new information, and how easy they found it to understand where people with different opinions were coming from. Each of these item response scales ranged from 0 to 3.

Independent variables included pre-test scores of the same items, as well as an indicator for participating in the simulation group. While we recognize that there were differences in the simulations experienced across the group, our purpose here is not to test a specific kind of simulation for efficacy, but rather to try to identify overall effects of simulation as a generalized activity associated with students’ civic development. The <mark>characteristics</mark> of the simulations that are <mark>shared across all groups</mark> are those of what we consider to be “MUN-style” simulations more broadly:
<mark>Debate</mark> by committee <mark>of a real, controversial</mark>, public <mark>issue</mark>, with participants representing real actors or stakeholders.

Whatever happened to social revolution? If one concedes that <mark>we live in a</mark> particular <mark>social order</mark> – capitalist, racist, extractive and patriarchal – <mark>hurtling towards extinction in a matter of decades,</mark> then the <mark>fundamental transformation</mark> of that order <mark>becomes a</mark> biological as much as a <mark>political imperative</mark>. <mark>Yet</mark> fundamental, <mark>rapid transformation</mark> of the social order <mark>appears nowhere </mark>in the programme of any significant political movement today. As Enzo Traverso notes in his magisterial intellectual history of revolution, the closest thing to a programme of totalising change is to be found not on the Left but amongst the Islamist epigones of Qutb and Khomeini. ‘The new anti-capitalist movements of recent years do not resonate with any of the left traditions of the past’, writes Traverso, but ‘being orphans … must reinvent themselves.’ This situation is a most puzzling one. On a cruder metric than that of fundamental social transformation, <mark>the second decade of the</mark> twenty-first <mark>century has been</mark> <mark>unprecedentedly ‘revolutionary’, in the</mark> sense of the depth and <mark>breadth of popular protest</mark>. <mark>The Arab revolutions</mark> of 2011 both initiated and <mark>prefigured this trend</mark>, <mark>marking</mark>, in the words of the global events database project GDELT in 2019, ‘<mark>a</mark> near-<mark>vertical inflection point in</mark> which two decades of relative calm instantly reversed into several years of elevated <mark>global unrest’</mark>. <mark>From</mark> the <mark>anti-austerity protests</mark> of Southern Europe <mark>to</mark> the <mark>anti-racist rebellions</mark> in the United States, amongst many other examples, apparently <mark>leaderless</mark> and <mark>spontaneous</mark> <mark>mass movements swarmed</mark> into streets and squares. The 2010s witnessed a wave of protest greater than any since that sparked by the Russian Revolution of 1917. As Samuel J. Brannen, Christian S. Haig, and Katherine Schmidt note in their report ‘The Age of Mass Protests’, across the world anti-government protests increased by 11.5 per cent year on year throughout the 2010s. All of the five largest demonstrations in US history occurred during the decade. The Middle East and North Africa saw the ‘largest concentration of activity’ and sub-Saharan Africa the ‘fastest rate of growth’; a dip in protest activity in 2013–17 was followed by a renewed global expansion of protest leading to 290.5 per cent more protests at the end of the decade than at the start. Table from Beissinger, ‘The Urban Advantage in Revolution’, 2020. This is another topography of Traverso’s revolutionary ‘long twentieth century’. <mark>Four peaks</mark>, prior to the Arab revolutions of 2011 – which rank with the periods surrounding the establishment and fall of the Soviet Union in terms of new revolutionary episodes per year – <mark>are visible</mark> in this illustration. The first peak is the revolutionary wave that preceded and then was accelerated by the Russian Revolution in 1915–19, continuing into the early 1920s; a smaller and more chronologically isolated burst at the end of World War II is followed by the third peak, counter-intuitively at its height in the early 1960s (corresponding to the wave of anti-colonial revolutions) and then slightly decreasing in the latter part of the decade; and the fourth begins its upward ascent after 1975 to peak in the collapse of the Soviet bloc in the early 1990s. <mark>The overall trend is upward, with a renewed ascent from 2010 onwards</mark>. <mark>Why</mark>, then, <mark>the appearance not just of</mark> political and social <mark>stasis</mark> <mark>but</mark> active <mark>retrogression</mark>? Where is the twenty-first century equivalent of the ‘Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen’, of Dessalines’ ‘Empire of liberty’, or the ‘Decrees of the Second Congress of Soviets’? In part, the answer is contained within the question. What <mark>Beissinger’s chart represents</mark> are <mark>not</mark> revolutions in the sense of revolutionary <mark>outcomes but</mark> revolutionary <mark>episodes</mark>: the ‘mass siege of an established government with the aims of displacing the incumbent regime and substantially altering the political or social order’. The distinction between episode, or revolutionary situation, and outcome is accompanied here by another – between (political) revolutions that transform at most the form of the state, and those (social revolutions) that undo and remake the social relations of which the state forms a part. Political revolutionary <mark>episodes are</mark> <mark>becoming more common</mark>: social revolutionary <mark>outcomes so rare as to be almost unimaginable</mark>. <mark>This shrinking of the revolutionary horizon has political effects</mark>. In states lacking the apparatus of procedural democracy that accompanies bourgeois domination in the West, revolutionary episodes tend to become limited to the installation of such an apparatus. Global warming and worldwide financial networks are examples of what the ecological theorist Timothy Morton calls hyperobjects.
