Open-source case doc read by Fairmont Prep PP (season Elo rank #92) on the 2025-2026 november-december PF topic — "Resolved: The United States federal government should require technology companies to provide lawful access to encrypted communications." — at: Glenbrooks Speech and Debate Tournament.

In 2020, a group of senators proposed the Lawful Access to Encrypted Data Act. As Security Expert Ken Olstead finds, the bill would’ve required companies to build encryption backdoors to assist with government investigations, almost exactly mirroring the affirmative plan. However, the proposal failed spectacularly, never making it out of committee.
This was not because our legislators are soft on crime, but because they recognized a fundamental truth about encryption: you can’t weaken it for adversaries without weakening it for everyone.
Because collective punishment is never the solution, Fairmont negates.
Our first argument is privacy.
Encryption is the linchpin. Schneier of Lawfare writes that by scrambling digital information so only the sender and recipient can read it, encryption protects personal data and devices, shielding citizens from mass surveillance.
However, Research Analyst Grant Baker warns that granting the government access to encrypted communications would let authorities spy on citizens, including journalists, activists, and government critics, eroding checks on government power and the privacy of hundreds of millions.
This risk isn’t hypothetical. The Guardian reports that the NSA (national security agency) has been caught spying on citizens by tapping phone lines and monitoring private conversations; a scandal made public by Edward Snowden in 2016. Legal Expert Corynne McSherry adds that the FBI has repeatedly exploited loopholes and pressured companies to conduct millions of warrantless searches.
Canada’s encryption policy confirms the danger. Professor Ponsford of McGill University finds that in Canada, which permits lawful access, agencies routinely obtain data without warrants and even compensate companies that comply. Their requests are granted 99.9% of the time.
But the impact goes beyond our borders. Kravets finds that adoption sets a dangerous precedent. If the U.S. demanded access to encrypted data, other governments would do the same.
Professor Napolitano of UC Berkeley explains that countries like Turkey have slid from democracy into authoritarianism by using surveillance to control and repress their populations.
And history proves the danger of unchecked power: Professor Rummel documents that the Soviet Union killed 62 million, communist China executed 35 million, and Nazi Germany murdered 21 million—grim reminders of how authoritarian regimes turn control into catastrophe.

Our second argument regards the economy.
Award-winning reporter Eleanor Pringle writes that tech growth is the only sector holding the economy together --- if it experienced a downturn, shocks would spill globally.
Unfortunately, weakened encryption would create economic devastation. Policy Fellow Joel Gladwin explains exposing the communication privacy of millions of consumers would collapse digital trust. Investors would flee from US technology companies en masse, collapsing aggregate demand and destroying the revenues of tech businesses. Not only that, but businesses would leave US markets to regain access to more stable markets.
This is empirically proven: Redacto in 2025 confirms that when the UK required Apple to create a backdoor in 2016, Apple instead refused to cooperate and deactivated their services in the region. The same exact thing happened with Signal in Sweden.
The impacts are devastating: IT Expert Thomas Thurman explains that a world without encryption would collapse critical infrastructure like banking and e-commerce. The economic impact would be in the trillions of dollars, outweighing even the Great Depression.
According to the IMF, the next global recession could send 900 million into poverty, three times the entire US population. Professor Mueller concludes that poverty is associated with a 71% increased risk of death.
At the end of the day, when the history of Congressional decisions supports a rejection of the aff, it’s clear lawful access will only do more harm than good. Because when the heart of our democracy and the global economy is at risk, we must not break the backbone of our communications.

From this perspective, weakening encryption is likely to impose costs on securing data, and pose a threat to “trust” in digital products and services, including trust in using the Internet and other data networks for e-commerce, which will increasingly include the entire economy.
Increased compliance and security costs (69%), legal and remediation costs (46%), and damage to trust, reputation, and brand (42%) were highlighted as the biggest threats to SMEs from mandated encryption backdoors. In the simplest economic analysis, the increased cost of providing “trusted” services will raise the costs of supply and decrease end-users’ willingness to pay. This would suggest an upward shift in aggregate supply and a downward shift in aggregate demand, resulting in a new higher equilibrium price at a lower level of aggregate demand. Prices would be higher and aggregate demand would be lower, producing what economists refer to as a “deadweight loss” associated with encryption backdoors.
