How do you prevent the spread of illegal imagery without compromising the privacy of users? That was the question facing engineers at <mark>Apple. Under pressure from</mark> Western <mark>governments to crack down on</mark> the dissemination of child sexual abuse material (<mark>CSAM</mark>), the tech giant was <mark>also keen to preserve</mark> the ability of its users to communicate <mark>private</mark>ly using <mark>end-to-end encryption</mark>.
Their <mark>solution</mark> was a <mark>perceptual hashing algorithm</mark> called NeuralHash. The software harnessed a process known as ‘hashing,’ wherein <mark>images are broken</mark> up <mark>into segments and given an id</mark>entifying <mark>signature – </mark>usually a garbled series of letters or numbers – <mark>known as a ‘hash.’</mark>
In most cases, this signature is compared between the sender’s device and that of the receiver, to ensure that the image sent has not been corrupted in transit. A perceptual hashing algorithm, meanwhile, is capable of matching a known image to its hash, even when that image has been manipulated in such a way as to elude conventional scanning.
The method is computationally expensive, meaning that it usually works best when embedded inside the servers where the images are stored. By extension, perceptual hashing algorithms require access to the hash associated with the original image to know what they’re looking for.
Up until the late 2010s that wasn’t a problem, with millions of people content to send, receive and report images over unencrypted channels like MSN and Facebook Messenger. The rise of encrypted messaging services like WhatsApp and Signal, however, vastly reduced the volume of imagery that perceptual hashing algorithms could analyse.
Where Apple’s engineers innovated with NeuralHash, however, was to place their software inside users’ iPhones – a technique known as ‘client-side scanning’. <mark>Wary of creating a backdoor</mark> into their customers’ private messaging channels, the <mark>company instead tasked the algorithm with vetting images</mark> just before they were encrypted and uploaded to their iCloud account. If the number of hashes pertaining to suspected CSAM exceeded more than 30, Apple would then decrypt the images, manually review them and, if necessary, call law enforcement.
Is NeuralHash secure?
When NeuralHash was first announced in August 2021, security researchers cried foul. While all critics acknowledged the gravity of the problem the software was designed to solve, many believed that Apple’s solution came at far too high a price.
Aside from the sinister implications of a corporate entity scanning private content on users’ devices, some argued the company was introducing new and unnecessary security vulnerabilities, too. “Apple is compromising the phone that you and I own and operate, without any of us having a say in the matter,” wrote tech analyst Ben Thompson. “The capability to reach into a user’s phone now exists, and there is nothing an iPhone user can do to get rid of it.”
Acknowledging the severity of public criticism of NeuralHash, Apple scaled back its plans before indefinitely delaying the release of the software the following month. That did not, however, end the debate over client-side scanning. The UK and the EU, for example, remain enthusiastic about its capabilities in thwarting the spread of CSAM, with both publicly stating their willingness to compel platforms to institute much stronger content moderation policies aimed at its eradication.
If anything, NeuralHash was taken as an example of a tech company finally doing something proactive about this issue – one that could define the future of content moderation.

<mark>● Lawful Access</mark>
○ Message History: With appropriate legal process, can <mark>the service provider disclose messages</mark> that a user previously sent or received <mark>to law enforcement</mark>?
○ Message Interception: With appropriate legal process, can the service provider disclose messages that a user sends or receives going forward to law enforcement?
Note that each of these technical properties is distinct. Each poses a different set of design challenges. And, importantly, there may be room for stakeholder consensus on content moderation that has proven elusive on lawful access.
In the balance of this paper, I will discuss how to possibly implement each of the content moderation properties.
User Reporting
In an end-to-end encrypted messaging system, a service provider cannot read the content of messages—but intended recipients can. Enabling user reports is, therefore, not a technical challenge. If a user receives a message that he or she believes contains harmful content, the user
can simply submit that message to the service provider (e.g., with a “Report Message” option).
The technical challenge, rather, is authenticating user reports. How can a service provider have confidence that a specific sender actually sent the flagged message, and at a specific time? Authentication is especially important for law enforcement, since harmful content in a messaging app may become forensic evidence in a criminal proceeding.
There is, thankfully, a straightforward means of establishing whether a message reported by a user is authentic. The service provider can associate each message with a cryptographic statement of authenticity that it was sent by a particular sender at a particular time, without learning the content of the message. If a user reports a message to the service provider, the provider can then check the statement of authenticity and verify the flagged message.
Facebook dubbed this design “message franking,” and it is already deployed in Facebook Messenger. More recent academic work has formalized and expanded on Facebook’s approach (Grubbs et al. 2017, Dodis et al. 2018, Leontiadis & Vaudenay 2018, Tyagi et al. 2019, Huguenin-Dumittan & Leontiadis 2019).

Introduction
Perceptual hashing, also known as robust hashing, is a content fingerprinting technique that generates similar binary hashes for perceptually similar contents and different hashes for different contents [1]. Perceptual hashing is useful in various applications such as content moderation, authentication, and detection. Numerous perceptual hash algorithms (PHAs) have been developed or deployed for practical applications [2]. Early examples include the algorithm used on YouTube to identify copyrighted material [3] and algorithms used in online image search engines of Google, Microsoft Bing, etc [4].
Recently, <mark>PHAs have</mark> gained more <mark>critical importance</mark>, particularly <mark>in</mark> responding to the requirement of <mark>reporting illicit content</mark>. For instance, in cases involving Child Sexual Abuse Media (CSAM), US media service providers are legally obliged to examine suspected illegal content and report it to a “Cyber Tip Line” operated by the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC). Microsoft’s <mark>PhotoDNA and Meta’s PDQ were</mark> immediately <mark>deployed for this purpose</mark> [5][6][7].
With a growing emphasis on user privacy, technologies like <mark>end-to-end encryption</mark> have become prevalent. This shift <mark>has led to the deployment of PHAs on the client side</mark> before data encryption [8]. For example, Apple has proposed to deploy NeuralHash on all its iOS devices [9]. However, this client-side deployment has raised significant concerns among the client users about their data privacy, which inadvertently arouses a widespread criticism regarding the security and privacy-preserving capabilities of PHAs [10]. Digital Platforms and Platform Economics
A digital platform is an online entity that facilitates interaction between two or more distinct economic entity², allowing them to create, communicate, exchange value, or collaborate. A platform economy is a business model where digital platforms enable multiple economic entities to come together and interact in a way that “benefits” all the entities involved. Examples include Uber, Swiggy, Amazon, Instagram, and WhatsApp. Unlike traditional markets, platform economies are shaped by:
● Network effects
● Positive feedback loops
● Switching costs
● Lock-in
Together, these forces can propel one platform to dominance and erect high barriers to entry for challengers.
Network Effects
Network effects are force multipliers that allow a product/platform to become stronger when more people join or use it. For example, if most of your friends are on Instagram, it makes sense to join the same network. The larger the groups of people on a network, the stronger the network becomes. There are different types of network effects — Direct Network Effects, Indirect Network Effects, and Data Network Effects.
Direct Network Effects occur when more users join a one-sided platform (like a messaging app). As the user base grows, so do the opportunities to communicate and connect, driving both higher adoption rates and an ever-larger network.
Indirect Network Effects occur on two-sided platforms that connect distinct user groups — typically buyers and sellers. An increase in users on one side of the platform enhances trade and business opportunities for users on the other side. For example, on ride-hailing platforms like Uber, which connect drivers and riders, more drivers on the road improve the experience for riders, and vice versa.
Network Effects as the Engine for WhatsApp’s Growth
Launched by Jan Koum in 2009, WhatsApp 1.0 began as a service for updating address-book statuses, but quickly evolved into a way for people to message friends via their status updates. Koum — known for his ethos of “No Ads! No Games! No Gimmicks!” — then transformed WhatsApp 2.0 into a clean, ad-free messaging platform at a time when most phones were internet-connected³ and the only alternatives were SMS, MMS, or BlackBerry’s BBM. This initial critical mass of users created the perfect conditions for WhatsApp to corner the market: with most of a person’s contacts already on WhatsApp, new users automatically adopted it as their default messaging app, driving direct network effects.
WhatsApp’s integration with businesses further reinforced indirect network effects: a larger user base attracted more companies, which in turn reached more users and expanded the platform’s ecosystem.
Positive Feedback Loops
In systems thinking, complex networks usually have feedback loops, which can either be positive or negative. Positive feedback loops reinforce a particular feature or behaviour of the network, thereby leading to exponential changes in the system. Negative feedback loops, on the other hand, produce effects that run counter to the changes observed in the system. Both these loops act so that the system eventually achieves its version of homeostasis.
Positive Feedback and Tipping: Achieving Market Dominance
WhatsApp’s growing user base, combined with its intuitive interface and strong UX features, created a positive feedback loop that drove further adoption. Rapid expansion in key markets such as India and Brazil accelerated this viral growth. Together, network effects and the feedback loop tipped the market in WhatsApp’s favor, building a wide moat around its user base and producing a winner-take-all dynamic — evident in monthly active users rising from 10 million in October 2010 to 100 million in October 2012.⁴

Switching costs may be social (losing established connections) or pecuniary/transactional. When these costs are high, they effectively lock users into a platform, <mark>even if</mark> <mark>its features</mark> or pricing <mark>become</mark> economically <mark>unattractive</mark>⁵.
