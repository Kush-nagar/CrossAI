SUMMARY Rapid advancements in clean energy technologies have spurred a significant surge in China’s demand for critical minerals. However,
developing clean-energy-critical minerals (CECMs) increasingly threatens China’s biodiversity. This study conducts a comprehensive spatial
analysis of the impacts of CECMs and fossil energy minerals (FEMs) on China’s protected areas (PAs). We found that the impacts of CECMs are
exceeding those of FEMs. Within the boundaries of PAs, we identified 1,338 developed mining ore fields, with an additional 618 earmarked for
potential mining activities. Approximately 6,307 km2 of PAs directly overlap with ore fields, and 167,551 km2 are indirectly impacted by energy
mining activities. Our findings suggest that imposing strict mining restrictions in PAs could mitigate 67% of the
mining threat. This study underscores the urgent necessity for proactive conservation strategies to
safeguard China’s PAs from the escalating risks posed by the expansion of clean energy technology.
Keywords Biodiversity protected area energy transition critical minerals Mining Introduction Protecting biodiversity is a global priority,
underscored by international agreements such as the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)1 and the Convention on Biological
Diversity (CBD).2 The 2022 Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (GBF) emphasizes the urgency of combating biodiversity loss with
ambitious targets for 2020–2050.3 Establishing protected areas (PAs) is critical to addressing increasing human pressures on biodiversity.4 PAs
can effectively prevent biodiversity loss, especially for vulnerable species with small geographic ranges.5,6 However, mineral extraction,
fundamental to modern society, poses significant risks to PAs through habitat destruction,7 soil erosion,8 and releasing pollutants,7,9 with direct
and indirect impacts encroaching on them and threatening their ecological integrity.10,11 Large-scale mining affects approximately 49 million
km2 of land globally, with over 50% of primary metal deposits in biodiverse regions.12 About 7% of iron, copper, bauxite, and zinc mines overlap
with PAs, and 27% of iron mines are within a 10 km radius outside the boundaries of PAs.13 The global shift toward clean energy technologies
presents opportunities and risks for biodiversity conservation.14,15 While the energy transition is critical for mitigating climate change and its
associated biodiversity loss, the increased demand for mineral resources to support clean energy technologies places new pressures on
ecosystems.16 Multiple studies have indicated spatial congruence between key mineral areas for clean-energy-critical minerals (CECMs) and
biodiversity conservation zones.17 For instance, Junk et al. analyzed the impact of mining activities on African great apes,18 while Sonter et al.
quantified the overlap between minerals associated with renewable energy production and biodiversity hotspots.12 These studies mainly focus
on the effects of mining activities on specific species or minerals related to certain types of clean energy technologies, such as those used in
wind and solar power. However, it remains unclear whether the extensive critical minerals required for energy transition will lead to greater or
less biodiversity loss compared to traditional fossil energy minerals (FEMs). Addressing this gap is crucial for a deeper understanding of the
relationship between mining activities and biodiversity and for informing policy decisions promoting energy transition while protecting
biodiversity. It will also contribute to achieving multiple SDGs and conservation targets of the GBF (Figure 1). Figure 1 Mining activities threaten
biodiversity Show full captionFigure viewer China occupies a preeminent position in the global landscape of energy transition minerals, ranking
among the top three global producers of copper, graphite, rare-earth elements, and lithium. It is particularly notable for its graphite and
rare-earth-element reserves, which are among the world’s highest.19 With China’s vigorous pursuit of energy transition, the demand for mineral
resources used in clean energy technologies is expected to increase significantly.20 China’s domestic mineral mining production has grown
significantly in recent years.21 Non-ferrous metal production reached 74.7 million tons in 2023, up 7.1% from 2022.22 While critical material

Previous studies have predominantly relied on large-scale global mining datasets. While valuable for broad analysis, these studies often
overlooked the importance of local data and specific threats posed by artisanal and small-scale mines that are prevalent in regions like
China.12,25 To address existing gaps, this study identifies the spatial consistencies between critical mining ore fields for clean energy
technologies and key biodiverse regions by examining mining activities inside and outside PAs. To this end, we utilized publicly available data on
registered mining ore field datasets in China to classify and refine various mineral resources. Subsequently, we delineated impact zones around
mining ore fields (Figure 1) and integrated the dataset of Chinese mining locations with the distribution range of PAs. We aim to provide insights
that align with China’s energy transition goals while safeguarding its rich biodiversity. Results Mining ore field distribution Our study identified

density gradually decreasing as development progresses from post-operation to future projections. Notably, PAs overlapping with mining

threats to China’s PAs Concerns about biodiversity loss have focused primarily on agriculture and
forestry, as these activities lead to large-scale land-use changes, such as deforestation.26,27 GBF Target 10 also
highlights the need to reduce biodiversity impacts from the agriculture, forestry, and aquaculture
sectors.3 However, our research reveals that the impact of mining activities should not be overlooked. Several studies
have also raised similar concerns, emphasizing the importance of paying attention to biodiversity losses driven by
mining. For example, habitat loss from deforestation is a major driver of biodiversity loss, and research
indicates that mining is gradually becoming a leading global cause of deforestation.28,29 Sonter et al. reveal
that large traditional and renewable energy mines are threatening global biodiversity hotspots.12 Beyond land-use
impacts, mining affects biodiversity through pollution of hazardous substances.30,31 Mining leaves tailings
that contain waste and residue from the beneficiation process and cause surface damage that needs
timely repair.32,33 The impact of hazardous materials can extend beyond operational phases. For example,
historic and current metal mining activities produce hazardous concentrations of toxic wastes that can affect the ecological security of
watersheds.31 Approximately 9% of tailing facilities globally are within PAs, with an additional 20% located within 5 km of PA boundaries.30 Our
study indicates 314 post-operation and 1,024 active-operation mines within China’s PAs, which require urgent attention. Mining impacts PAs in

both post-operational and active-operational stages. Given the significance of biodiversity, the mining industry needs to acknowledge and
address the resulting biodiversity impacts, advancing in harmony with SDGs and GBF conservation goals where possible.11,34 Studies have
expressed concerns about urging the mining sector to adopt ecologically responsible practices,16,35 especially for active mines.36 GBF Target 7
calls for reducing pollution impacts on biodiversity,3 underscoring the need for the mining industry to prioritize ecological responsibility and
support global conservation efforts.37 Ecological restoration is also essential.38 One of GBF’s key goals is the "30 × 30″ target, which aims to
restore 30% of degraded key ecological areas by 2030.39 Our findings reveal many post-operational and active-operational mines within PAs
where ecological restoration can help meet this goal. Recently, the Chinese government has emphasized ecological rehabilitation of mines,
setting national standards for mine restoration and regulating the supervision and assessment of restoration work.40,41 The “concurrent mining
and reclamation” requirement has been implemented for mine extraction, combining source protection with whole-process treatment and
restoration.40,42 A notable case is the Altay mine in Xinjiang, where the greening rate has reached 70% through tree planting and tailing water
reuse, achieving zero wastewater discharge. Many other restoration projects nationwide demonstrate similar successes in ecological
rehabilitation.43,44 The threats from CECMs are surpassing those from FEMs Our results indicate that further CECM extraction will surpass the
threat posed by FEMs to PAs in the future in China. Previous studies have also highlighted this concerning trend; future increases in the density
and scale of renewable energy material mines are expected to exacerbate biodiversity threats in critical ecological regions.12 The above findings
remind us to pay attention to the potential negative impacts on biodiversity from climate change mitigation measures. GBF Target 8 specifically
addresses this issue, advocating for minimizing negative and fostering positive impacts of climate action on biodiversity.3 Our research results
support the necessity of this proposal while also providing evidence for the trade-offs in several SDGs (such as SDG 13 and SDG 15). Some
researchers argue that the current spatial overlay analysis cannot accurately assess the biodiversity impacts of energy transition.36 In the future,
through improvements in recycling and mining technologies, we will require fewer critical minerals, thus reducing the impact on
biodiversity.23,36 For instance, a significant portion of the copper used in solar photovoltaic and wind power generation can already be recycled
through traditional methods.45,46 Similarly, circular economy strategies for new battery technologies show significant potential in reducing
primary material demands.47,48 Research institutions, governments, and businesses should work together to build a circular society, which
could reduce the need for new mining of critical minerals.49 Research institutions can strengthen studies on enhancing the recyclability of
emerging clean energy technologies to preempt future waste management issues.50 Governments and industries should collaborate to develop
standardized protocols for efficient and sustainable recycling processes,51 establish robust recycling systems,34 and encourage the
incorporation of higher percentages of recycled content through regulatory measures and economic incentives.52 For future research, it is
recommended that a comprehensive evaluation model be established to analyze and compare the economic and ecological effects of different
recycling technologies and policies to identify the optimal strategy for balancing energy transition and biodiversity conservation.52 In addition,
some studies argue that more detailed data are required for a more accurate quantitative assessment of mining impacts associated with the
energy transition.16,53 Specifically, the biodiversity impacts of mining activities are influenced by multiple factors, such as the type of ore and
the mining technology used.36 For example, studies indicate that, depending on the type of ore and mining techniques used, lithium extraction
can have different mechanisms of impact and varying effects on ecosystems.54 Land-use area is another essential factor. In recent years, some
studies have used high-definition satellite imagery to identify the actual land-use area of mining activities.25 Based on these data, more
accurate assessments of the biodiversity impacts of mining activities can be made. By combining statistical and satellite data, the operational
status of mining can be cross-verified. Therefore, we recommend that future research focuses on integrating such mineral land-use data with
site-specific data, including mineral type, extraction intensity, mining technology, and local ecological characteristics, to improve the accuracy of
direct and indirect biodiversity impact assessment models and better examine ecosystem-specific effects. Restricting mining in PAs to mitigate
biodiversity impact GBF Target 1 requires that biodiversity loss in important areas be close to zero by 2030.39,55 According to our findings, the
number of ore fields within PAs and their associated impact ranges are significantly higher than those outside the boundaries. Timely
withdrawal and restriction of mining activities from PAs is crucial for achieving GBF conservation goal efforts. Mining restrictions may trigger a
shift of mining activities to less regulated areas or increase illegal mining.56 More decisive action is needed to monitor and manage mining
activities in PAs to avoid further threats.41,57 The GBF has a monitoring framework that guides related monitoring efforts. In addition, advanced
satellite mapping technologies are increasingly being utilized to monitor the environmental impacts of mining operations.58 High-resolution
satellite images allow continuous monitoring of large and remote areas, ensuring that illegal mining activities are promptly identified and
addressed.25,59 By capturing detailed before-and-after images, satellites can track the progress of reclamation efforts, such as re-vegetation
and soil stabilization.60,61 Satellite imagery can also monitor early signs of tailing pond leakage or breakage to mitigate environmental disasters,
protecting local biodiversity and human communities.62 Incorporating satellite technologies into the monitoring and management strategies of
mining operations can significantly enhance biodiversity conservation and ensure the long-term sustainability of mining practices. The Chinese
government prioritizes balancing PAs and mining activities. Since 2017, it has been cleaning up prospecting and mining in PAs and restricting the
entry of new mining rights.42,63 China’s "Guidelines on Establishing a Nature Reserve System with National Parks as the Main Body" underscore
the strict measures required to limit mining development within PAs.64 A prominent example is the Qilian Mountain
National Park, where mining in PAs of iron, coal, and rare metals had caused severe ecological
damage.63,64 In 2017, following a central environmental inspection, all mining sites were required to
be closed, and ecological restoration efforts were initiated. Through strict policy interventions and
rehabilitation measures, the ecosystem in Qilian Mountain has gradually recovered, with noticeable

improvements in biodiversity. Our study confirms the effectiveness of restrictive policies in reducing the
disturbance caused by mining activities within PAs. If strictly enforced, then the threat of mining in
China’s PAs could potentially reduce the threat by two-thirds. In addition, conservation efforts must
extend beyond the boundaries of PAs,57 and about one-third of the impacts are caused by mining
activities outside the border. Collaborative efforts involving conservation organizations, the mining industry, and government
agencies are needed in peripheral areas.16 This includes reassessing policy frameworks related to mining permits and environmental impact
assessments to incorporate stricter standards for areas adjacent to PAs. More concerns are needed to balance energy transition and biodiversity
conservation From a biodiversity conservation perspective, a strict ban on mining activities within PAs is undoubtedly the optimal measure, as it
can reduce the biodiversity impact of mining by more than 60% in China. However, policy formulation must consider a range of other factors,
including potential socioeconomic impacts. For regions heavily reliant on mining, the mining sector is an economic pillar, providing many local
employment opportunities.65 A blanket restriction on mining within PAs could devastate the local economy, lead to unemployment, and
potentially trigger social instability.56 Moreover, indiscriminately restricting mining in PAs could result in shortages of critical materials,
hindering the progress of the energy transition. In the context of escalating geopolitical tensions, ensuring the security of supply chains for
critical materials is particularly important.66 A ban on domestic mining in PAs could lead to greater reliance on imports, jeopardizing supply
chain security. Therefore, a policy based solely on ecological concerns is one sided. A flexible, phased, and integrated approach is recommended,
considering economic, social, and environmental impacts to avoid severe disruptions to local economies and social structures.37 It is advised
that stakeholder collaboration be enhanced, fostering multi-stakeholder engagement to develop feasible and integrated approaches.67
Specifically, a gradual exit strategy could be implemented for active mining within PAs based on local economic conditions, biodiversity
importance, and ecological restoration capacity.68 For regions heavily reliant on mining, responsible mining practices should be introduced to
gradually reduce mining activities, supported by economic transition initiatives to help these areas identify new drivers of economic growth.56
Additionally, companies could be required to allocate a portion of their profits to establish conservation funds, supporting ecological restoration
and protection efforts within these areas.69 For future mining projects, the areas eligible for development and the order of extraction could be
determined based on the importance of biodiversity in different locations, and sustainable mining practices could be adopted to minimize
biodiversity impacts.12 Furthermore, pilot programs that combine development with restoration could be implemented, and best practices
could be shared to reduce the impact of mining activities.70 The two successful cases mentioned earlier also employed various measures to
achieve their goals. For future research, it is recommended to consider scenarios and objectives in the context of energy transition, ecological
protection, and geopolitical conflict, collecting more detailed data (such as mineral production, reserves, and socioeconomic data of mining
areas). This will enable an analysis of the social, economic, and ecological impacts of different policy combinations (e.g., strict restriction,
ecological restoration, and sustainable mining practices), providing a more comprehensive reference for policy development on mining activities
and biodiversity conservation in the context of the energy transition. Such research would benefit not only China but also other countries,
promoting the coordinated development of a global energy transition and biodiversity conservation and contributing to the achievement of
multiple SDGs and conservation goals of the CBD. Experimental procedures This study was conducted in three steps (Figure S1). Firstly, a list of
ore field data with geographic coordinates for each record point location was obtained, and qualified records were screened for data analysis.
The ore field data were categorized by mineral type and activity attributes. Meanwhile, the polygonal data of the PAs were obtained to count
the occurrence of ore fields within and around these reserves. Secondly, different distance buffer zones were created around the ore field points
to define the scope of influence produced by mining activities. Finally, the nature reserve area affected by mineral activities was obtained by
spatially overlaying the ore field data with the nature reserve. Data acquisition and preprocessing (1) Mining ore field inventory: the
Chinese Geological Survey Bureau defines ore fields as areas where industrial ore bodies have been confirmed through mineral exploration
projects, with resource estimates reaching at least half the upper limit specified in the “Criteria for the Division of Mineral Resource Reserves
Scale.” Geographic coordinates of the ore fields were obtained from the "National Ore Field Database 2021"71
(http://data.ngac.org.cn/mineralresource/index.html), which includes data on ore types, names, geographical coordinates, transportation
locations, ore deposit sizes, geological work achievements, and mining statuses. It is the latest and most comprehensive publicly available
Chinese mineral deposit database. From this database, a total of 228,189 records of mining ore fields were collected. After filtering out
duplicates and records with missing locations, mineral types, or scale attributes and excluding fields explicitly stated to have no future
development potential, a total of 123,626 valid records were retained. The valid records were classified based on their mining status into three
categories: post-operation, active operation, and pre-operation (Table S4). Meanwhile, 50,378 energy ore fields were screened based on mineral
type, distinguishing between CECMs and FEMs (Table S5). For mining ore fields with associated mineral products, classification was done
according to their main mineral products. For details on data preprocessing, please refer to the supplemental information. (2) China’s
PAs: we obtained PA data from The World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA) at https://www.protectedplanet.net. The dataset used in this
study is based on 2018 WDPA data, covering a total area of 1.44 million km2. According to the WDPA definition, these PAs correspond to China’s
natural PAs. The data quality has been validated by various studies and the Chinese official natural PA data.72,73 After removing duplicated
polygons, the final retained area was approximately 1.4 million km2. Buffer setting The mineral ore field dataset provides coordinates of all-scale
ore fields. Due to the unclear boundaries of mining polygons, buffers were used to represent the direct and indirect impact areas of ore fields.
Previous studies indicate that mining polygons range from 0.26 to 30.7 km2, and mines less than 2 km2 are called small-scale or artisanal
mining.18,25 In our dataset, small-scale mining areas account for over 70%, significantly higher than regular ones. To capture the impacts of

small-scale mining, a 1 km buffer was used to approximate direct impacts. The 1 km buffer can roughly cover the destruction areas caused by
surface land occupation, exposed mine pits, and other significant impacts. Indirect impacts of mining are often larger than direct impacts,
potentially extending beyond 50–70 km from the mining boundary.12,18 For example, large-scale industrial mining in the Brazilian Amazon
region caused deforestation up to 70 km away, and the Grasberg open-pit mine in West Papua, New Guinea, resulted in forest loss more than 42
times bigger than the mining area itself.74 Tailings from the Mamut copper mine generated heavy metal pollutants, with ecotoxicological risks
detectable up to 36.5 km away.75 While previous studies mostly focused on large-scale mining with impact boundaries set at 50 km, China’s
predominance of small-scale mining led us to conservatively choose a maximum buffer distance of 10 km to capture local and medium-scale
impacts.25 Acknowledging the possibility of longer-distance impacts, we also set 50 km buffer zones as the limit for indirect impacts.
Spatial overlay analysis To determine the proximity of the mine site to PAs, we overlaid the spatial data of ore fields onto the consolidated
polygon data of PAs. Ore fields situated within PAs or 1, 5, and 10 km from PA boundaries were included in the analysis (Figure 1). This approach
quantified both the direct and indirect impacts of mining activities on PAs by capturing the overlap between ore fields’ impact range and PAs.
Additionally, we analyzed ore fields outside PA boundaries to illustrate potential threats to PAs even under stricter conservation measures. This
process was repeated for subsets of mining areas based on development status (post-operation, active operation, and pre-operation) and
mineral types (CECMs, FEMs). All spatial analyses were conducted using ArcGIS software. Conclusion The energy transition is urgent
to address climate change. However, the transition will increase demand for critical minerals,
potentially exacerbating biodiversity loss. To investigate this issue, this study analyzed the spatial overlap between critical
minerals, traditional energy minerals, and PAs based on China’s mineral database. The results indicate that mining activities already
pose a threat to China’s PAs and that the future extraction of critical minerals will present a greater
threat than traditional energy minerals. These findings offer empirical support for policy formulation
regarding mining activities within PAs. A strict restriction on mining in PAs could reduce biodiversity
impacts by over 60%. However, an integrated strategy combining dynamic monitoring, flexible policies, and multi-stakeholder
collaboration is likely more effective in achieving long-term conservation and socioeconomic balance within and outside PAs. Future research
should incorporate a scenario analysis to systematically evaluate the effects of various management approaches on social, economic, and
ecological aspects, thereby promoting the achievement of multiple SDGs and CBD conservation targets.

requiring decisions on how to move forward in the most sensible way to preserve biodiversity. The brown bear (Ursus arctos) is a large carnivore

umbrella species (Simberloff, 1999). Additionally, due to their role as a keystone species, brown bears may be particularly crucial for habitat

natural range of the species is neither being reduced nor is likely to be reduced for the foreseeable future”, and that “There is, and will probably

the southern edge of the species' range and are therefore an essential element of European biodiversity.

et al., 2021), and need systematic conservation actions. At the same time, Greece has set ambitious goals for the
development of RES within its national climate policy. Benefiting from the fact that most of the country is public land and its high wind
and solar potential, Greece has been favouring the development of windfarms and photovoltaic projects at an unprecedented scale, even in
N2Ks. The country has already reached 44 % of its 2030 national goal for wind harnessing (Kati et al., 2021). RES development, however, can
have a negative impact on bears at both local (i.e., at the construction site) and distant scales (De Gabriel Hernando et al., 2020; Ferrão da Costa

development may be exacerbated by the secondary effects of other RES-associated works (e.g., access
roads, disposal areas) or the synergistic, cumulative impacts of RES development with other human
activities. All these potential adverse effects raise concerns about the compatibility of current RES
development efforts with Greece's commitments and goals to protect its biodiversity.

transitionMultiple stressors 1. Introduction The effects of anthropogenic climate change are incontrovertible (IPCC, 2022). On the other hand,
the solutions to our crisis are subject to relentless controversy and dispute. One of the most strongly promoted pathways out of this planetary
problem is a transition to renewable energy-systems. Building these new systems will, however, require vast amounts of minerals and metals.
Modelling by the International Energy Agency predicts that by 2040 the total global mineral demand for new energy technologies is set to
double under the United Nations’ Stated Policies Scenario and quadruple under the Sustainable Development Scenario (IEA, 2021). This demand
is increasing the pressure for more metal mining, much of which will occur in ecologically sensitive environments and in the territories of
Indigenous peoples (Luckeneder et al., 2021, Owen et al., 2023). Simply put, the prevailing pathway to address climate change locks-in an
extractive-hegemony, whereby mining becomes essential for planetary survival. The need for more resources to build ‘clean’ energy-systems is
paradoxically both a consequence and a driver of climate change and the expansion of the extractive industries. There are many places around
the world, such as the Pacific Islands region, where increased demand for these resources will have a compounding effect: it will intensify the
socio-economic, political and ecological pressures of extraction, which in turn will diminish local capacity to respond to the effects of climate
change. At the same time, the effects of climate change will ensure that extractive activities and their impacts are more volatile. Together, these
processes, drivers and consequences form an ‘energy-extractives nexus’ that must be understood if we are going to avoid the production of
sacrificial zones on the path to global sustainability (Bainton et al., 2021). In this paper we introduce the concept of compound exposure to show
the contradictions found in the primary solution to climate change – where the solution, in the form of low-carbon energy-systems that are very
mineral intensive serves to compound underlying forms of vulnerability and enlarge exposure to harm. This same solution also contributes to
climate change and influences local responses to environmental hazards, thus compounding the localised effects of climate change. In
developing this concept, we extend and link the scholarship on mining and climate change (e.g. Bebbington et al., 2015) and the justice impacts
of supplying raw materials for renewables (e.g. Heffron, 2020, Sovacool et al., 2020) together with the scholarship on vulnerability to climate
change in the context of multiple stressors (e.g. Kennedy et al., 2023, McCubbin et al., 2015, O’Brien et al., 2004, Räsänen et al., 2016).
Compound exposure draws attention to the pre-existing vulnerabilities in those places where extractive projects are located or planned. These
vulnerabilities span various social, ecological and governance dimensions, and include the already existing effects of climate change and the
legacies of resource extraction. Once we understand these relationships and feedback loops, we can begin to grasp the future forms of exposure
that will accompany the expansion of the extractive industries under changing environmental conditions.
We develop this concept in the context of the Pacific which has a devastating history of resource exploitation and is acutely exposed to the
effects of global climate change. The Pacific Ocean and its islands are also rich repositories of the resources needed for the development of
low-carbon energy-systems and technologies, otherwise termed energy-transition minerals and metals or ‘ETMs’ (Lèbre et al., 2020). At present
there is insufficient knowledge of these orebodies and the costs of extracting and processing them to be able to assess their economic viability.

compound exposure, we have isolated a Pacific sub-set from a global dataset of projects that are extracting or proposing to extract ETMs. Our
Pacific sub-set also maps the pre-existing levels of vulnerability in the locations where those ETM projects occur, including exposure to climate

change. The indicators reveal higher levels of situated vulnerability surrounding ETM projects in the Pacific compared with most ETM projects in
other parts of the world. A rush for the resources in the Pacific is thus likely to create new patterns of harm in places already exposed to
multiple intersecting vulnerabilities. The remaining body of this paper is organised into five sections. The next section introduces the Pacific
setting. Section three outlines our methods, followed by the findings for the Pacific sub-set which we compare with the global dataset. We then

respond to environmental change. We conclude that in order to avoid the worst effects of compound exposure in places like the Pacific, we
need greater coordination across global and regional scales around the supply and consumption of natural resources.
2. Plunder and peril in the Pacific The Pacific stretches from the east coast of Australia across a vast expanse of ocean to the west coast of
America. Powerfully described as a ‘sea of islands’ (Hau'ofa, 1994), the Pacific is comprised of some 22 ‘small island developing states’ or ‘large
ocean island states’ as most Pacific Islanders prefer to see them.1 The plundering of the provinces for their rich natural resources has a long
pedigree that began in the nineteenth century as colonial powers progressively encompassed Pacific places and peoples. The plundering
persisted as independent Pacific nations then committed themselves to extractive-led development, while various French territories, such as
New Caledonia, continued to experience forms of neo-colonial exploitation that transferred wealth and resources back to the European
continent. In recent years, the region was thought to provide approximately 2 m tonnes of copper, nickel, gold, manganese and aluminium per
annum to the global marketplace, worth around US$2.6bn (The Guardian, 2021).
The last few decades have seen a new ‘scramble for the Pacific’ (Fache et al., 2021). As foreign powers and private corporations compete for
access to territories and resources, Pacific nations are entering into new deals and new alliances and signing offtake agreements that reshape
regional power dynamics. Former colonial powers, including the United States, have been unsettled by the presence of new players like China,
Indonesia, India and Taiwan who exercise considerable economic and diplomatic influence in the region to reinvent the geopolitical order in
diverse and unpredictable ways. Familiar patterns of social and environmental harm have been witnessed at extractive projects across the
Pacific region. For example, the extensive exploitation of phosphate from Nauru and Banaba completely decimated these island landscapes
(Teaiwa, 2015). In New Caledonia nickel mining has generated fierce conflicts over environmental impacts, Kanak land rights, and the
distribution or ‘rebalancing’ of economic and political power between local regions and the French metropole (Filer and Le Meur, 2017,
Horowitz, 2009). Similarly, in Papua New Guinea, the social and environmental impacts of the massive Panguna copper mine on the island of
Bougainville sparked a ten-year civil war that cost thousands lives, while the abandoned mine site and its toxic overflows have been the subject
of a human rights investigation (Regan, 2017, Tetra Tech Coffey, 2024). On the opposite side of the country, the giant Ok Tedi gold and copper
mine became one of world’s worst extractive legacies when the government permitted the operator to dump millions of tons of mine waste in
the local river system (Kirsch, 2014). Meanwhile, the extraction of gold and bauxite in Solomon Islands has fuelled various kinds of human and
ecological violence that extend beyond the boundaries of these contentious projects (Allen, 2013).
In each instance mining projects have given rise to accelerated forms of social disintegration, entrenched new
forms of inequality (Bainton & McDougall, 2021), induced gendered impacts (Macintyre, 2011), created livelihood deprivations and contests
over scarce resources (Beer & Schwoerer 2022), threatened cultural heritage (Bainton et al., 2012), and displaced people from their customary
lands. Landscape transformations have remade Indigenous lifeworlds and irreversibly reshaped senses of place (Rumsey & Weiner, 2004), while
the privatisation and enclosure of common property combined with resource governance failures ensure that extractive
capital accumulates elsewhere with little local benefit (Bainton & Skrzypek, 2021). We anticipate that these effects
are likely to become more severe as the demand for ETMs increases in response to calls for urgent action on climate
change (Skrzypek et al., 2022). Climate change is an existential threat to Pacific lives. Across the region, the impacts of climate change are
experienced in diverse ways in different locations, including sea-level rise, desertification, increased soil salinity, flooding, as well as permanent
loss of land through shoreline erosion (Kumar, 2020). Many Pacific communities frequently endure extreme weather events, such as cyclones
(Ballard et al., 2020, Chand et al., 2020). These events have long-lasting consequences, particularly in countries with limited infrastructure or
capacity to mitigate and respond to environmental hazards. While other parts of the world are experiencing similar threats, the twin issues of
land availability and climate displacement are especially problematic in Pacific nations (Ramsay et al., 2023). Exposure to climate risks raises
critical and unprecedented questions about sovereignty, security and continued statehood of low-lying Pacific states that have already begun
losing their land to sea-level rise (Kelly and Foth, 2022, Rayfuse and Crawford, 2012). And generally, we find there is a concentration of risk and
exposure to harm to a much higher degree than in most other parts of the world. The 2021 World Risk Index, which assesses disaster risk for
181 countries around the world, observed that the Pacific (or Oceania, as it is named in the report), has the greatest levels of disaster risk due to
‘high exposure to extreme natural events’ (Mucke, 2021). Three Pacific nations – Vanuatu, Solomon Islands and Tonga – were named as the
three most at-risk countries in the world, with Papua New Guinea ranking 9th, Fiji 14th and Kiribati 19th. These types of risk rankings merely
confirm what Pacific leaders have long known. The Pacific Islands Forum Boe Declaration on Regional Security, endorsed by Pacific leaders in
2018, declared climate change to be ‘the single greatest threat to the livelihoods, security and wellbeing of the peoples of the Pacific’ (PIFS,
2018). Four years later, at a meeting in Fiji, the Pacific Islands Forum leaders declared a climate emergency in the Pacific (PIFS, 2022a).
Exposure to the effects of climate change is exacerbated by pre-existing socio-economic vulnerabilities and inequalities that persist throughout
the region (Weir et al., 2017). For many Pacific countries, extreme environmental events stretch existing capabilities and limited economic
resources. In this context, there is a push to utilise the region’s natural resources to stimulate economic growth and boost local capacity to deal
with these events. As the global demand for ETMs increases the pressure to extract resources from the Pacific, we find mixed views throughout

the region. Some Pacific leaders want to secure the development opportunities that mining can create, while others are concerned about the
wide-reaching effects of mining on their peoples and environments. These debates include highly contentious conversations about
implementing untested seabed mining technologies in the region (Bainton and Skrzypek, 2022, Childs, 2019, Lilford and Allen, 2023). While
some Pacific states, including Fiji, Vanuatu, Samoa, Palau and the Federal States of Micronesia have called for a moratorium on seabed mining
activities, others, such as Cook Islands, Nauru, Tonga and Kiribati, have decided to sponsor seabed exploration projects within their territory or
within international waters. The double bind facing the Pacific is perverse and pernicious. Pacific countries desperately need world leaders to act
to stop the world from overheating. But this same situation, and the vulnerabilities that persist throughout the Pacific, also provides the pretext
to extract more resources from the region. Major mining companies have seized this opportunity, strategically rebranding themselves as
producers of ‘green metals’ and the providers of ‘transformative’ or ‘nation-building’ opportunities. Assessed from this vantage point, mining
will provide the materials for a global energy transition to address the climate crisis and provide the economic base to sustain Pacific nations
and build their capacity to adapt to a changing climate. The Pacific faces a perilous future if climate action is not achieved quickly enough – but
large parts of the Pacific may also become ‘sacrifice zones’ (Scott & Smith, 2017) in the pursuit of raw
materials for the primary solution to this problem.

global analysis of the situated environmental, social and governance risks for ETM mining projects.

Following the approach developed by our colleagues, we then used the Pacific sub-set to map the situated forms of vulnerability in the places
where these ETM projects are located. This provided the basis for a comparison between the Pacific sub-set and the global dataset. For our
purposes, we inverted the point of focus from ‘supply risks’ to the risks to people and places. In other words, we have focussed on pre-existing
vulnerability (i.e. the contextual factors that may amplify exposure to the social and environmental harm arising from increased pressure to
extract in these places under volatile environmental conditions). We made other minor adaptations to our colleagues’ methodology, where
certain measures were replaced or complemented to cover gaps in data for the Pacific region, including a layer for extreme weather events,
datasets for Indigenous landownership7 and as we discuss below, indicators for climate change.
We used a set of 23 spatial indicators aggregated into seven dimensions of vulnerability: extreme events, water, conservation, communities,
land uses, social vulnerability and governance. The three environmental dimensions (extreme events, water and conservation) cover the
proximity of mining projects to key biodiversity conservation areas, risks related to the availability and quality of freshwater resources in the
region, and the possibility of extreme weather or seismic events that may trigger mine waste containment breaches. The three social
dimensions (communities, land uses and social vulnerability) include communities in proximity to the project, the existence of competing land

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanisms—no magnitude, probability, timeframe, or scope comparisons between impacts, and no "even if" framing that pre-emptively subsumes opponent offense. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like **"Weighing — Climate tipping points outweigh on magnitude & irreversibility: Amazon dieback releases 75–120 Gt CO₂, locking in 0.3°C+ warming within decades — exceeds any economic dislocation claim"** (citing e.g., Lenton et al. *Nature* 2023 or Armstrong McKay *Science* 2022) and would be placed after the Amazon tipping-point evidence in Contention 1 or as a standalone "Weighing" module before the contentions.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards preemptively answer likely Con positions (e.g., "lawful access breaks E2E encryption → security vulnerabilities → harms dissidents/journalists"; "China/Russia exploit backdoors"; "FBI 'going dark' claims exaggerated"; "alternative: metadata/traditional investigation"). An offensive spike would look like **"Spike — Lawful-access mandates don't weaken encryption: US/UK/AU 'client-side scanning' proposals preserve E2E security while enabling warrant access — Carnegie Endowment 2024"** or **"Spike — Encryption backdoors disproportionately harm authoritarian targets: China/Russia already compel decryption; US mandate adds no new capability — Freedom House 2023"**, placed in the 1AC after uniqueness or as a dedicated "Spikes" contention.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines answering the responses the case will actually face (e.g., "deforestation link is Chinese policy, not US encryption mandate"; "Amazon tipping point uncertain"; "mining-driven deforestation ≠ lawful access"; "China won't change behavior from US law"). A defensive spike would be **"Frontline — US lawful-access rule creates leverage to condition tech transfer/trade on Chinese supply-chain enforcement: CSIS 2024"** or **"Frontline — Amazon deforestation is driven by Brazilian/Peruvian governance gaps, not encryption — US mandate enables forensic tracking of illegal timber shipments — EIA 2023"**, placed after the China-deforestation link cards.

### Evidence quality
**Mixed — Strong on climate/deforestation UQ, weak on link-to-resolution:**
- **Strong UQ cards:** **Butt 2026** (Telegraph, WMO/Copernicus data, same-day recency) — high-quality institutional sources documenting accelerated warming; **WMO 2025** (UN authoritative voice, 10-2025, CO₂ growth rates) — top-tier dataset recency/authority; **Finer 2025** (MAAP satellite monitoring, 6-2025) — primary dataset, specialist author; **Zhang et al. 2025** (*Nature*, 12-2025, 236k mining sites, 87% accuracy) — elite journal, massive dataset, recent.
- **Weak link cards:** **Sun et al. 2024** (Nexus/Cell Press, Chinese PA mining restrictions → 67% threat reduction) — **does not mention encryption, lawful access, or US policy**; author quals are PhD in optical fiber, not policy. **Donnelly 2025** / **Gustin 2025** (journalistic, Inside Climate News) — credible but secondary reporting. **Borges 2025** (CPG industry outlet) — non-peer-reviewed, advocacy-adjacent. **Stalley 2021** (Earth Island Journal, 2021) — outdated for "China uniquely key" claim post-2022 policy shifts. No card connects US encryption mandate → reduced Chinese Amazon deforestation.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case is pinned to a single, attenuated link chain: US encryption mandate → (unstated mechanism) → Chinese supply-chain transparency → reduced Amazon mining/deforestation → climate mitigation. No alternative impact scenarios (e.g., **CSAM/child exploitation investigations enabled**, **terrorism/domestic extremism disruption**, **ransomware attribution/deterrence**, **allied intelligence sharing**, **rule-of-law normalization for digital warrants**). A flexible case would include at least two independent contentions with distinct links (e.g., Contention 1: Child Safety — **Thorn 2024** "E2E encryption blocks 95% of CSAM detection"; Contention 2: National Security — **Wray 2024** "FBI lost access to 7,800 devices in 2023").

### Warrant depth
**Mixed — Strong warrants on climate/deforestation UQ, missing warrants on the critical link:**
- **Butt 2026**: Warrants "climate events decades ahead of schedule" with WMO/Copernicus data, Burgess quote, ocean heat records — **warranted**.
- **WMO 2025**: Warrants CO₂ acceleration with ppm trends, sink reduction, vicious-cycle mechanism — **warranted**.
- **Zhang et al. 2025**: Warrants mining-driven deforestation 2-3× prior estimates with 236k-site inventory, 87% accuracy, 19,765 km² / 0.75 Pg CO₂ — **warranted**.
- **Sun et al. 2024**: Claims "strict mining restrictions in PAs reduce 67% of threats" but **no warrant connecting this to US lawful-access mandate**; the study analyzes Chinese domestic PA policy, not international supply-chain leverage via encryption law.
- **Donnelly 2025 / Gustin 2025**: Warrant Chinese port/rail infrastructure → Amazon deforestation risk with specific projects (Chancay, trans-Amazonian corridors) — **warranted on driver, not on solvency**.
- **No card warrants**: "Requiring lawful access to encrypted communications → technology companies provide supply-chain visibility → Chinese miners/exporters face enforcement → Amazon deforestation drops."

### Internal consistency
**INCONSISTENT — Framing mismatch between resolution and case:**
- **Resolution**: "US federal government should require technology companies to provide lawful access to encrypted communications."
- **Case framing**: Entire 1AC is climate/deforestation/Amazon/China-mining UQ with **zero mention of encryption, lawful access, technology companies, warrants, or digital forensics** until the final card (Sun et al. 2024), which discusses **Chinese protected-area mining restrictions** — a domestic Chinese conservation policy.
- **Undercut**: Contention 1 uniqueness (Amazon collapse, Chinese mining dominance) implies the problem is **Brazilian/Peruvian governance + Chinese demand**, solvable by **trade policy, supply-chain due-diligence laws (EUDR), or bilateral agreements** — not by compelling Apple/Signal/WhatsApp to decrypt messages. The case does not argue that encrypted comms are used to coordinate illegal logging/mining, nor that lawful access would expose those networks. **No cohesion between the resolutional mechanism and the terminal impact.**

### Overall construction score
**Score: 2/10** — The case assembles high-quality climate/deforestation uniqueness evidence but fails to connect it to the resolutional mechanism (lawful access to encrypted communications), omits all weighing/spikes/collapse-flexibility, and lacks a single warranted link card, making it functionally non-topical in its current construction.
