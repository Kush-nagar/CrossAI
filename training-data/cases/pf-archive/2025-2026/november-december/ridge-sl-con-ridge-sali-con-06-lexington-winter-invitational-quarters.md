UQ: Chinese investments in international extraction underpin mineral flows to China
The University of Virginia 25, The University of Virginia, 11-18-2025, [Prestigious American Public Research University], "Chinese Investment in
Mineral Resources — National Security Data and Policy Institute", No Publication,
https://nationalsecurity.virginia.edu/research/chinese-investment-mineral-resources//mattli
How does Chinese foreign direct investment in critical minerals abroad impact the flow of trade? We find that Chinese investments typically
involve obtaining majority ownership in the mining companies abroad (up to 99% ownership). In
extreme cases like the Democratic Republic of the Congo, exports of cobalt to China have increased by
almost 300,000 tons post-investment. We further observe that the countries receiving investment also
account for the vast majority of China’s mineral supply. This indicates that these relationships are
becoming more and more dependent, which introduces potential for PRC vulnerability. We conclude with
numerous policy recommendations to bolster US production of minerals critical to microelectronics manufacturing and to counter Chinese expansion. Implications
and Main Takeaways • China has directly invested upwards of $24 billion in lithium, cobalt, and nickel into
equity stakes in mining and processing companies outside mainland China. Notable target countries
include Chile, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Indonesia, and Myanmar. These investments have
led to a disproportionate increase in the quantity of minerals traded back to China. • Following
investment, China heavily relies on the countries it has invested in for its mineral imports. The
percentage of mineral imports going to China from the investee countries is usually around 80-95% of
China’s total imports of that mineral. China, in turn, makes up the large majority of the target country’s
exports. • China is vulnerable to disruption to some mineral trading relationships due to its reliance on
the countries that it has invested in. DRC and Indonesia supply almost 95% of China’s imports of crucial
mineral inputs. Opportunities exist for the United States to establish a stronger foothold in the trade of critical minerals. We recommend establishing a
greater domestic presence in lithiumbattery manufacturing and recycling to support the need for raw materials. We also recommend potential partnerships that can
undermine Chinese investment. Introduction As a part of its efforts to gain supremacy in the microelectronics and
next-generation power markets, the People’s Republic of China (PRC) has used its Belt and Road
Initiative (BRI) to invest in critical mineral operations abroad. This paper investigates how these investments impact the
subsequent trade of these critical minerals to China. Our previous work has examined the vulnerabilities present in the critical minerals supply chain based on
trading patterns. In our Microelectronic Supply Chain Analysis series of white papers, Vulnerability (2025) and Lock-In and Substitutability (2025) have identified
overly important actors in the critical mineral trade network, such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) with cobalt or Turkey with boron products.
Overreliance on these countries and their unique geological assets makes it difficult for China to find
appropriate substitutes when challenges arise. However, this earlier work was limited to the insights trade data can provide and left
questions about how these dependencies are formed and maintained. We have identified infrastructure investment as a key indication of relationships that will
become highly dependent and would be resistant to substitutability. Investments support critical mineral trades by providing the
capital needed to build necessary infrastructure that might not be attainable otherwise. But
subsequently, the investment could create downstream vulnerabilities as it concentrates a country’s
focus on the return of that investment. For example, in Indonesia, China spent over $600 million on a nickel-processing facility. This has allowed
Indonesian mining to produce more nickel than it might have previously, and was only made possible by
Chinese funding. As a result, China has an extra interest in recouping that investment by selling to
Chinese manufacturers. This also supports Chinese manufacturing priorities, like their domination of
lithium battery production. This investment is not usually a one-time payment. In most cases, China

retains large portions of equity in the mining or processing of the backed mineral. Both the
infrastructure investment and the retained equity create a relationship that pushes China to prioritize
trade with their investee countries. These countries find that these investment and trade connections
are very difficult to renegotiate in the future and place them in a disadvantaged position vis-a-vis China. We find that investment does
lead to great increases in trade between the investee country and China. But importantly, we show the
highly-reliant nature of these relationships, as the huge majority of Chinese imports of these minerals
comes from the investee country, rather than from a variety of suppliers.
UQ: We need to keep mining minerals to meet increasing demand
Li 25, Serena Li, 10-8-2025, [Serena is from Maryland and earned her M.A. from Stanford University in International Policy Studies, with a focus on sustainable
global food and agricultural systems, and her B.S. in Learning and Organization Change from Northwestern University with a minor in History], "The Critical Minerals
Conundrum: What You Should Know", World Resources Institute, https://www.wri.org/insights/critical-minerals-explained//mattli
The world is racing to increase supplies of lithium, cobalt, copper and other "critical minerals" that are
building blocks of modern technology. Not only are these minerals used in industries like defense,
healthcare and electronics, but they are also key to producing the clean energy technologies that are
increasingly powering our lives. Yet critical minerals present an environmental and social conundrum. While necessary in the fight against climate
change, mining and processing of these minerals can drive greenhouse gas emissions, pollute the environment and bring risks to nearby communities. Mining and
production are also concentrated in a small handful of countries, and surging demand is adding to geopolitical tensions — from trade disputes to global debates over
the prospect of deep-sea mining. There is no question the world will need a lot more of these minerals, and quickly. But how do we meet this demand in a way that's
responsible and equitable? What Are Critical Minerals and Why Do They Matter? Critical minerals are those that are essential to
countries' economies or development. They are generally in high demand and prone to supply chain
disruptions, although countries differ on which minerals they define as "critical" depending on their level of access, what the minerals are used for, their
export value and national supply risks. Most countries' critical mineral lists include copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite
and rare earth elements. These are used widely in clean energy technologies, such as wind turbines,
solar panels, EV batteries and motors, as well as in energy transmission infrastructure like power lines.
Additional minerals important for the energy transition include aluminum, manganese, silicon and silver, among others. Many critical minerals are integral to other
industries as well. NATO includes several on its list of essential raw materials for defense. And they are
needed to manufacture the semiconductors that underpin our electronics, from smartphones and
computers to lighting and medical devices. What's the Difference Between Rare Earth Elements and Critical Minerals While the terms "rare
earth elements" and "critical minerals" are sometimes used interchangeably, the two are not the same. Rare earth elements are a subset of critical minerals that
includes 17 elements (such as neodymium, promethium and cerium) used extensively in clean energy systems and other advanced technologies. Contrary to their
name, rare earth elements tend not to be rare. The challenge is that they are often found in very low concentrations and are intermingled with other minerals,
making it difficult and uneconomical to extract them. Can the World Meet Growing Mineral Demand? Rapid growth in zero-carbon
technology has supercharged the world's need for critical minerals, and demand is expected to keep climbing. The
International Energy Agency (IEA) estimates that if governments are to meet their announced energy and climate pledges, critical mineral demand could
more than double from 2022 levels by 2030 and quadruple by 2050. Despite this high demand, there
are likely enough critical minerals to meet the world's need. But the reality of accessing them is
complex. Some critical minerals, like aluminum and palladium, have stable reserves on land that can fulfil the world's demand through 2050 and likely beyond.
For others, like copper, nickel and cobalt, current reserves are likely to come up short, and it will take a combination of
more exploration, technological advances and economic incentives to increase supply. New mining requires long
lead times. Part of the challenge with scaling up critical mineral supplies is that mining operations move slowly. From discovery to first production, it takes an average
of 15.5 years to develop new mining projects, depending on the mineral, location and mine type. Long lead times raise questions about the world's ability to ramp
up output as demand grows, especially if companies wait for deficits to emerge before committing to new projects. Mining and processing are concentrated in a

handful of countries. Extraction and processing of critical minerals are highly concentrated, both in terms of
geography and ownership. For example, in 2023, Indonesia held 42% of global nickel reserves and 54%
of global nickel production, while the Democratic Republic of Congo held 55% of global cobalt reserves
and 74% of global cobalt production. Once mined, the majority of these minerals are shipped to China
for processing before they can be applied in end uses. China processes more than half of the world's
lithium, two-thirds of its cobalt, one-third of its nickel and nearly all rare earth elements. China's
dominance in processing stems from early strategic investments in global mining and its history of looser
environmental standards — although lax regulations are tightening as harms from irresponsible mining come to light. The country's longstanding
investment in clean energy development has also propelled it as a leader in mineral-intensive EV
battery and solar panel manufacturing, driven by a combination of relatively low, capital and land costs.
L: Reducing resource extraction cripples China’s supply of resources
The University of Virginia 25, The University of Virginia, 11-18-2025, [Prestigious American Public Research University], "Chinese Investment in
Mineral Resources — National Security Data and Policy Institute", No Publication,
https://nationalsecurity.virginia.edu/research/chinese-investment-mineral-resources//mattli
To gain supremacy in the microelectronics and next-gen power markets, China has invested upwards of
$24 billion in lithium, cobalt, and nickel operations across the world. These investments in critical
mineral mining and processing outside mainland China have increased both the flow of key materials
to China and the dependency between China and its investee countries. In extreme cases like the Democratic Republic of
the Congo (DRC), cobalt exports have increased by nearly 300,000 tons post-investment. How should analysts and policymakers respond to these developments?
Where is there opportunity for the US to establish stronger footholds in the critical minerals trade, and how should it pursue this goal? Which of China’s trade
dependencies are resilient, and which are vulnerable to targeted disruption? NSDPI researchers explore these questions in this paper building on the
microelectronics supply chains analysis in our 00018 research and 00016 research. Going beyond trade data, this new study examines how China developed,
maintains, and grows its critical mineral trade relationships. Because these trade deals involve substantial infrastructure investment, they have become highly-reliant
relationships where the vast majority of Chinese lithium, cobalt, and nickel imports depend on a few invested
countries rather than diverse suppliers. This presents strategic opportunities for the US to counter Chinese dominance where they have
previously invested. In addition to identifying such opportunities, this paper offers policy recommendations to bolster US production of critical minerals. Key
Takeaways 80–95% of Chinese imports of key minerals come from countries like Chile, the DRC, Indonesia,
and Myanmar, where China has made significant investments. In turn, China makes up the majority of
these investee-nations’ exports of minerals like lithium, cobalt, and nickel. Because these investee-nations and China
rely so heavily on each other for trade, all would be left extremely vulnerable if the trade flows of key
materials were to be disrupted or cease. The DRC supplies nearly 95% of China’s cobalt, for example.

L: Reducing the supply of minerals skyrockets prices
Yuan 24 [Pengfei Yuan, (Institute of Blue and Green Development, Shandong University), 3-1-2024,
"Assessing The Supply Risks Of Critical Metals In China'S Low-Carbon Energy Transition", Global
Environmental Change, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959378024000293?] //
luke
Carbon neutrality requires the strategic deployment of emerging clean energy technologies at an unprecedented speed
and scale (Friedlingstein et al., 2020, Rogelj et al., 2018, Zhang et al., 2022, Zhang and Chen, 2022, International Energy Agency, 2021). These
clean energy technologies, such as solar photovoltaics, wind turbines, and electric vehicles (Graedel et
al., 2015, Graedel et al., 2015, Li et al., 2020), generally require more tremendous amounts of critical
metals (CMs) than their fossil fuel counterparts (Hao et al., 2019). This reaction catalyzes the new supply chain
dynamics for these CMs, for which substitutes are not readily available. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), the annual demand
for more than 30 types of CMs (International Energy Agency, 2021, International Energy Agency, 2021), including rare earth elements, platinum
group metals, and other metals such as copper, lithium, cobalt, and nickel, is projected to increase by 6–9-fold (International Energy Agency,
2021). However, most CMs are geologically rare, scarce, and scattered (Graedel et al., 2015, Graedel et al., 2015), and their production capacities
are difficult to quickly expand (Wang et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022, Elshkaki et al., 2016). The rapid scaling of these metals far outpaces their

committed mine production (Zeng et al., 2022, Olivetti et al., 2017, Xu et al., 2022). Therefore, potential risks surrounding the stability of the
CMs supply are emerging as a major threat for ensuring a green and low-carbon future (Zhang et al., 2021, Yokoi et al., 2022). China stands
out as the world’s largest importer of CMs to support its ambitious clean and low-carbon energy
deployment strategy (Rosenau-Tornow et al., 2009, Kim et al., 2015, Qiao et al., 2021). More than 15 types of CMs depend on foreign
supply from a limited number of countries (Gulley et al., 2018). For example, in the case of cobalt and nickel, which are key components of
electronic vehicles, less than 20 % of their mining production comes from domestic supply (United States Geological Survey, 2023). In this
context, China’s outsourcing of CMs is prone to constraints on suppliers’ ability (e.g., local mining
conditions (Yager et al., 2012)) and willingness (e.g., trade restrictions (Gulley et al., 2018,
Santillán-Saldivar et al., 2021)) due to worldwide geopolitical issues (Li et al., 2020, Ren et al., 2021).
These factors may trigger unexpected shocks such as price spikes (He et al., 2022) and consequently
result in supply disruptions, especially for CMs with high market prices, which are irreplaceable in
pivotal clean energy technologies (Blengini et al., 2017, Gemechu et al., 2016, Wang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023). As China
continues to invest more in renewable energy infrastructure (Wang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023, Yuan
et al., 2023), this may lead to greater challenges for CMs supply within the context of highly uncertain
global geopolitics (Gómez et al., 2023). Therefore, it is imperative to gain a better understanding of China’s current supply risk to
facilitate early risk warning. Scientific knowledge about the multifaceted supply risks of CMs is fundamental for navigating risk management
strategies and ensuring an efficient low-carbon energy transition (Graedel et al., 2015, Graedel et al., 2015, Zhang et al., 2021, Helbig et al.,
2016, Nassar et al., 2020, Liang et al., 2022). Previous research on the assessment of national CMs supply risk has generally focused
on resource endowment and import reliance perspectives (European Commission, 2023, European Commission, 2023, Yu et al., 2021) and has
not identified other critical factors (e.g., geopolitics, price surges, and changes in technology). For
example, unstable geopolitics can lead to strained CMs trade relations or disrupt mining activities,
adversely buckling and breaking the clean energy supply chain (Gemechu et al., 2016, Su et al., 2021,
Vakulchuk et al., 2020). Similarly, metal price increases can be transmitted to downstream sectors, such as clean energy equipments or
infrastructures, making them expensive to afford and consequently impeding the progress of the energy transition (Yan et al., 2021, Zhang et al.,
2022, Zhang and Chen, 2022, Sun et al., 2022). Furthermore, supply risk can change as new mining activities are operating, international
relations change, and energy technologies undergo transformation (Graedel et al., 2015, Graedel et al., 2015, Kalt et al., 2021). A static
evaluation falls short in capturing the dynamic characteristics of risks driven by those factors for achieving continuous risk monitoring and
real-time adaptation (Gulley et al., 2018, Zhou et al., 2019, Jasiński et al., 2018, Wei et al., 2022). Therefore, a more comprehensive
risk-assessment framework that can identify the critical nodes of supply risk and track their historic evolution is needed to prevent China’s CMs
supply disruptions from different angles and to provide early risk warnings.

China drives Africa’s battery metals buildout China has established itself as a dominant force in Africa's
mining sector, with a strategic focus on securing essential resources for its manufacturing and energy
transition goals. As global demand for critical minerals escalates, China's involvement in Africa's mining industry is reshaping the regional
dynamics of resource extraction, economic development, and geopolitical influence. Part of an intensifying geopolitical contest for critical
minerals supply Africa has become the epicenter of an intensifying global competition for critical minerals, as
countries scramble to secure supply chains for the materials required to power the energy transition.
The continent's strategic importance has surged as it accounts for approximately 30% of the world's
mineral resources, many of which are classified as critical for battery manufacturing and clean energy
infrastructure. With global demand for lithium, graphite, cobalt, copper and manganese projected to increase exponentially over the next

Demand for battery materials expected to outstrip supply by 2035 The global transition to renewable energy and electric vehicles is creating an
unprecedented surge in demand for critical minerals, as extracting and processing capacity struggles to keep pace with the accelerating demand
for energy transition technologies. Minerals including lithium, cobalt, nickel, and rare earth elements have become the backbone of the clean
energy economy, serving as essential components in lithium-ion batteries, photovoltaic solar panels, hydrogen electrolysers, electric motors and

investment and supply chain diversification, particularly within the context of Africa’s mineral resources. China dominates extraction,

over critical mineral extraction and processing operations, enabling Beijing to dominate downstream
manufacturing of renewable energy technologies and granting China competitive advantages over
Western economies. The dominance extends across the entire value chain from mining to
manufacturing, with Chinese state-owned companies controlling the vast majority of global minerals
extraction and refining capacity and end-use manufacturing. This vertical integration allows China to
maintain outsized influence over the production of EVs, photovoltaic solar panels, wind turbines and

PlanFootnote1 issued in 2021. If China is a leading country in mining production, its dominant position appears even more evident in the downstream sector of the
mineral industry due to its large capacity in refining, as shown on Fig. 1. Despite significant reserves and mining production in a
wide variety of metals, China still needs to import large amounts of ore and concentrate from other
mining countries to supply the demand of its refining and manufacturing industries (Ministry of
Natural Resources of the People’s Republic of China 2021). This dependence on ore and concentrates
imports is the economic rationale behind its domination of the central and downstream segments of
the value chain. Beginning in the early 2000 s, Chinese enterprises have systematically
internationalized their operations, with foreign direct investments (FDI) driving global project development. In October

geopolitical objectives, and that of securing the supply of raw materials needed by its domestic

Providing support to the expansion of Chinese firms overseas has thus been a priority in the government foreign policy ever

invest massively in many sectors abroad including the metal sectors. The investments in the metal
sector take the form of Chinese private or state-owned enterprises (SOEs) financing new mining
projects or expansions of existing mining capacities, acquiring major holdings or buying out other
companies mainly operating on the upstream segments of metals supply chain (exploration, mining,
processing capabilities). Chinese FDI policy has attracted much attention over the past few years and has been subject to expanding screening
regulations. In the scientific literature, several regional analyses are available, providing in-depth policy analysis on Chinese firms’ approaches to
investing in strategic projects, including metals-related projects, in: the European Union (Kratz et al.
2024; Farooki 2018), Africa (Anderson 2023; Hagan 2023; Ericsson et al. 2020; Gulley et al. 2019; Edinger and Pistorius 2011), South America

though Chinese FDI in the metal sector are frequently mentioned, especially in the body of literature focusing on critical minerals (US terminology, see US Energy Act
2020) and critical raw materials (EU terminology, see EU CRM Act 2024), there still lacks a global and comprehensive assessment on the destination, and evolution of
these investments. This paper aims at fulfilling this gap by addressing the following questions: What are the key trends and patterns of Chinese foreign direct
investments in the metal sector regarding their evolution and geographic distribution? How are these investments integrated into China’s global political strategy?
What are the effects of these investments on the critical mineral supply chains in the context of the energy transition? Our paper addresses these questions by
proposing a global quantitative assessment of the Chinese investments in the metal sector from 2005 to 2024, using the dataset from American Enterprise Institute

security strategy and the reaction of several host countries. Overall, this study cross-analyses the imbrication between resources, technology and economic issues
and in the light of global power competition. Institutional context of China’s global political strategy China’s industry transformation: from manufacturing hub to
high-tech industries In the early post-Mao era, Deng Xiaoping's reforms incentivized the production of large quantities of affordable consumer goods. Four special
economic zones (SEZs) were established on the southeast coast in 1980 and 1981: three in Guangdong and one in Fujian. These SEZs were designed to attract foreign
investment and labour force from the countryside, enabling large-scale production of inexpensive"Made in China"goods dedicated to exports on global markets (Luo
and Zhu 2018). The 2000 s marked a significant economic boom for China: the country joined the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001 and became the
second-largest economy by 2010. But many of the easy-growth-wins had already been achieved during the transition from traditional to labor-intensive industries,
making further growth more challenging by pursuing the same development pattern (Naughton 2021). The appointment of Xi Jinping as Chairman of
the Communist Party in 2012 and as President of the PRC in 2013 coincides with a strategic shift from an
economy based on common goods manufacturing and hard infrastructure operations (steel industries,
bulk minerals) toward high-technology industries (Li 2018; Kennedy 2015). On March 5, 2015, at the 12th Plenary Session of the
People's Assembly, the former Prime Minister Li Keqiang announced a 10-year plan that redefined China’s industrial priorities. The plan “Made in China 2025”
released by China’s State Council (国务院) aimed to ensure China’s competitiveness and leadership on innovation and
technology at the horizon 2049 for the centenary of the PRC founding. This plan was elaborated by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT)
and the Chinese Academy of Engineering, revealing a preliminary reflexion aligning scientific expertise, industrial challenges and global strategic outlook on a long
term perspective. It promotes the rapid development of ten well-positioned strategic sectors such as generation IT,
high-end equipment, new materials, and biotech and pharma. Such transition from being a global
manufacturing hub to a world-class industrial power necessitates new mineral resources especially
metallic elements. Securitization of materials supply, thus, becomes crucial to the modernisation of these sectors
(Andersson 2020; Yan et al. 2021). Chinese strategists are well aware of that: in the Part III, Section VI, one key area focused on “New materials” (新材料) promoting
the development of an integrated military-civilian industry, while paying close attention to supply security concerns. The document also set the target to increase
domestic market share of Chinese suppliers for “core basic parts and key basic materials” to 70% by 2025. These documents reflect that supply security in specific
materials are considered national security concerns by the government. The material-energy nexus: raw materials requirements at global scale The expansion of
new technologies, especially energy transition and digital technologies, requires an increased supply of
several metallic elements. From a fundamental perspective, any energy system has material
prerequisites, and vice versa, implying that energy and material systems are deeply interconnected
(Ashby 2013; Hu et al. 2024). This material-energy nexus was first highlighted by early scholars (Mumford 1934; Simondon 1958) and has recently been studied in
the energy transition context. Extensive research has shown that the expansion of low-carbon energy and digital technologies
requires an increasing supply of specific metals such as copper, nickel, lithium, gallium, lanthanides, and others (Vidal et al. 2013;
Graedel et al. 2015; Fizaine and Court 2015; Seck et al. 2020; IEA 2021; Hache et al. 2022; IEA 2024). This is primarily because metallic
elements are essential component of electronic technologies involved in low carbon energy system,
from production (e.g., solar panel, wind energy), distribution (e.g., power grid), storage (e.g., batteries,
fuel cells) and end-uses (e.g., electronic and digital devices). Although some metals have specific applications—such as silver (Ag)
for solar PV, lanthanides (Nd, Dy, Pr) for permanent magnets used in wind turbines, lithium (Li) for batteries, aluminium (Al) for infrastructure, or copper (Cu) for
power grid—they are, to some extent, all contributing to sustain modern life by being “part of the system”, as
a result of the nexus between energy and materials. The scale at which mineral security must be addressed, from production to
consumption and associated pollution is thus inherently global (Chakrabarty 2019) and entangled with
interdependence and geopolitical dynamics. Ensuring a secured and growing supply of these materials
appears as a core challenge for China’s industrial strategy to be successful. China’s FDI and mineral supply security The
modernisation of China's industries is coupled with a supply security strategy that relies on FDI in the metal sector and infrastructure control abroad. By
supporting mergers, acquisitions, and investments in foreign countries, the government encourages

Chinese firms to establish a global value chain system that secures materials supplies for the domestic
high-tech industries. The overseas expansion of Chinese companies through FDI, particularly within the
framework of the Belt and Road Initiative (一带一路) are based on the principle of"mutual benefits” as stated in several official strategic
documents. For instance, the National Mineral Resources Planning (2016–2020) states: “Deepen the international mining cooperation of the"Belt and Road",
strengthen overseas mineral resource exploration and development, improve the quality and level of foreign investment,
introduce advanced technology and management experience, actively participate in global mining governance, and promote the formation of a deeply integrated
and mutually beneficial cooperation pattern.”.Footnote3 Applied to FDI in the metal sector, we assume that this principle implies Chinese firms bringing
funds and technical expertise to develop critical infrastructure abroad including (but not limited to)
mineral extraction projects. Because of their central role in the economic process, and more widely to the functioning of societies, energy
infrastructures (power plant, pipelines, refinery, electricity grid, etc.) and transport infrastructures (ports, railway, car road, etc.) are also key security targets. The
mineral industry heavily depends upon this infrastructure due to the global nature of its supply chains and the associated significant energy demand. By extending
their control over these critical infrastructures, Chinese firms build a relationship of dependence with foreign countries,
constituting a diplomatic pledge potentially favoring further access to the mineral resources needed by
China. The links between such dependency framework and a privileged access to specific mineral deposits through favored treatment in mining concession
attribution, exploitation license or cooperation agreement are particularly hard to prove, especially in countries with weak institutional structure (Cooke et al. 2015).
In this context, FDI should be considered as a proxy of a (potentially larger) dependency framework. Several organisations act as intermediates between the
government and firms, such as the China Chamber of Commerce of Metals, Minerals & Chemicals Importers & Exporters (CCCMC), and are in charge of monitoring
trade situations, collecting and studying economic information from the industry, and advising the relevant government departments to design adapted policies. By
carrying out industry inspection and examination these organisations behave as national agencies, ensuring Chinese companies adhere to the laws and regulations
of the government but also providing consulting support to companies in decision-making to overcome trade barriers, accompanying them in responding to foreign
trade investigations, and making regular reporting to the government to shape further supportive regulations (CCCMC 2025). Foreign direct investments (FDI) are
economic instruments that can create interdependence between the investing actor and the receiver of the investment. From a liberal perspective, FDI should
mitigate the possibility of conflict between the involved parties (Copeland 2015; Pevehouse 2004). However, when FDI provides the investing actor with control over
critical infrastructure in the target country, the interdependence can become asymmetrical. This control allows the investor to make the infrastructure unavailable
for arbitrary reasons, and implies a significant economic and political linkage to a foreign country (Godzimirsky and Andersen 2024). In this paper, we engage with
what Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye described as “complex interdependence” characterised by resources distribution, infrastructure ownership and vulnerabilities
among a wide variety actors, including many non-state actors, operating at the international level (Keohane and Nye 1977; Godzimirsky and Andersen 2024). Our
study analyses FDI as one instrument of China’s global political strategy, addressing mineral supply security challenges. Methodology Our methodology integrates
both quantitative and qualitative approaches to describe the key trends and patterns in China’s FDI in the metal sector and their implications for global mineral
supply chains. In this paper, the term “mineral” refers to mineral-based commodities that are metal-bearing raw materials, such as ores and concentrates. The
primary dataset used in this study is sourced from the American Enterprise Institute (AEI) within the project Chinese Global Investment tracker. Our analysis
specifically examines FDI reported under the categories “investment” and “construction”, which corresponds to the combined data from datasets No.1 and No. 2.
The dataset we used tracks 4466 transactions, across 166 countries and 14 different sectors, from 2005 to July 2024, capturing the dynamics of China’s FDI over
nearly two decades. The AEI database focuses on significant investments, excluding transactions smaller than 95 million USD, to capture the most impactful FDI
activities. Descriptive statistics were then used to summarize the most salient trends and patterns in China's FDI in the metal sector. The term “metal sector” is used
according to the definition of the AEI, and refers to metal-related industrial activities, including mineral exploration, mining, processing (upstream segment of the
value chain) and refining, smelting and manufacturing (downstream segment of the value chain). It is important to note that Chinese FDI are mostly oriented
towards upstream activities in the metal sector. The analysis focused on identifying the geographic distribution, sectoral allocation, and temporal evolution of these
investments. The qualitative data were further enriched by additional data from the European Union Joint Research Center (EU JRC), the US Geological Survey
(USGS) and the Resources Trade Earth dataset, which provided insights into global mineral reserves and production trends. To complement the quantitative findings,
a qualitative review of policy papers, reports from international organizations, and industry analyses was conducted, with the case of Australia and Peru more
specifically analysed. Overall, quantitative data highlighted the scale and distribution of China's investments in the metal sector globally, while the qualitative
analysis offered insights into the geopolitical implications of these investments. Analysis Global trends and patterns of the Chinese FDI in the metal sector Trends in

Such information can thus still be emotively powerful, blurring the line between malign and benign use. The next economic crisis is closer than you think. But what you should really worry about is what comes after: in the current social,
political, and technological landscape, a prolonged economic crisis, combined with rising income
inequality, could well escalate into a major global military conflict.
The 2008-09 global financial crisis almost bankrupted governments and caused systemic collapse.
Policymakers managed to pull the global economy back from the brink, using massive monetary stimulus,
including quantitative easing and near-zero (or even negative) interest rates.
But monetary stimulus is like an adrenaline shot to jump-start an arrested heart; it can revive the patient, but it does nothing to cure the disease. Treating a sick economy requires structural reforms, which can cover everything from
financial and labor markets to tax systems, fertility patterns, and education policies.
Policymakers have utterly failed to pursue such reforms, despite promising to do so. Instead, they have remained preoccupied with politics. From Italy to Germany, forming and sustaining governments now seems to take more time
than actual governing. And Greece, for example, has relied on money from international creditors to keep its head (barely) above water, rather than genuinely reforming its pension system or improving its business environment.
The lack of structural reform has meant that the unprecedented excess liquidity that central banks injected into their economies was not allocated to its most efficient uses. Instead, it raised global asset prices to levels even higher
than those prevailing before 2008.
In the United States, housing prices are now 8% higher than they were at the peak of the property bubble in 2006, according to the property website Zillow. The price-to-earnings (CAPE) ratio, which measures whether stock-market
prices are within a reasonable range, is now higher than it was both in 2008 and at the start of the Great Depression in 1929.
As monetary tightening reveals the vulnerabilities in the real economy, the collapse of asset-price bubbles will trigger another economic crisis – one that could be even more severe than the last, because we have built up a tolerance
to our strongest macroeconomic medications. A decade of regular adrenaline shots, in the form of ultra-low interest rates and unconventional monetary policies, has severely depleted their power to stabilize and stimulate the
economy.
If history is any guide, the consequences of this mistake could extend far beyond the economy. According to Harvard’s
Benjamin Friedman, prolonged periods of economic distress have been characterized also by public antipathy
toward minority groups or foreign countries – attitudes that can help to fuel unrest, terrorism, or even
war.
For example, during the Great Depression, US President Herbert Hoover signed the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Tariff
Act, intended to protect American workers and farmers from foreign competition. In the subsequent five years, global trade
shrank by two-thirds. Within a decade, World War II had begun.

To be sure, WWII, like World War I, was caused by a multitude of factors; there is no standard path to war. But there is reason to believe that
high levels of inequality can play a significant role in stoking conflict.
According to research by the economist Thomas Piketty, a spike in income inequality is often followed by a great
crisis. Income inequality then declines for a while, before rising again, until a new peak – and a new disaster. Though causality has yet to be
proven, given the limited number of data points, this correlation should not be taken lightly, especially with wealth and income inequality at
historically high levels.
This is all the more worrying in view of the numerous other factors stoking social unrest and diplomatic
tension, including technological disruption, a record-breaking migration crisis, anxiety over
globalization, political polarization, and rising nationalism. All are symptoms of failed policies that could
turn out to be trigger points for a future crisis.
Voters have good reason to be frustrated, but the emotionally appealing populists to whom they are increasingly giving their support are
offering ill-advised solutions that will only make matters worse. For example, despite the world’s unprecedented
interconnectedness, multilateralism is increasingly being eschewed, as countries – most notably, Donald Trump’s US –
pursue unilateral, isolationist policies. Meanwhile, proxy wars are raging in Syria and Yemen.
Against this background, we must take seriously the possibility that the next economic crisis could lead
to a large-scale military confrontation. By the logic of the political scientist Samuel Huntington , considering such a scenario could
help us avoid it, because it would force us to take action. In this case, the key will be for policymakers to pursue the structural reforms that they
have long promised, while replacing finger-pointing and antagonism with a sensible and respectful global dialogue. The alternative may
well be global conflagration.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanism — no probability, magnitude, or timeframe comparisons between impacts; no "even if" framing; no meta-weighing instructions for the back half. Each contention ends with an impact (supply disruption, price spikes, delayed transition) but never compares them against a likely Pro impact (e.g., national security, law enforcement access). A filling card would be a 2024–25 Brookings/CSIS piece tagged **"Weighing: Mineral supply disruption outweighs law-enforcement access — probability 90% vs <10%, magnitude global recession vs localized investigations, timeframe immediate vs speculative"** placed as a standalone "Weighing" subpoint after Contention 2.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** Zero cards preempt specific Pro arguments (e.g., "encryption backdoors stop terrorism," "China already has access via hacking," "key escrow solves"). Every card is purely defensive UQ/link/impact for the Con narrative. A filling card would be a 2024 Lawfare/CISA analysis tagged **"Spike: Pro's 'lawful access' fails — end-to-end encryption architecture makes selective access mathematically impossible; backdoors create systemic vulnerability exploited by PRC/APT groups"** placed as a new Subpoint C under Contention 1.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines against the responses Con will actually face (recycling, substitution, domestic mining, allied supply chains, demand reduction). The case asserts irreplaceability (Yuan 24) but never cites evidence that *anticipates and answers* "recycling closes the gap" or "Na-ion batteries replace Li/Co." A filling card would be a 2024 IEA/USGS study tagged **"Frontline: Recycling/substitution cannot close 2030–35 deficits — recycling meets <15% of Li/Co demand by 2035; Na-ion energy density limits EV substitution to <5% of fleet"** placed as a "Defensive Spike" module after Yuan 24 in Contention 1.

### Evidence quality
The **University of Virginia 25** (National Security Data and Policy Institute, 11-18-2025) and **Sadden 25** (S&P Global Commodity Insights, 9-30-2025) are high-quality: institutional authors, primary datasets (trade flows, mine-level forecasts), <3 months old at tournament. **Yuan 24** (Global Environmental Change, peer-reviewed, 3-1-2024) is a strong academic source with 100+ citations in-text. **Li 25** (WRI, 10-8-2025) and **Jones 25** (Ember, 8-26-2025) are reputable NGO/analyst sources but rely on secondary synthesis. **Irfan 12-18-2025** (Vox) is a journalist summary — weakest card; would lose evidence comparison to any peer-reviewed or government source. Overall: 3/6 cards win tiebreaks; 2 are solid; 1 is vulnerable.

### Collapse flexibility
Two *distinct* impact scenarios exist: (1) **Contention 1** — Green transition fails → price spikes → global decarbonization stalls (Yuan 24, Jones 25, Sadden 25); (2) **Contention 2 (implied by UQ cards)** — US loses critical-mineral leverage vs China → geopolitical vulnerability (UVA 25, Li 25). However, both collapse through the *same* internal link: "reducing extraction cripples China's supply." No independent link (e.g., "backdoors discourage US tech investment," "allies reject US cloud services") exists. The case is pinned to one mineral-supply chain; a Pro "recycling solves" answer collapses both contentions simultaneously.
