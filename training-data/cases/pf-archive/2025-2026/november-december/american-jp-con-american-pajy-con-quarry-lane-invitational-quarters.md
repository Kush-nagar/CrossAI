BRUSSELS, July 17 (Reuters) - The European Commission wants to open up part of its
proposed 2 trillion euro EU budget for 2028-2034 to nuclear energy, a move likely to
divide the bloc's member states, which Germany immediately rejected. In an annex to its
mammoth budget proposal published on Wednesday, the Commission listed nuclear power as an activity
countries can fund through their national share of the budget - specifically, "new or additional fission energy
capacity installed in GW". Around 865 billion euros of EU funding will be available under these national
spending plans. The move would be a sea change for the EU, whose current budget does not
fund conventional nuclear power plants - reflecting a long-running conflict
between pro-nuclear EU members like France and Sweden and traditionally anti-nuclear countries like
Germany and Austria. "Germany rejects any subsidization of nuclear power from the EU budget," its
environment minister Carsten Schneider said on Thursday, adding that Berlin respected the choice of other
countries to build reactors. "However, respect for national sovereignty in energy matters also means not
claiming EU funds for this expensive path, a quarter of which comes from German taxpayers' money,"
Schneider said. France's energy ministry did not immediately respond to a request for comment. Swedish
energy minister Ebba Busch declined to comment. The Commission's budget proposal marks the
start of years of intense negotiations among EU nations, which must
all approve the final budget. EU countries have long been at loggerheads over whether to
promote atomic power to reduce CO2 emissions, a dispute which has delayed
policymaking on climate change and energy in the bloc. That dynamic had
appeared on the cusp of a shift earlier this year, when German Chancellor Friedrich Merz signalled Berlin
would no longer object to treating nuclear power on a par with renewable energy in EU policies. Countries
including Denmark and Italy had also signalled a shift in their past opposition to nuclear power. However,
some EU diplomats said that this softening of positions had not extended into support for EU funding.
"There is no chance EU money goes to new nuclear," one EU country diplomat
said. The EU's current budget explicitly bans member states from

building nuclear power plants using their share of hundreds of billions of euros in
regional development funds - although the budget offers some limited funds for nuclear research and
decommissioning of old reactors.

Small modular nuclear reactors (SMRs) are getting more political support in Europe and are likely to benefit from
a planned increase in spending on nuclear R&D through the Euratom programme. But industry insiders and
other observers say this will not be enough for the rapid deployment of SMRs. “The industry needs money for the
SMR vendors to advance their own research and accelerate the deployment of their SMRs,” Emmanuel Brutin,
director general of industry group NuclearEurope, said to Science|Business. “The supply chain needs money and
clear perspective [targets] to increase capacity.” The increase in funding planned for nuclear fission R&D in the
next round of the Euratom programme is a positive development but is not suitable for addressing these gaps.
So, Brutin hopes that SMR research can be included in other EU funding mechanisms to speed up deployment.
“Project owners and vendors could use Euratom funds to do some research, but they are not suitable [for all their
needs] given the usually long duration of these projects and the fact that, to produce something useful for a
specific vendor, they would have to make public parts of their intellectual property,” Brutin said. The
unwillingness of the industry to open up in other ways is a further drag on progress, according to Didier
Jacquemain, senior nuclear safety specialist at the OECD Nuclear Energy Agency. “SMR designs are advancing
at different readiness levels, but restricted access to vendor data hampers collaborative research and the
development of shared infrastructure,” he said. “In parallel, research on novel designs focuses on
proof-of-concept rather than on fully substantiated safety cases. As a result, critical safety knowledge gaps
persist, with implications for regulatory approval which may impact the deployment of some concepts.” Open
access data Sharing results is an important consideration in the development of Europe’s capabilities in SMRs,
according to Fulvio Mascari, a researcher in the nuclear department at ENEA, Italy’s National Agency for New
Technologies, Energy and Sustainable Economic Development. “To further accelerate deployment, research
should focus on large-scale experimental programmes to develop open-access data,” he told Science|Business.
This means that, in addition to valorising its existing infrastructures, the EU should invest in large-scale
experimental facilities. “This would enable the demonstration of innovative [SMR] features, the development of
reliable computational tools and methodologies to simulate plant
behaviour across all plant states, the assessment of effective accident management strategies, and the training
of a new generation of experts.” This last element, he said, is “key to building the critical mass required to
deploy safe nuclear technology on schedule.” Newcleo, a French company developing lead-cooled SMRs, also
sees an infrastructure gap. According to a spokesperson, Europe lacks a pan-European
collaborative R&D infrastructure to test materials and fuels for all
advanced modular reactors (AMRs), of which SMRs are a subset, and more specifically a high flux and
high-power fast neutron irradiation facility. As things stand, over 80 SMR technology designs have reached
various stages of development in 18 countries, but there are very few commercial SMRs in operation. Two
notable cases are Russia’s Akademik Lomonosov, a floating nuclear power plant equipped with two KLT-40S
SMRs, and HTR-PM, a modular high-temperature gas-cooled reactor owned by the China Huaneng Group. In
Europe, Norway, Sweden and Poland have the most projects underway, butthe majority of reactors
are only at the pre-investment phase. Euratom currently co-funds 16 SMR projects

addressing both safety and performance issues, including accident scenario analysis, passive safety, licensing
harmonisation, novel fuels and hybrid energy systems. Research gaps For Mascari, safety is “the cornerstone of

security and independence. Newcleo agrees, but sees this as a matter for industry rather than public research.
“As the industry already operates with a ‘safety first’ approach, researchers should focus on designing with

above pre-industrial levels. On a high emissions pathway, temperatures would rise 1.5°C by around 2026, 2°C by
around 2039, 3°C by around 2060 and 4°C by around 2078. The 2015 Paris climate agreement saw world
leaders commit to limit temperature rise to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels and to aim to limit the
increase to no more than 1.5°C by the end of the century. Governments also produced a set of pledges detailing
how they intend to reduce emissions. If countries meet all these pledges, global warming is likely to reach 2.4°C
by the end of the century, but possibly as high as 3.3°C. If all mid-century net-zero targets that have been
announced are met, it would take the world to about 2°C with a range up to 2.8°C. Policies that are

3.5°C. In the past, it has taken thousands of years for temperature to rise by a few degrees, and dramatic
changes are already occurring as a result of a 1.2°C increase. The climate system may have many unpleasant

Climate Change (IPCC) scenarios. The IPCC’s Representative Concentration Pathways (RCPs) and Special
Report on Emissions Scenarios (SRES) model different scenarios for how temperatures might change over the

Chapter 1 - The Threat from China A quarter of a century ago, there was a widespread belief that China could be ‘Westernised’ and steered towards liberalism. President Clinton, speaking in 2000 about China’s accession to the World Trade Organisation, outlined that while membership in the WTO would not make China liberal overnight, “it will move China faster and further in the
right direction.”20 Today, that optimism has vanished. A rare bipartisan consensus has emerged in Washington: China is the United States’ most formidable competitor and the primary threat to its national security and interests. The 2017 National Security Strategy, issued under President Trump’s first administration, stated unequivocally that “China and Russia want to shape a world antithetical to U.S. values and interests. China
seeks to displace the United States in the Indo-Pacific region, expand the reaches of its state-driven economic model, and reorder the region in its favor.” It also underscored American fears that Beijing was building the most capable and well-funded military in the world after the U.S., with that gap closing by the day.21 Trump’s successor, Joe Biden, largely continued the firm stance taken on China. Rather than reversing Trump-era
policies, his administration expanded them by introducing a variety of economic measures, such as the Inflation Reduction Act. Likewise, a diplomatic strategy was undertaken to solidify U.S.-led alliances in the Indo-Pacific to push back against China’s increasingly aggressive posture. In 2022, President Biden’s National Security Strategy described that China “is the only competitor with both the intent to reshape the international
order and, increasingly, the economic, diplomatic, military, and technological power to advance that objective.”22 It was this growing power that the administration saw as harming America’s interests and tilting the global order for the Chinese Communist Party’s (CCP) benefit. The current administration has appointed known China hawks to key positions: Marco Rubio as Secretary of State and Mike Waltz, formerly National Security
Advisor and now nominee for Ambassador to the United Nations. Meanwhile, influential think tanks close to the Trump administration, such as the Heritage Foundation, have stated that “a totalitarian Communist dictatorship is engaged in a strategic, cultural, and economic Cold War against America’s interests, values and people.”23 This view has become embedded in the U.S. foreign policy establishment. Both parties agree that
the goal is no longer to manage the competition with China but to win it. China, for its part, has undergone a similar transformation in perspective. It previously saw areas of cooperation with Washington, mainly in the realms of counter-terrorism and climate change, but now believes that the two countries are in a protracted competition with each other. Its approach to the competition with the U.S. is holistic, incorporating not just
decision-makers in the Politburo but a range of different stakeholders and actors that are being mobilised in this competition, from the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) to businesses and academic institutions.24 Its objective is clear: displace the U.S. at the top of the international system through a combination of economic, technological, military, and diplomatic means. The Science and Tech Competition The science and
technological dimensions of America’s competition with China run far deeper than its competition with the Soviet Union during the Cold War. President Biden’s National Security Advisor, Jake Sullivan, argued that the U.S. would pursue a policy of maintaining “as large of a lead as possible” over its adversaries in technology, attempting to keep China behind in strategic technologies with defence applications.25 Meanwhile, President
Xi outlined in 2024 that while China had made breakthroughs in strategic technology fields, even becoming the world leader in some, there are weak spots. Therefore, he stated that China “must further enhance its sense of urgency and intensify its efforts … so as to secure a leading position in sci-tech competition and future development.”26 Xi’s remarks are the latest expression of a long-standing ambition of the Chinese
Communist Party. Announced in 2015, the ‘Made in China 2025’ plan highlighted his goal of becoming the global leader in critical technology manufacturing and development, including semiconductors, electric vehicles and biotechnology. By 2049, it aims to dominate a broader set of strategic sectors, including AI, quantum, and robotics.27 As one former official at China’s Ministry of Science and Technology stated, “[t]he core of the
U.S.-China rivalry is the technology war … Whether the U.S. can defeat China or whether China can rise from adversity will depend on the technology war.”28 In pursuit of this goal, China has rapidly increased its investment in the research and development of new technologies. By 2022, China was reportedly spending over 2.5% of its GDP on research and development (R&D), representing just shy of $650 billion, while the U.S.
spent just over $700 billion.29 Due to the opacity of Chinese financial reporting, there is a possibility that its spending is higher than this. Notably, China’s R&D expenditure is mainly state-led, whereas the U.S.’s is mostly driven by the private sector. In quantum technologies, for instance, the Chinese government announced spending of $15.3 billion in 2022, compared to the U.S. government’s $3.7 billion and the UK’s $1.3 billion.30
This centralised, state-led model enables the CCP to more directly align technological investment with its broader strategic competition with the United States and do so faster. Beijing’s investments are paying dividends. According to the Australian Strategic Policy Institute’s (ASPI) Critical Technology Tracker, which assesses global research performance, strategic intent, and potential future science and technology capability across 64
critical technologies, including in defence, computing, cyber, and AI, China has made remarkable progress. Between 2003 and 2007, the U.S. led the world’s innovative research in 60 of the 64 tracked technologies. By contrast, China only led in three. Fast forward to 2019-2023 and the landscape had shifted dramatically. China led in 57 of the 64 technologies, while the U.S. led in just seven.31 This trend underscores the need for the
U.S. and allies to expand its innovation efforts – presenting opportunities for deeper collaboration with key allies like the UK. Concerns about the development of Chinese innovation and technology are not localised in the U.S. Sir Alex Younger, the former Chief of MI6, recently stated that China’s mastery of key technologies is a “pacing threat” to the UK and one that is just as important as the threat currently facing the UK from
Russia. He warned that the UK needed to stay at the forefront of the development of key emerging technologies, without which the UK’s “security prospects will be pretty dim.”32

Concern about Chinese Capabilities
Washington has grown increasingly alarmed by the expansion of China’s military capabilities – as have its Five Eyes
allies.35 In its latest annual threat assessment, the U.S. intelligence community described China as “the most
comprehensive and robust military threat to U.S. national security.”36 As part of its push to build a world-class military,
China has complemented its advances in defence technology with rapid increases in defence
spending and the size of its armed forces. Although Beijing is not necessarily seeking an open war with
Washington, its military build-up signals a clear intent to close the gap to the U.S. The U.S. will struggle to match China

numerically and the disparities are expected to grow. In just four years, the People’s Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) has
expanded by the equivalent of the entire Royal Navy and now constitutes the largest navy in the world.37 Similarly,
China has a shipbuilding capacity 230 timesthat of the United States.38
China’s primary intention for its rapid military build-up is to deter, and if necessary defeat, U.S. intervention in the
Indo-Pacific. Previously, China was reluctant to display belligerence in the Indo-Pacific due to the capability gap with the
U.S. This may help to explain why the CCP has not yet launched an invasion of Taiwan, opting instead for economic
coercion and an increase in military exercises in the Taiwan Strait. However, as its capabilities have gradually caught up
with Washington, Beijing has felt greater freedom to use assertiveness and aggression
in pursuit of its aims.39
Belligerence over Taiwan risks a conflict with catastrophic global consequences – both
in terms of human casualties and economic disruption. Such a conflict could very easily spill into other nations and
regions. The most effective means of deterring Chinese aggression is by making it infeasible or too costly to pursue. That
entails not only sufficient stockpiles of munitions and equipment – which war games conducted by the Center for
Strategic and International Studies suggest would be exhausted within weeks – but also superiority in new military
technologies.40
One of America’s enduring military advantages is its ability to project power globally. As of 2021, the U.S. had overseas
military bases in at least 80 countries – a figure that has doubled since the Cold War. China, conversely, only has one
overseas military base, located in Djibouti – only a short distance from Camp Lemonnier, a U.S. base. However, American
intelligence outlines that the Chinese military intends to expand its global footprint in the decades ahead, describing that
it “will continue to pursue the establishment of overseas military installations and access agreements to project power
and protect China’s interests abroad.”42 It also suggests that China is looking to exploitits global infrastructure
projects through the Belt and Road Initiative for military purposes.
Officers in the U.S. Navy have warned that since the Second World War, no power has come close to threatening U.S.
command of the world’s oceans, “but China is poised to do so within the next several decades.”43 An expansion
of Chinese overseas military bases could pose a threat to America’s military position. A
greater number of PLAN overseas military installations – which in many ways depends on its diplomatic and
development strategy – whether in Asia, Africa or Latin America and the Caribbean, can be used to collect vast
amounts of intelligence, disrupt U.S.shipmentsin times of crisis and resupply its navy with weapons,
ammunition, and equipment to challenge America’s control of the sea.44
Whilst China does not pose an immediate military threat to the UK like Russia does, it is widely recognised by defence and
security officials that Beijing is a more long-term security threat to the UK, despite the reluctance of the Government to
label it as such. The first reason for this is the use of sub-threshold activities, including cyber attacks, espionage,
information warfare, and economic attacks. Following a series of cyber attacks on UK companies, government
departments, critical infrastructure, and politicians, the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre named China as the “pacing
threat in the cyber realm.”45 Second is the development of global power projection capabilities as outlined above. The
NATO strategic concept in 2022 outlined that China poses a challenge not simply to the security of the Indo-Pacific but
also the Euro-Atlantic due to the use of its political, economic, and military tools.46 Already, Beijing has been accused of
conducting sub-threshold activities against European NATO members. The damage to undersea infrastructure in the Baltic
Sea by Chinese vessels has demonstrated that the UK and European NATO members should not only increase capabilities
and spending to counter Russia, but also China.

The U.S. currently has an advantage in its network of global alliances. For decades, U.S. alliances across Europe, Africa,
and Asia have given it a decisive edge over adv˜ersaries. Its allies have supported the U.S. militarily, provided vital
intelligence, and allowed unrivalled global power projection. China may have grown in power, but it has not generated
comprehensive partnerships that have significantly boosted its geopolitical position.
However, the growing alignment between China, Russia, Iran, and North Korea presents
a new and evolving challenge to U.S.security and interests. Though not a formal alliance, the relationships
between these states have strengthened their capabilities and improved resilience.47 The
deepening cooperation between Beijing and Moscow is the most concerning. Beyond the announcement of a ‘no limits’
partnership between the two countries, just before Russia’s illegal invasion of Ukraine, the two nations have been
explicitly aligning themselves with one another to foster a global order in their image and support their shared
objectives. China has provided considerable economic assistance to Russia since the start of the full-scale invasion of
Ukraine, including exporting components used in weapons, and has improved Russian capabilities in autonomous,

missile, and cyber systems.48 It has propped up the Russian economy by increasing imports of oil and gas and has
supported Russia in conducting financial transactions in the face of sanctions.49
The deepening of China-Russia cooperation extends beyond the economic sphere. Over the past decade, the two
countries have increased the number of joint military exercises, including in the Mediterranean.50 The
Arctic, as will be discussed later, has the potential to be another area of increased China-Russia cooperation that could
have severe consequencesfor both the U.S. and the UK. Immediately after the inauguration of
President Trump, a conversation was held between Xi and Putin, firming up their support for one another, safeguarding
the interests of the two countries, and suggesting that the two nations have far greater military ties over the coming
years and decades, demonstrating the growing indivisibility of the Indo-Pacific and Euro-Atlantic.51
The growing cooperation between Russia and China, and to a lesser extent Iran and North Korea, has several
consequences for the defence and security of the U.S. and UK. First,there is the higher potential of cyber
attacks and espionage activity from both nations, with chances of coordinated sub-threshold
activities. Already, it is widely believed that Moscow and Beijing are aligning cyber activities against the West with
shared tactics and joint operations. Indeed, The Times reported in April 2022 that China-based hackers launched a cyber
attack on Ukraine’s military facilities just before the full-scale invasion.52 Second, China’s investments in R&D for
military technologies, as well as its rapid build-up of capabilities, can help to support and bolster Russia’s
military capabilitiesthrough information and technology exchanges and exports of defence products. Third, their
campaigns to undermine the West via developing countries has the potential to harm Western global power projection
and create a world more permissive of Russian and Chinese aggression.

Threat to Undersea Cables Whilst a direct attack on U.S. or UK territory is unlikely, Russian undersea activities pose a serious threat to NATO countries through covert means – notably sabotage of critical undersea infrastructure. Undersea cables carry 95% of international data
and facilitate around $10 trillion in financial transactions daily across the world, with some of the most important cables – and the oldest – being between the U.S. and Western Europe. They are indispensable for everyday communication, financial operations, and the secure transmission of classified information.61 The UK depends on subsea cables and pipelines for 99% of its digital communications and three quarters of its total
gas supply.62 Moscow’s attention to transatlantic undersea cables has increased and it has vessels that can map and sever undersea cables to subvert transatlantic military and government communications, eliminate internet access, and cause enormous economic disruption for NATO countries.63 It is not beyond the realms of possibility that Russia sabotages transatlantic cables. There has consistently been sabotage in the Baltic
Sea since the start of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine in what many believe is a “systematic campaign of anonymous, covert attacks on those countries helping Ukraine.”64 Dmitry Medvedev, Russia’s former President and close ally of President Putin, stated in 2023 that there were no constraints “to prevent us from destroying the ocean floor cable communications of our enemies.”65 Whilst Medvedev’s rhetoric is intentionally
inflammatory, Russia’s repeated targeting of critical European infrastructure suggests that the threat of sabotage to transatlantic undersea cables must be taken seriously. Adversaries in the Arctic Another matter of concern is Russia’s role in the Arctic. There is another rare bipartisan consensus in Washington that America needs to be paying closer attention to the region, both due to the opportunities and the strategic challenges it
presents. As sea ice recedes, the region is becoming more accessible. This can reduce shipping times between Europe and Asia by as much as 50%. Likewise, there are enormous energy reserves in the Arctic, with the region holding an estimated 13% of the world’s undiscovered conventional oil resources and 30% of its undiscovered conventional natural gas resources, as well as substantial critical mineral reserves.66 Once seen as a
zone of cooperation insulated from geopolitics, the Arctic has emerged as a frontier of great power competition. The U.S. Coast Guard during the first Trump administration argued “[t]he Arctic’s role in geostrategic competition is growing,” warning that U.S. adversaries are using the Arctic to conduct their activities “under a cloud of ambiguity that makes it difficult to mount an effective or timely response.”67 In 2024, the Biden
administration released an Arctic strategy, which outlined that the region “is critical to the defense of our homeland, the protection of U.S. national sovereignty, and our defense treaty commitments.”68 Interest in the Arctic spiked further following President Trump’s suggestion that the U.S. acquire Greenland for national security reasons. Access to, and dominance of, the Arctic is a central consideration in Russia’s global strategy.
Russian leaders have spoken extensively about the need for Russia to increase its military presence in the Arctic. In 2022, Sergei Shoigu, then the Defence Minister, declared that Russia needed to “create an appropriate grouping of troops in Northwest Russia.”69 Although Moscow has struggled to have a large army presence in the Arctic due to the focus on Ukraine, its Northern Fleet – making up two-thirds of Russia’s nuclear strike
capabilities – has been largely unaffected.70 Even before the war in Ukraine, the Kremlin outlined its intention to upgrade its Northern Fleet with new anti-ship cruise missiles and an expanded fleet of nuclear-powered submarines as part of a goal to phase NATO out of the Arctic.71 According to the Council on Geostrategy, the total missile firepower of Russia’s Northern Fleet comes to over 800 missiles, with 720 of these from
submarines.72 Tensions between autocracies and the West in the Arctic are likely to intensify. The region acts as a first line of defence for the U.S., particularly through its partnerships with Canada and Northern European nations. NORAD, for example, which is a joint U.S.-Canada military command, is responsible for early warning systems and the protection of North America and has recently tracked Russian and Chinese aircraft
around Alaska.73 The Arctic is an area of deepening China-Russia cooperation. Due to the strains of the war in Ukraine, Russia has sought a closer partnership with China in the Arctic to offset NATO countries’ perceived advantages.74 In 2018, China described itself as a ‘near-Arctic’ power and is now starting to match Russian interest in the region. While Beijing cites scientific research and economic development as its primary
motivations, there is an unmistakable strategic dimension to its ambitions. The Arctic represents the shortest missile flight trajectory between China and the U.S., making it crucial for any potential conflict.75 There are also fears in Washington that submarines passing through the Arctic and the Greenland Iceland-UK Gap undermine NATO’s ability to prevent Russian and Chinese infiltration of NATO waters. Such movements could
undermine NATO navies, transatlantic shipping, and critical undersea cables, whilst also allowing U.S. adversaries better intelligence. In response to rising collaboration between Russia and China and due to the gaps in its Arctic capabilities, the U.S. has been looking to strengthen collaboration with NATO allies in the region. One glaring disparity is in icebreakers. Russia far exceeds the U.S. in terms of icebreakers and ice-capable
patrol ships, causing serious concern in Washington. These vessels are essential for asserting an Arctic presence, enabling scientific research, facilitating trade, and supporting military operations throughout the year. To address this shortfall, the U.S., along with Canada and Finland, signed ICE Pact to produce polar icebreakers and allow each member to access new equipment and capabilities in the region, as well as creating a more
competitive shipbuilding industry. U.S. officials have stated that this arrangement is a “message to Russia and China that the United States and its allies intend to … doggedly pursue collaboration on industrial policy to increase our competitive edge in strategic industries.”77 Despite recent tensions between Washington and Ottawa, this arrangement is likely to continue unscathed as the U.S. is in desperate need of these icebreakers
and an increase in its Arctic capabilities. There is a strong willingness from the U.S. to strengthen collaboration with NATO allies in Arctic security to counter the activities of Russia and China in the region, including with the UK. Over the next decades, the threat from Russia will not disappear, even as the threat to the U.S. from China intensifies. It is committed to military modernisation, will look to destabilise the West through covert
activities, and will attempt to take steps to challenge and erode NATO’s maritime role. Furthermore, like China, it is turning to developing countries to undermine Western influence. Chapter 3 - An Anti-Western Order China’s Anti-American Movement The final piece of China’s strategy to displace the U.S. at the top of the global hierarchy is by diminishing Western influence in developing countries and encouraging greater alignment
with China. Developing countries are the foundation for the strategic objectives of the Chinese Community Party and over the past 15 years Chinese development assistance spending has increased by 525%.78 Through the $1 trillion Belt and Road Initiative, the Global Development Initiative, the Global Security Initiative, and the Global Civilisation Initiative, the CCP is utilising influence to foster a global order in its favour. Western
efforts to counter China’s growing influence have faltered. With the dismantling of soft power mechanisms such as the United States Agency for Global Media and a lack of funding for the BBC World Service, autocratic powers are filling the global information vacuum. Likewise, the dismantling of USAID, alongside cuts to UK and European development assistance budgets, means the West is set to fall behind China as an economic
partner for many countries. All the while, Beijing has pledged to increase its commitments. By positioning itself as the preferred – and increasingly, only – development partner and combining this outreach with expanding military and diplomatic influence, Beijing is steadily reshaping global alignments, drawing more countries away from the West. In this complex, multi-dimensional global competition, influence plays a crucial role.
The rise of Chinese influence in developing countries has several consequences: it creates a permissive environment for an invasion of Taiwan as fewer nations would condemn or take action against this aggression, improves Beijing’s global intelligence gathering, facilitates greater economic ties between China and developing countries that could allow the CCP to disrupt supply chains, shapes both China’s and America’s role in the
future international order, and forms new norms, rules, and values that underpin that order.79 China’s messaging towards developing countries repeatedly asserts that the U.S. is not a reliable nor capable ally, while presenting itself as a more equitable alternative. Under President Xi, Beijing has claimed that “[t]he hegemonic, domineering, and bullying practices of using strength to intimidate the weak, taking from others by force
and subterfuge, and playing zero-sum games are exerting grave harm … The United States has been overriding truth with its power and trampling justice to serve self-interest.” China, meanwhile, “opposes all forms of hegemonism and power politics, and rejects interference in other countries’ internal affairs.”80 At the start of April, the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs Spokesperson’s X account posted a video claiming that a
U.S.-led global order is one of violence, hegemonism, greed, exploitation, subversion, surveillance, backstabbing, suppression, provocation, and tariffs. However, a China-led world order would see no war, security for all, equality among nations, justice, shared prosperity, opportunity for all, civilisations coexisting in harmony, and global solidarity.81 In reality, Beijing seeks to prevent the growth of democracy, freedom, and human
rights globally. The undermining of U.S. development efforts and soft power has strengthened Beijing’s hand, helping it to build relations with nations that have typically been more aligned with Washington. Vietnam, for example, has had a turbulent relationship with China, particularly due to disputes in the South China Sea. Despite its communist system of government, Hanoi has been more accurately seen as a U.S. Indo-Pacific
ally. Nevertheless, President Xi recently visited Hanoi, looking to create stronger economic ties between China and Vietnam, sign agreements on supply chains, and urge Vietnam to join China in opposing “unilateral bullying.”82 W hile the United States maintains more extensive military ties with developing nations, Beijing is rapidly expanding its influence through security cooperation as part of its Global Security Initiative (GSI). In
2023, China’s Ministry of Public Security trained over 2,700 foreign law enforcement officers, as well as launching several training programmes for forces from nations like the Dominican Republic and South Africa, helping to export the CCP’s principles and repressive security systems into the African continent.83 There are three key drivers for the expansion of international security arrangements. First, it helps Beijing to conduct
surveillance of the Chinese diaspora abroad and stifle criticism of the Party. Second, it helps to create better conditions for Chinese investments abroad. Third, it promotes Chinese soft power by portraying Beijing as a responsible security actor globally. As the International Institute for Strategic Studies has remarked, through the GSI, China has been “successful in persuading countries to join efforts that challenge global-security
norms and the United States’ role in global-security governance.” It has served “to promote China’s alternative global order and oppose U.S. hegemony.”84 The CCP’s international security arrangements, much like its economic engagement, often come with detrimental impacts to human rights, freedoms, and sovereignty. For instance, in 2016, cooperation between Chinese and Kenyan security services saw the arrest of 44
Taiwanese nationals and deportation to China, whereupon they received jail sentences of up to 15 years, even though some of those arrested had previously been acquitted in a Kenyan court. Similarly, in Egypt, China’s intelligence services interrogated Chinese students in an Egyptian prison soon after the signing of a cooperation agreement between Egypt and the Ministry of Public Security.85 These cases illustrate how China
leverages security partnerships to export its repressive norms and assert its interests abroad, often undermining the sovereignty of others. Just because most of China’s economic, diplomatic, and soft power activities are intended to diminish America’s position in the world and boost its own, does not mean that Britain has been spared. The UK has also been in the firing line. Beijing’s messaging to developing countries – especially
Commonwealth nations – contains anti-British themes which seek to undermine the UK’s position and influence in the world. Beijing continues to frame the UK as a colonial power, and itself as a nation that has suffered from colonialism. Soon after coming to power, President Xi sought to legitimise China’s economic activities in developing countries through this use of rhetoric and discourse. In attempting to legitimise the Belt and
Road Initiative in Jamaica – a Commonwealth nation – the Chinese ambassador in Kingston wrote: “As a nation that has suffered from foreign aggression, exploitation and underdevelopment for a long period in history, China fully recognises and understands the needs and dignity of developing nations.”86 Likewise, when Barbados became a republic, Chinese state-owned media suggested that the island nation had "adopted a
brand-new path” and encouraged Barbados to take a separate path from Britain.87 Messaging like this is not exclusive to the Caribbean but features widely to promote the idea of China as a responsible development partner and encourage greater sympathy for its vision for a global order. Despite its rhetoric, China’s actions in developing countries are far from altruistic. Beijing’s engagements are designed to further its ambitions of
reshaping the global order in its image, while advancing its broader strategic goals, such as establishing a military capable of global power projection, and securing economic and technological dominance – particularly by controlling critical mineral supply chains. Together, these objectives challenge America’s position and security, whilst posing a direct threat to the economic and security interests of Britain and its position in the
world. Russia’s Global Reach Moscow accuses Washington of being “the main inspirer, organizer and executor of the aggressive anti-Russian policy of the collective West” – a charge that has also been levelled at the UK. As such, Moscow’s main priority, which has been laid out very explicitly, is to “eliminate the vestiges of domination by the U.S. and other unfriendly states in global affairs.”88 The Center for European Policy Analysis
argued that “[i]n Russia’s struggle to make the world safe for its brand of autocracy, the United States is the target. The battleground, however, is Europe.”89 Its illegal invasion of Ukraine is indeed the most blatant attempt from the Kremlin to reshape the global order. However, it is far from the only front. This same struggle is playing out in Africa, Asia, and Latin America and the Caribbean as well. Framing Russia’s role as merely
regional risks, therefore, naively missing the inroads Moscow is making elsewhere. Countering this requires more than military might; it demands a comprehensive foreign policy. A recent Coalition for Global Prosperity report highlighted how Russia exploits development challenges to insert its priorities into the foreign and domestic policies of many developing countries.90 As such, the UK and the U.S., alongside other allies, need
to use well-funded and well-designed development budgets to combat the conditions that allow Moscow to extend its influence. Importantly, new methods for development need to be considered to allow for speedier, more focused, and more flexible development approaches that align with strategic priorities. Russia’s Motivations for its Anti-Western Movement • “Counter anti-Russian activities carried out by foreign states and
their associations, and create conditions conducive to stopping such activities.” • “Provide assistance to Russian allies and partners in promoting shared interests, ensuring their security and sustainable development.” • “Consolidate Russia’s position in the world economy.” • “Ensure Russia’s interests in the world’s oceans, space and airspace.” • “Enhance Russia’s role in the global humanitarian space.” • “Consolidate the position for
the Russian language in the world.” • “Contribute to the preservation abroad of historical truth and the memory of Russia’s role in the world.”91 Moscow sees, therefore, that in a world that is increasingly dominated by hard power – itself being the main architect of that world – there remains a role for influence to achieve one's objectives. Like China, Russia desires the return of spheres of influence. Yet, while this is often
interpreted as meaning Russia’s near periphery – Ukraine, Belarus, Georgia, the Baltics, and several Central Asian nations – it also wants to extend its influence across continents. African and Latin American countries sit firmly on Russia’s radar, and in many ways, it has successfully extended its power and gained an ability to impact the policies and positions of many nations. Its use of paramilitary groups propping up regimes, the use
of disinformation and electoral interference, and arms supplies have ensured that Russia attracts more support in international forums than it did previously, that it can exploit resources to fund its war in Ukraine, and ensure Russia’s military interests outside of its near periphery. Western military leaders have expressed great concern over Russia’s rising influence in developing countries. General Michael Langley, the Commander of
the United States Africa Command, stated that “the Russian Federation is really trying to take over Central Africa as well as the Sahel.” He stressed that in the competition across Africa with Russia, the U.S. and its NATO allies needed to work better to develop “more strategic partner programs across the continent”.92 Chapter 4 - Economic Security is National Security It has long been true that no world leader makes foreign policy
decisions without considering domestic implications. Today, foreign and domestic policy are more intertwined than ever. A resilient economy and a strong industrial base, capable of innovating and outcompeting foreign rivals, are essential to America’s global standing and its national security. And vice versa, a powerful and comprehensive foreign policy supports domestic industry by securing vital resources and opening up export
markets through an effective development and diplomatic strategy. ‘Foreign Policy for the Middle Class’ This interplay between foreign and domestic policies was no better shown than at the start of the Biden administration. In the years leading up to Biden’s presidency, the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace ran a project named ‘Foreign Policy for the Middle Class’, exploring how U.S. foreign policy should evolve to better
serve the interests of American workers. Featuring Biden’s soon-to-be National Security Advisor, Jake Sullivan, it acknowledged that the “United States must be strong at home to be strong abroad.” Meanwhile, for renewal at home, the U.S. “must
conceive of its role in the world differently.” It advocated a policy agenda that would preserve the benefits of American business dynamism and trade openness, increase U.S. competitiveness globally, and boost industrial resilience. Central to this vision was managing the strategic competition with China and countering its efforts towards economic and technological hegemony.93 Although the idea of a ‘foreign policy for the middle
class’ faded from administration rhetoric following the invasion of Ukraine, the principles were reflected in much of Biden’s industrial strategy. During his presidency, Joe Biden pledged around $1 trillion in subsidies for semiconductor manufacturing and research, energy investment, and infrastructure spending.94 The Inflation Reduction Act funnelled hundreds of billions of dollars for clean energy and industrial revitalisation and
productivity.95 Meanwhile, the CHIPS and Science Act served a dual purpose. First, it aimed to strengthen American supply chain resilience for semiconductors by reshoring their manufacturing and diversifying supply chains. Second, it sought to counter China’s ascendency by preventing companies that received subsidies from the U.S. government from producing chips in China.96 By prioritising sectors dependent on adversaries,
the Biden administration channelled enormous subsidies to re-establish or expand tech manufacturing in the U.S. This quasi-protectionist approach has been intensified by the second Trump administration, which previously placed tariffs of 145% on China in an attempt to encourage American consumers to buy American goods and create conditions for domestic industrial investment.97 Whatever the future U.S. tariffs on China,
one thing is certain: the desire to bolster U.S. industry in the face of Chinese competition will not disappear anytime soon. Fears over China permeate throughout the American economy. Politicians have voiced serious concerns about over-reliance on Western adversaries for pharmaceutical products, urging friend-shoring with the UK. Between 2016 and 2022, there was a 650% increase in U.S. pharmaceutical imports from
China.98 In agriculture, states across the country have raised fears over Chinese companies owning land and businesses. As such, the State of Missouri recently banned Chinese-owned farmland near the many military facilities in the state.99 These fears are not unique to the U.S. In the UK, the 2021 Integrated Review described China as “the biggest state-based threat to the UK’s economic security,” reflecting a gradual alignment on

the economic risks.100 Economic security relies on a strong and comprehensive foreign policy. There are resources that industries need and export markets that will see huge growth opportunities. Stepping back from developing countries at a time when domestic industries are in need of a boost is, therefore, counterproductive. A Mineral-Dominated Economy China’s hold on critical minerals poses significant challenges for
Western economies. In his first administration, President Trump declared the reliance on China for critical minerals a “national emergency.”101 More recently, the Secretary of State, Marco Rubio, claimed in his Senate confirmation hearing that China is “scooping up” mineral rights from its economic engagement across the world.102 Should the U.S. fail to diversify its critical mineral supply chain away from China, there is a fear that
much of what America depends on for its economic growth will be dictated by Beijing. Critical minerals are the lifeblood of modern economies.
From defence and electric vehicles to computing, renewable energy, and medical technology, a wide range of industries depend on reliable access to these resources. As global demand accelerates, competition to secure these resources will shape the behaviour of many global players, not least the U.S. and China. The International Energy Agency predicts that demand for critical minerals will increase by up to 350% by the middle of
this century. In defence alone, critical minerals are used in a wide array of applications, including weapons systems, ammunition, and aerospace technologies. NATO has listed twelve critical minerals and materials that are essential for its defence capabilities: aluminium, beryllium, cobalt, gallium, germanium, graphite, lithium, manganese, platinum, rare earth elements, titanium, and tungsten.104 China dominates the U.S. import
supply for several of these, serving as the primary import source for germanium, graphite, rare earths, and tungsten, and is a major provider of gallium.105 With almost complete Chinese dominance over mineral processing, Western supply chains are over-exposed to China, and in times of tension, Beijing has been able to weaponise minerals to undermine adversarial industries. In 2010, China put an export ban on rare earth
elements to Japan following a territorial dispute, forcing Tokyo to radically change its approach to supplying key mineraldependent industries.106 More recently, the Biden administration faced Chinese export restrictions on minerals with military applications such as gallium, germanium, antimony, graphite, and tungsten.107 Likewise, as the trade war between China and the U.S. intensified under President Trump’s recent tariff
regime, China again ordered restrictions on the exports of rare earths to the U.S., undermining America’s ability to build fighter jets, missiles, and unmanned vehicles.108 As the economic, technological, and military competition between the two superpowers intensifies, Beijing has an advantage due to its dominance in this field. America’s industry will struggle to revive itself and its military weakened relative to China as supplies
needed for its industries stall. Concerns have been voiced by the UK’s Defence Select Committee which argued that reliance on China for critical minerals used in the defence supply chains presents a significant risk to weapons and ammunition production in the UK and, therefore, the country’s defence.109 Similarly, the National Security Strategy, released in June 2025, stated that advances in science and technology would mean
“greater competition for sources of energy and critical minerals. The frontrunner in this competition could achieve technological predominance,” which would have profound implications for the UK’s safety and security.110 It is not simply the UK’s dependence on China that poses a threat to UK security, but broader allied dependence, particularly America’s. The UK is a large importer of military equipment from the U.S. – which, as
highlighted, relies on critical minerals. The UK currently has almost 40 operational F-35B Lightning II fighter jets, built by Lockheed Martin in Texas, and seeks to expand that number over the next decade.111 There is a wide variety of critical minerals needed to successfully support the production of F-35s, as well as other defence imports. For instance, each F-35 jet requires upwards of 400 kilograms of rare earths,112 cobalt
supplies for engines, and beryllium, lithium, and gallium for a variety of other applications. Likewise, UK government attempts to promote green energy are vulnerable as a result of high levels of Chinese domination. With lithium, graphite, cobalt, and rare earths crucial in the make up of batteries, wind turbines, solar panels, electric vehicles, and more, the UK’s energy and economic security will be under threat due to China’s
dominance in the processing of these minerals. Without sure and diversified supply chains, the UK could find itself in a dangerous economic knot, with long-term consequences for national resilience. A Testament to Beijing’s Foreign Policy China’s dominance over critical minerals is a testament to its foreign policy success. Through the Belt and Road Initiative, the CCP has invested billions to seize control of critical minerals across
Africa, Asia and Latin America to export them to China for processing. According to AIDDATA, China “has bankrolled mine acquisitions, the development and expansion of mineral extraction infrastructure, and the day-to-day operational needs of mine owners and operators.”113 The Chinese Communist Party has no intention to stop there. It appears determined to protect its supplies by investing more in critical mineral mines. Nor
is it alone in doing so. Russia and its paramilitary groups have gained access to mining rights through its role in supporting authoritarian regimes. With Africa home to upwards of 30% of the world’s mineral reserves,114 the need for the U.S., UK, and allies to mount a serious competitive response has never been more urgent. Many developing countries are now seeing the consequences of China’s economic statecraft, undermining
the supposed ‘win-win’ nature of its financing. Beijing’s investments in critical minerals are having disastrous human and economic consequences. In the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC), it is estimated that 40,000 children work in cobalt, lithium, and rare earth mining, and China-led mining activities have only increased child labour issues. Indeed, Congolese lawyers have indicated that in China-owned or -operated mines in the
DRC, children often work seven days a week, for more than 12 hours a day, and are exposed to injuries and diseases. Meanwhile, environmental safeguards are overlooked, accelerating environmental degradation.115 The troubling practices are not limited to the DRC. In Zimbabwe, trade unions have pleaded to the Chinese embassy for its companies to abide by local laws. Workers there have accused Chinese mining companies of
undermining health and safety and physical abuse.116 Consequently, many developing nations are eager to diversify their mining partnerships. China’s continuous disregard for environmental, social, and governance (ESG) standards and local regulations has made Western companies – which have higher ethical and operational standards – more attractive alternatives. However, the U.S., UK, and allies have so far failed to offer a
compelling alternative and have not created the conditions for greater private sector involvement. Failures to adequately tackle development challenges like political instability alongside weak economic and judicial systems have meant that Western companies have been reluctant to invest and operate in mineral extraction due to risk.117 Some of the most mineral-rich nations are the most fragile and hardest to conduct business
in. To challenge China’s control effectively and ensure secure supplies for U.S., UK, and other NATO allies’ defence and technologies industries, a more coordinated approach is essential – one that actively looks to reduce risk and open doors for Western companies to operate in these markets with confidence – and uses an effective diplomatic and development strategy to boost partnerships with mineral-rich partners. That said,
there have been some steps to address challenges. In 2022, a group of fourteen countries: Australia, Canada, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, India, Italy, Japan, Norway, South Korea, Sweden, the UK, and the U.S., as well as the EU, announced the creation of the Minerals Security Partnership (MSP). The partnership aims to create more diversified and resilient supply chains for critical minerals, promote ESG standards in mining
and processing, and increase the recycling of minerals.118 The MSP, however, has largely failed to engage effectively with mineral-rich nations. PART 2 - OPPORTUNITIES AND CHALLENGES FOR UK-U.S. COOPERATION The closeness of the relationship between the UK and the U.S. is fundamentally driven by each other’s ability to contribute to the other’s foreign policy objectives. Taking steps to keep the U.S. on top in its competition
with the new axis of autocracies, mainly China and Russia but also Iran, must be a priority for UK foreign policy. Making practical policies to support Washington in tackling shared threats and challenges in the areas outlined in the previous chapter would strengthen the transatlantic alliance in an era of potential vulnerability and support UK defence, security, economic prosperity, and values over the coming decades. Most
importantly, it provides a pathway to keep Washington involved and committed to the relationship with the UK, as well as the wider transatlantic relationship. It is equally important for the UK to avoid overcommitting and underperforming, which could be just as harmful as not committing in some areas at all. Overextension would not only cast doubt on the UK’s value but would also weaken UK security by leaving gaps ripe for
exploitation by Russia and China. There remains, nevertheless, a plethora of things that the UK can be doing to counter China, deter Russia and support U.S. interests. And there remain many challenges that the UK will have to confront. The UK’s approach to China, AUKUS, and burden sharing in European security, as well as rethinking international development and derisking critical supply chains all, if done well, will support the
goal of stabilising and securing the strength of UK-U.S. relations over the coming decades. Chapter 5 - The UK’s China Approach The UK’s approach to China has long been inconsistent, distorted, and reactive. From the so-called ‘Golden Era’ of UK-China relations that envisaged close investment and trade ties between Beijing and London, to the downturn in relations post-Cameron, all the way through to current attempts to court
economic opportunities, the UK has lacked a coherent and enduring China approach. Successive leaders have likewise struggled to maintain consistency. Official rhetoric has shifted regularly from describing China as a systemic challenge, as described in the Integrated Reviews, to labelling Beijing as a threat, a term used by Rishi Sunak and echoed by other senior figures in the last Conservative government. Most recently, the 2025
Strategic Defence Review called China a “sophisticated and persistent challenge.”119 Both parties’ China approaches have been widely criticised for a lack of clarity, coherence, and specifics. Experts at the Royal United Services Institute accused the previous Conservative approach as one that “smacks of ‘cakeism’, promising to secure all the benefits and address all the negatives that China presents, while ducking value judgements
and tough choices on priorities.”122 Meanwhile, the current Labour approach is far too vague for practical policy-making. Neither party has clearly defined red lines or articulated what the top priority is – be it security, growth, or values This lack of coherence in language and policy could have consequences for the UK’s future relationship with the United States. Washington expects allies to take a firm and clear stance. A tendency
towards reactive, short-term policy-making risks damaging the UK’s standing in the eyes of American leaders, putting a strain on the military, intelligence, and diplomatic ties with the country’s closest ally. Trump’s chief trade advisor, Pete Navarro, recently accused the UK of being a “compliant servant of communist China.”123 While this is an exaggerated and inflammatory claim, it highlights the pressing need for consistency in how
the UK articulates and enacts its China policy. While it is right to recognise China as a threat – and do so consistently – this should not lead to total disengagement. Cutting off dialogue or cooperation entirely would be counterproductive, especially in addressing global challenges like health security. Meanwhile, with the likelihood of rising tensions between Washington and Beijing, complete breakdowns in dialogue between the UK
and China could increase global instability. China is becoming more important to the global health ecosystem, particularly in disease management and antimicrobial resistance (AMR). It has funded antibiotic research significantly and, given its high rates of AMR in China, will be central to the development of new products, technologies, and medicines to tackle the challenge.124 Greater collaboration on pandemic preparedness and
AMR will be essential to curbing future infectious disease outbreaks. Likewise, as global volatility and unpredictability increase, it becomes more important for the UK to have high-level dialogue with Chinese officials. As the closest partner to Washington, the UK is uniquely positioned to serve as a mediator between Washington and Beijing to de-escalate tensions and reduce the risks of miscalculation on both parts. As chances of
conflict by misunderstanding, miscalculation, and an asymmetry of information become more likely, these meetings between military leaders are invaluable to avoid conflict, serving as important intelligence-gathering exercises for both the West and China to understand foreign policy and military intentions and capabilities. Nevertheless, a trip by the Chief of Defence Staff (CDS), Admiral Sir Tony Radakin, to China in April 2025 was
the first official visit by a UK CDS in a decade.125 Sparse meetings between military leaders from adversarial nations are likely to increase tensions, distrust, and chances of conflict. Consequently, to maintain peace in the IndoPacific, the UK should engage more directly and more regularly with the Chinese military leadership.126 In recent years, UK politicians have been strong and consistent when exposing and speaking out against
the CCP’s repression and abuses in Xinjiang, Tibet, and Hong Kong. Previous Conservative governments regularly condemned the CCP’s targeting of Uyghur Muslims, forced labour, restrictions on religion, and the persecution of minorities across China, especially in Xinjiang and Tibet. Parliament has been equally vocal. In 2021, the House of Commons passed a motion that declared that crimes against humanity and genocide were
occurring in Xinjiang.127 Similarly, the erosion of the Sino-British Joint Declaration, in particular through the National Security Law, led to the new Hong Kong BN(O) visa route which allows Hong Kongers to live, work, and study in the UK.128 When there has been Chinese repression against pro-democracy protests in the UK, politicians have also spoken up. However, when actions like this occur across the rest of the world, silence
has been palpable. Due to the role that Chinese discourse, economic statecraft, and security activities play in bolstering its antiAmerican and anti-Western movements, firmer approaches to tackling Chinese soft power and influence are needed. This includes using platforms at the UN and other international forums to spotlight the damaging impacts of Chinese global activity on human rights, sovereignty, and socio-economic
development. The UK has been failing to counter China’s soft power and its claims of SouthSouth cooperation – a pillar of its anti-American movement. The success of this movement hinges on developing nations believing that China is a more reliable partner than the West, and better understands and supports their interests. The narrative promoted from Beijing conceals a darker reality: human rights abuses, repressive practices,
and extraterritorial arrangements are embedded in its overseas activities. Publicly challenging China’s practices on the global stage would not only reinforce the UK’s commitment to transatlantic values but also signal to the White House that the UK is taking China’s anti-Western movement and its broader ambitions seriously. Such a move would reaffirm the UK’s alignment with the U.S. and demonstrate a willingness to lead in
defending a global order against an autocratic movement. Chapter 6 – AUKUS AUKUS is a trilateral partnership between the UK, U.S., and Australia that intends “to support a free and open Indo-Pacific that is peaceful, secure and stable,” as well as deepen information and technology sharing between the three partners.129 It is structured around two pillars. Pillar 1 focuses on equipping Australia with conventionally-armed
nuclear-powered submarines. This first involves the sale of Virginia class submarines, followed by the joint development of a next-generation submarine – SSN-AUKUS – by the UK and Australia in the 2030s and early 2040, integrating technologies from all three partners. Pillar 2 is much broader in scope, focusing on developing capabilities, technology sharing and interoperability in new defence technologies such as AI, quantum
technology, cyber, undersea, hypersonic, counter-hypersonic, and electronic warfare.130 For the UK, AUKUS presents significant industrial and strategic opportunities. It offers a boost to the defence sector, promising some 21,000 jobs and technological advancement. Similarly, the Royal Navy stands to benefit from acquiring the best nuclear-powered submarines in the world – an increasingly vital asset in light of Russia and China’s
investments in undersea capabilities. Likewise, the success of Pillar 2 can bolster the UK’s technological prowess and ensure the upper hand against adversaries. However, the UK is particularly exposed should AUKUS falter. Not only would the UK lose out on industrial opportunities, but it could lead to an overreliance on the U.S., potentially diminishing the UK’s standing in the relationship and calling into question Westminster’s
value as a partner.132 For the U.S., the importance of AUKUS’s success cannot be overstated. It greatly enhances the deterrence capabilities of the U.S., UK, and Australia in the face of China. Currently, the Virginia class submarines and the UK’s Astute class stand almost unrivalled in undersea warfare – only Russia comes close to competing. Nevertheless, Beijing’s industrial output and its investments in research and development
could mean that it soon catches up.133 Despite a review being ordered in Washington on the future of the deal, AUKUS has received immense bipartisan support on Capitol Hill due to its strategic benefits. Marco Rubio recently stated that AUKUS “is something that I think you’re going to find very strong support for in this administration”, with the partnership being “almost a blueprint” for how the U.S. should work with other
allies. AUKUS is, in his opinion, “one example of how we can leverage the power of these partnerships with allies … to reach outcomes and objectives such as creating a geopolitical and strategic balance in the Indo-Pacific region and beyond.”134 This shows that the ability of the UK to drive AUKUS forward will be one of the central considerations for Washington to put time, money, and resources into the relationship AUKUS Pillar
2, as argued by the Center for Strategic and International Studies, offers “game-winning potential in the strategic competition with China”, helping to secure both military and economic advantage for AUKUS nations long into the future.135 In 2023, China was ahead of the U.S. in 19 of the 23 technologies relevant to AUKUS Pillar 2.136 As highlighted in Chapter 1, China’s rapid technological progress threatens to outpace Western
efforts unless addressed collectively. A coordinated AUKUS approach to R&D, alongside other Indo-Pacific partners like Japan and South Korea, can help to close the gap in research performance to China.137 Exercising interoperability is a core American priority in working with allies, and AUKUS is beginning to make progress here. In 2024, the three partners conducted Maritime Big Play, a joint experiment in Australia aimed at
testing new defence technologies and improving interoperability. Innovators participated alongside militaries, allowing the evaluation of new technologies.138 Likewise, Japan joined as an observer following consultations about closer collaboration under Pillar 2.139 Improving interoperability through AUKUS will strengthen the ability to act in concert during joint operations and reduce friction in achieving shared objectives.
Despite the game-changing potential of both AUKUS pillars, there remain significant hurdles to overcome. One concern is whether the U.S. industrial base can cope with ramping up production to make up for the Virginia Class submarine sales to Australia while maintaining its own needs. The U.S. will need to simultaneously construct up to five new Virginia class submarines to maintain its strength.140 America’s ageing shipbuilding
capacity and underinvestment in industrial infrastructure could place the early phases of Pillar 1 at risk if the U.S. determines it cannot divert submarines to Australia. The success of Pillar 2 is indispensable. In September last year, the U.S. eased export controls on defence technology transfer by exempting AUKUS nations from its International Traffic in Arms Regulations, allowing the UK and Australia favoured access to the U.S.
defence industrial base.141 However, beyond the removal of export controls, there lacks a clear direction and strategy for Pillar 2. Questions remain about its goals, the intended outcomes and how the three nations will coordinate effectively to develop next-generation capabilities. The Strategic Defence Review outlined that the UK should ensure that AUKUS is an exemplar of co-innovation and industrial collaboration between
allies,” and that the UK should use “Pillar 2 to test and develop a template for future technology partnerships.”142 Despite an £86 billion commitment for defence- and securityrelated research and development from the Government in the National Security Strategy, there has been a worrying lack of coordination of efforts between AUKUS partners on Pillar 2.143 Greater collaboration in this space, as outlined by Dr Emma
Salisbury, “allows for cost-sharing and reduces the financial burden on any single nation. This can accelerate the development and deployment of cutting-edge technologies, benefiting all parties involved.”144 With better R&D comes greater progress but there has failed to be an environment between the three nations, and potentially with others – notably Japan and South Korea – to support joint innovation. With joint R&D efforts
being viewed positively in Washington, the UK should actively seek to shape this ecosystem, ensuring that innovation can occur at scale and pace. This will not just deter Chinese activities but also allow the UK to increase capabilities in the face of future Russian aggression. Chapter 7 - Demonstrating European Burden Sharing As outlined in Chapter 2, Northern Europe and the Arctic are set to be key areas of focus for the White
House over the next decades, driven by its strategic challenges and its economic opportunities. Likewise, Europe, despite the potential drawdown of U.S. forces, will continue to face security challenges that impact American interests. The UK’s position in the High North is unique. Despite not being an Arctic power – the Shetland Islands lie roughly 400 miles south of the Arctic Circle – the UK has historically played an important role.
The UK is one of the only non-Arctic nations that invests in its capability in the region and has a regular military presence there.145 For over half a century, the UK has conducted cold-weather training in Norway, and every year Royal Marines train U.S. Marines in Arctic warfare, enhancing their operational, tactical, and survival skills.146 Given the high turnover rate among U.S. Marines, with only around a third reenlisting, British
experience and leadership in the region is highly valuable.147 Likewise, the UK has consistently contributed to major Arctic exercises. Since 2006, Norway has hosted Exercise Cold Response, giving allies military opportunities to train and operate cohesively in the toughest circumstances. In 2022, the UK contributed six Royal Navy ships – including one aircraft carrier – and 2,000 personnel to the 27-country exercise.148 It is under
the Joint Expeditionary Force (JEF) that the UK can truly prove to the U.S. the value of UK and European partners in tackling threats spanning from Europe and the High North. The JEF is a UK-led military partnership focused on the High North, North Atlantic and Baltic Sea. Consisting of the UK, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Iceland, Latvia, Lithuania, the Netherlands, Norway, and Sweden, it enables rapid deployment of forces across
multiple environments. The JEF is not a duplicate of NATO and falls outside of its command structure. However, all JEF members are in NATO and the group enhances NATO activities by ensuring a more flexible and rapidly deployable response to security crises and threats, even those below the Article Five threshold where NATO is unable or unwilling to respond.149 With Washington not just fearing conventional threats from
Russia, but also subthreshold activities, such as sabotage to critical infrastructure like undersea cables, the role of the JEF becomes even more important to the U.S. Russian vessels seeking to disrupt transatlantic infrastructure pass through JEF waters, making regional surveillance, defence, and partnerships paramount. In recent leaders meetings of the JEF, the group has remarked that one of its aims is to "constrain, contest, and
counter Russia’s aggression and threats to regional security” by increasing efforts to deter, defend against, and counter Russian sabotage, acts of violence, cyber and electronic interference, and disinformation.150 In response to the growing sub-threshold threat, the JEF has intensified efforts to safeguard critical underwater infrastructure. In 2023, a JEF Response Option was activated for the first time. This saw the deployment of
29 vessels and 11 aircraft to patrol a million square miles of sea. In 2024, Nordic Warden was launched whereby all 10 members tracked and monitored the Russian shadow fleet as it was looking to damage undersea infrastructure.151 U.S. frustration with European defence spending is long-standing and demonstrating concrete action to share the burden is key to maintaining U.S. engagement in European security. The JEF is a great
vehicle for this. First, all JEF members have consistently spent over 2% of their GDP on defence and have seen some of the fastest rises in defence spending out of all NATO countries. Indeed, the Royal United Services Institute outlines that since 2014, JEF members have seen an average real-term defence spending increase of 150%, compared to 108% for the rest of NATO’s European members. Lithuania, for example, has seen a
327% increase. Likewise, all JEF members have pulled their weight when assisting Ukraine. Of the top 10 European NATO members providing assistance to Ukraine (as a percentage of GDP), eight are in the JEF.153 Yet, a key challenge in demonstrating the JEF’s value is the lack of awareness and understanding of the group in Washington. Outside specialised circles, knowledge of the JEF’s mission, operations, and structure remains
limited – even within the Pentagon and State Department. As the leader of the JEF, the UK must do more to promote the group’s role in European and transatlantic security to key audiences in the White House, Pentagon, State Department, and on Capitol Hill. Failure to do so risks allowing U.S. perceptions to be shaped by underperforming allies, potentially justifying a reduced American commitment to European defence. At the
same time, the UK can and should be doing more than simply demonstrating the JEF’s role in burden sharing. As discussed in Chapter Two, a resurgent Russia after the war in Ukraine will continue to be a defining threat for the UK, U.S., and NATO allies for years to come. Beyond the war, the Kremlin’s objectives are unlikely to change and it is likely to undertake large investments in advanced technologies and force size. In this
context, calls to halt increases or even reduce defence spending once the war ends will be dangerously short-sighted. Some may argue that the crisis has passed and that ‘peace’ allows for cutbacks. If the UK is serious about deterring future Russian aggression, it must continue to increase defence spending, capabilities, and innovation. American frustration over European defence spending is completely justified. The lack of
