In the first three quarters of this year, China added 310 gigawatts of renewable energy capacity, up 47.7
per cent year on year and accounting for 84.4 per cent of all new power installations in the country,
according to the National Energy Administration. This progress is more than climate leadership or capacity building; it involves
a holistic reconstruction of the energy system. China is replacing the 20th-century fossil fuel order with a 21st-century
“electro-industrial order” where power lies not in the control of oil and gas, but with those managing
power grids, batteries and minerals. This shift partly arises from China’s energy vulnerabilities. Despite its significant domestic
energy production, China’s rapid industrialisation was driven by imported oil and gas. As demand consistently exceeded domestic supply, China
became a net oil importer in 1993. This reliance on imported oil and gas – primarily through the Malacca Strait, which handles about 80 per cent
of its oil imports – combined with US naval control over key maritime routes in the South China Sea and Indian Ocean, increases China’s risks.
Maritime insecurity has further underscored the dangers of relying on sea-shipped fossil fuels. Consequently, China has turned to renewable
energy to reduce its vulnerabilities and enhance its long-term energy security. China’s drive towards renewable energy is, however, also affected
by domestic factors. Despite its rising renewable capacity, China remains heavily reliant on domestic coal for electricity. Last year, 58 per cent of
its electricity came from coal. Yet coal-fired power plants also create air pollution and public unhappiness, and this creates an incentive to
expand the drive towards renewable energy. But while the national policy on the energy transition is clear, local governments wield considerable
influence and they must balance energy priorities with the pursuit of national growth. The surprising hurdle slowing China’s switch to green
energy The surprising hurdle slowing China’s switch to green energy Beijing is driven by both environmental concerns and
structural energy inefficiencies to invest heavily in renewable energy. Through consistent policies,
advanced grid technology and manufacturing improvements, Beijing aims to build a comprehensive
renewable energy system. China’s journey to being an architect of the renewable energy order can be linked to its success across four
pillars: manufacturing, modernisation, storage and access to critical minerals. China dominates the renewable energy
manufacturing supply chain, from solar panels and wind turbines to batteries and electric vehicle parts.
Reinforced by policies, subsidies and research and development, China is influencing global technological standards and this confers both
economic and geopolitical advantages. As part of its grid modernisation, China’s investments in ultra-high-voltage transmission systems mean
that electricity generated in the sunny inland regions can be transmitted to the coastal cities. Its smart grid technology has found extensive
markets in Asia, the Middle East and Africa, extending its role well beyond that of a solar-panel producer. Meanwhile, China’s leadership in
battery storage enables it to ensure supply stability, essential for the sustainability of the renewable energy sector. Also, by partnering
with the Global South, China has access to critical materials such as lithium, cobalt and rare earths,
vital for batteries and renewable technology. But China’s renewable energy strategy is not just
reducing its energy vulnerabilities, it is also boosting its influence and positioning it as a leader in a new
energy order. This new order is being exported via the green Belt and Road Initiative, development pathways and norm-setting. China is
building solar farms, wind parks and hydropower projects abroad, offering an integrated ecosystem of finance, equipment, labour and training.
With Africa, South Asia and Southeast Asia increasingly adopting Chinese technology, China is modelling development pathways, providing
cost-effective solutions and strengthening energy resilience. Crucially, it is also setting the tech standards and norms.

CMs = critical minerals
China stands out as the world’s largest importer of CMs to support its ambitious clean and low-carbon
energy deployment strategy (Rosenau-Tornow et al., 2009, Kim et al., 2015, Qiao et al., 2021). More than 15 types of CMs depend on
foreign supply from a limited number of countries (Gulley et al., 2018). For example, in the case of cobalt and nickel, which are key components
of electronic vehicles, less than 20 % of their mining production comes from domestic supply (United States Geological Survey, 2023). In this
context, China’s outsourcing of CMs is prone to constraints on suppliers’ ability (e.g., local mining conditions
(Yager et al., 2012)) and willingness (e.g., trade restrictions (Gulley et al., 2018, Santillán-Saldivar et al., 2021)) due to worldwide
geopolitical issues (Li et al., 2020, Ren et al., 2021). These factors may trigger unexpected shocks such as price spikes
(He et al., 2022) and consequently result in supply disruptions, especially for CMs with high market prices,
which are irreplaceable in pivotal clean energy technologies (Blengini et al., 2017, Gemechu et al., 2016, Wang et al.,
2023, Wang et al., 2023). As China continues to invest more in renewable energy infrastructure (Wang et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2023, Yuan et al.,
2023), this may lead to greater challenges for CMs supply within the context of highly uncertain global geopolitics (Gómez et al., 2023).
Therefore, it is imperative to gain a better understanding of China’s current supply risk to facilitate early risk warning. Scientific knowledge about
the multifaceted supply risks of CMs is fundamental for navigating risk management strategies and ensuring an efficient low-carbon energy
transition (Graedel et al., 2015, Graedel et al., 2015, Zhang et al., 2021, Helbig et al., 2016, Nassar et al., 2020, Liang et al., 2022).

To limit the global temperature increase to 1.5 degrees C or close to it, all countries must
decarbonize—cut fossil fuel use, transition to zero-carbon renewable energy sources, and electrify as
many sectors as possible. It will require huge numbers of wind turbines, solar panels, electric vehicles
(EVs), and storage batteries — all of which are made with rare earth elements and critical metals. The
elements critical to the energy transition include the 17 rare earth elements, the 15 lanthanides plus scandium and yttrium. While many rare
earth metals are actually common, they are called “rare” because they are seldom found in sufficient amounts to be extracted easily or
economically. Elements such as silicon, cobalt, lithium, and manganese are not rare earth elements, but are critical minerals that are also
essential for the energy transition. Supplying these vast quantities of minerals in a sustainable manner will be a
significant challenge, but scientists are exploring a variety of ways to provide materials for the energy
transition with less harm to people and the planet. Demand is growing. The demand for rare earth elements is expected to
grow 400-600 percent over the next few decades, and the need for minerals such as lithium and graphite used in EV batteries could increase as
much as 4,000 percent. Most wind turbines use neodymium–iron–boron magnets, which contain the rare earth elements neodymium and

praseodymium to strengthen them, and dysprosium and terbium to make them resistant to demagnetization. Global demand for neodymium is
expected to grow 48 percent by 2050, exceeding the projected supply by 250 percent by 2030. The need for praseodymium could exceed supply
by 175 percent. Terbium demand is also expected to exceed supply. And to meet the anticipated demand by 2035 for graphite, lithium, nickel,
and cobalt, one analysis projected that 384 new mines would be needed. China once supplied 97 percent of the world’s rare
earth elements. Government support, cheap labor, lax environmental regulations, and low prices
enabled it to monopolize rare earth metal production. Today China produces 60-70 percent of the
world’s rare earth elements and is also securing mining rights in Africa. The U.S. produces a little over 14 percent
and Australia produces six percent of rare earth elements. In 2018, the U.S. was 100 percent dependent on other countries for 21 critical
minerals. After China halted exports of rare earth elements to Japan in a dispute, many countries became concerned about the political and
economic implications of depending on one market and began developing their own rare earth element production. The Biden administration
has prioritized the development of a domestic supply chain for rare earth metals and critical minerals. Mining’s environmental impacts. Mining
often causes pollution of land, water, and air, spread of toxic wastes, water depletion, deforestation, biodiversity loss, and social disruption.
Despite the fact that it is subject to federal and state environmental regulations, metal mining is the number one toxic polluter in the U.S. It’s
difficult to mine rare earth elements without causing environmental damage because of how they are extracted. One method involves removing
topsoil, then creating a leaching pool where chemicals are used to separate out the rare earth elements from the ore. The toxic chemicals can
seep into groundwater, cause erosion, and pollute the air. Another technique is to drill into the ground and use PVC pipes and hoses to pump
chemicals into the earth. The resulting mix is then pumped into leaching ponds for separation, creating the same environmental problems. In
addition, because rare earth elements are often found near radioactive thorium and uranium, the waste left after rare earth elements are
separated from the ore—tailings—contains chemicals, salts, and radioactive materials. Tailings are usually stored in ponds which can leak and
contaminate water resources. The Harvard International Review reported that mining to produce one ton of rare earth elements results in
nearly 30 pounds of dust, 9,600-12,000 cubic meters of waste gas including substances such as hydrofluoric acid and sulfur dioxide, 75 cubic
meters of wastewater, and one ton of radioactive residue—2,000 tons of toxic waste altogether. The world’s largest rare earth element mine,
Bayan-Obo in China, produced over 70,000 tons of radioactive thorium waste which is stored in a tailing pond that has leaked into groundwater.

mining. This has caused skeletal fluorosis and chronic arsenic toxicity in the population. In Jiangxi Province, which was also polluted by rare earth
element mining, experts say it could take 50 to 100 years to clean up the damage and restore the environment. Mining for other minerals such

contaminated the area and a Salmon River tributary; it is now a Superfund site. How can we supply the energy transition more sustainably?
With the growing demand for rare earth elements and critical minerals, mining practices that harm the environment will likely continue, if not
increase. “The pressure is such that that the first thing that might be disregarded and marginalized are the safeguards in order to fast track the

unnecessary. Here are some examples — most of which are still experimental and not yet ready for
large-scale application. Biomining. A variety of labs around the world are looking at ways to put biology to use in mining. Cornell

atomic numbers like dysprosium and terbium — from ores. The new electrokinetic method creates an
electric field above and below the soil, which improves the efficiency of the leaching so that lower
amounts of chemicals are needed. The method extracts more rare earth elements than traditional
mining and pollutes less.

The world is racing to increase supplies of lithium, cobalt, copper and other "critical minerals" that are
building blocks of modern technology. Not only are these minerals used in industries like defense,
healthcare and electronics, but they are also key to producing the clean energy technologies that are
increasingly powering our lives. Yet critical minerals present an environmental and social conundrum. While necessary in the fight against climate

production are also concentrated in a small handful of countries, and surging demand is adding to geopolitical tensions — from trade disputes to global debates over

responsible and equitable? What Are Critical Minerals and Why Do They Matter? Critical minerals are those that are essential to
countries' economies or development. They are generally in high demand and prone to supply chain
disruptions, although countries differ on which minerals they define as "critical" depending on their level of access, what the minerals are used for, their
export value and national supply risks. Most countries' critical mineral lists include copper, lithium, nickel, cobalt, graphite
and rare earth elements. These are used widely in clean energy technologies, such as wind turbines,
solar panels, EV batteries and motors, as well as in energy transmission infrastructure like power lines.

industries as well. NATO includes several on its list of essential raw materials for defense. And they are

name, rare earth elements tend not to be rare. The challenge is that they are often found in very low concentrations and are intermingled with other minerals,

technology has supercharged the world's need for critical minerals, and demand is expected to keep climbing. The

complex. Some critical minerals, like aluminum and palladium, have stable reserves on land that can fulfil the world's demand through 2050 and likely beyond.

though Chinese FDI in the metal sector are frequently mentioned, especially in the body of literature focusing on critical minerals (US terminology, see US Energy Act
2020) and critical raw materials (EU terminology, see EU CRM Act 2024), there still lacks a global and comprehensive assessment on the destination, and evolution of
these investments. This paper aims at fulfilling this gap by addressing the following questions: What are the key trends and patterns of Chinese foreign direct
investments in the metal sector regarding their evolution and geographic distribution? How are these investments integrated into China’s global political strategy?
What are the effects of these investments on the critical mineral supply chains in the context of the energy transition? Our paper addresses these questions by
proposing a global quantitative assessment of the Chinese investments in the metal sector from 2005 to 2024, using the dataset from American Enterprise Institute
(AEI), from the project Chinese Global Investment Tracker (AEI 2025). We qualitatively discuss the integration of these investments into China’s mineral supply
security strategy and the reaction of several host countries. Overall, this study cross-analyses the imbrication between resources, technology and economic issues
and in the light of global power competition. Institutional context of China’s global political strategy China’s industry transformation: from manufacturing hub to
high-tech industries In the early post-Mao era, Deng Xiaoping's reforms incentivized the production of large quantities of affordable consumer goods. Four special
economic zones (SEZs) were established on the southeast coast in 1980 and 1981: three in Guangdong and one in Fujian. These SEZs were designed to attract foreign
investment and labour force from the countryside, enabling large-scale production of inexpensive"Made in China"goods dedicated to exports on global markets (Luo
and Zhu 2018). The 2000 s marked a significant economic boom for China: the country joined the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001 and became the
second-largest economy by 2010. But many of the easy-growth-wins had already been achieved during the transition from traditional to labor-intensive industries,
making further growth more challenging by pursuing the same development pattern (Naughton 2021). The appointment of Xi Jinping as Chairman of
the Communist Party in 2012 and as President of the PRC in 2013 coincides with a strategic shift from an
economy based on common goods manufacturing and hard infrastructure operations (steel industries,
bulk minerals) toward high-technology industries (Li 2018; Kennedy 2015). On March 5, 2015, at the 12th Plenary Session of the
People's Assembly, the former Prime Minister Li Keqiang announced a 10-year plan that redefined China’s industrial priorities. The plan “Made in China 2025”
released by China’s State Council (国务院) aimed to ensure China’s competitiveness and leadership on innovation and
technology at the horizon 2049 for the centenary of the PRC founding. This plan was elaborated by the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT)
and the Chinese Academy of Engineering, revealing a preliminary reflexion aligning scientific expertise, industrial challenges and global strategic outlook on a long
term perspective. It promotes the rapid development of ten well-positioned strategic sectors such as generation IT,
high-end equipment, new materials, and biotech and pharma. Such transition from being a global
manufacturing hub to a world-class industrial power necessitates new mineral resources especially
metallic elements. Securitization of materials supply, thus, becomes crucial to the modernisation of these sectors
(Andersson 2020; Yan et al. 2021). Chinese strategists are well aware of that: in the Part III, Section VI, one key area focused on “New materials” (新材料) promoting
the development of an integrated military-civilian industry, while paying close attention to supply security concerns. The document also set the target to increase
domestic market share of Chinese suppliers for “core basic parts and key basic materials” to 70% by 2025. These documents reflect that supply security in specific
materials are considered national security concerns by the government. The material-energy nexus: raw materials requirements at global scale The expansion of
new technologies, especially energy transition and digital technologies, requires an increased supply of
several metallic elements. From a fundamental perspective, any energy system has material
prerequisites, and vice versa, implying that energy and material systems are deeply interconnected
(Ashby 2013; Hu et al. 2024). This material-energy nexus was first highlighted by early scholars (Mumford 1934; Simondon 1958) and has recently been studied in
the energy transition context. Extensive research has shown that the expansion of low-carbon energy and digital technologies
requires an increasing supply of specific metals such as copper, nickel, lithium, gallium, lanthanides, and others (Vidal et al. 2013;
Graedel et al. 2015; Fizaine and Court 2015; Seck et al. 2020; IEA 2021; Hache et al. 2022; IEA 2024). This is primarily because metallic
elements are essential component of electronic technologies involved in low carbon energy system,
from production (e.g., solar panel, wind energy), distribution (e.g., power grid), storage (e.g., batteries,
fuel cells) and end-uses (e.g., electronic and digital devices). Although some metals have specific applications—such as silver (Ag)
for solar PV, lanthanides (Nd, Dy, Pr) for permanent magnets used in wind turbines, lithium (Li) for batteries, aluminium (Al) for infrastructure, or copper (Cu) for
power grid—they are, to some extent, all contributing to sustain modern life by being “part of the system”, as

a result of the nexus between energy and materials. The scale at which mineral security must be addressed, from production to
consumption and associated pollution is thus inherently global (Chakrabarty 2019) and entangled with
interdependence and geopolitical dynamics. Ensuring a secured and growing supply of these materials
appears as a core challenge for China’s industrial strategy to be successful. China’s FDI and mineral supply security The
modernisation of China's industries is coupled with a supply security strategy that relies on FDI in the metal sector and infrastructure control abroad. By
supporting mergers, acquisitions, and investments in foreign countries, the government encourages
Chinese firms to establish a global value chain system that secures materials supplies for the domestic
high-tech industries. The overseas expansion of Chinese companies through FDI, particularly within the
framework of the Belt and Road Initiative (一带一路) are based on the principle of"mutual benefits” as stated in several official strategic
documents. For instance, the National Mineral Resources Planning (2016–2020) states: “Deepen the international mining cooperation of the"Belt and Road",
strengthen overseas mineral resource exploration and development, improve the quality and level of foreign investment,
introduce advanced technology and management experience, actively participate in global mining governance, and promote the formation of a deeply integrated
and mutually beneficial cooperation pattern.”.Footnote3 Applied to FDI in the metal sector, we assume that this principle implies Chinese firms bringing
funds and technical expertise to develop critical infrastructure abroad including (but not limited to)
mineral extraction projects. Because of their central role in the economic process, and more widely to the functioning of societies, energy
infrastructures (power plant, pipelines, refinery, electricity grid, etc.) and transport infrastructures (ports, railway, car road, etc.) are also key security targets. The
mineral industry heavily depends upon this infrastructure due to the global nature of its supply chains and the associated significant energy demand. By extending
their control over these critical infrastructures, Chinese firms build a relationship of dependence with foreign countries,
constituting a diplomatic pledge potentially favoring further access to the mineral resources needed by
China. The links between such dependency framework and a privileged access to specific mineral deposits through favored treatment in mining concession
attribution, exploitation license or cooperation agreement are particularly hard to prove, especially in countries with weak institutional structure (Cooke et al. 2015).
In this context, FDI should be considered as a proxy of a (potentially larger) dependency framework. Several organisations act as intermediates between the
government and firms, such as the China Chamber of Commerce of Metals, Minerals & Chemicals Importers & Exporters (CCCMC), and are in charge of monitoring
trade situations, collecting and studying economic information from the industry, and advising the relevant government departments to design adapted policies. By
carrying out industry inspection and examination these organisations behave as national agencies, ensuring Chinese companies adhere to the laws and regulations
of the government but also providing consulting support to companies in decision-making to overcome trade barriers, accompanying them in responding to foreign
trade investigations, and making regular reporting to the government to shape further supportive regulations (CCCMC 2025). Foreign direct investments (FDI) are
economic instruments that can create interdependence between the investing actor and the receiver of the investment. From a liberal perspective, FDI should
mitigate the possibility of conflict between the involved parties (Copeland 2015; Pevehouse 2004). However, when FDI provides the investing actor with control over
critical infrastructure in the target country, the interdependence can become asymmetrical. This control allows the investor to make the infrastructure unavailable
for arbitrary reasons, and implies a significant economic and political linkage to a foreign country (Godzimirsky and Andersen 2024). In this paper, we engage with
what Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye described as “complex interdependence” characterised by resources distribution, infrastructure ownership and vulnerabilities
among a wide variety actors, including many non-state actors, operating at the international level (Keohane and Nye 1977; Godzimirsky and Andersen 2024). Our
study analyses FDI as one instrument of China’s global political strategy, addressing mineral supply security challenges. Methodology Our methodology integrates
both quantitative and qualitative approaches to describe the key trends and patterns in China’s FDI in the metal sector and their implications for global mineral
supply chains. In this paper, the term “mineral” refers to mineral-based commodities that are metal-bearing raw materials, such as ores and concentrates. The
primary dataset used in this study is sourced from the American Enterprise Institute (AEI) within the project Chinese Global Investment tracker. Our analysis
specifically examines FDI reported under the categories “investment” and “construction”, which corresponds to the combined data from datasets No.1 and No. 2.
The dataset we used tracks 4466 transactions, across 166 countries and 14 different sectors, from 2005 to July 2024, capturing the dynamics of China’s FDI over
nearly two decades. The AEI database focuses on significant investments, excluding transactions smaller than 95 million USD, to capture the most impactful FDI
activities. Descriptive statistics were then used to summarize the most salient trends and patterns in China's FDI in the metal sector. The term “metal sector” is used
according to the definition of the AEI, and refers to metal-related industrial activities, including mineral exploration, mining, processing (upstream segment of the
value chain) and refining, smelting and manufacturing (downstream segment of the value chain). It is important to note that Chinese FDI are mostly oriented
towards upstream activities in the metal sector. The analysis focused on identifying the geographic distribution, sectoral allocation, and temporal evolution of these
investments. The qualitative data were further enriched by additional data from the European Union Joint Research Center (EU JRC), the US Geological Survey
(USGS) and the Resources Trade Earth dataset, which provided insights into global mineral reserves and production trends. To complement the quantitative findings,
a qualitative review of policy papers, reports from international organizations, and industry analyses was conducted, with the case of Australia and Peru more
specifically analysed. Overall, quantitative data highlighted the scale and distribution of China's investments in the metal sector globally, while the qualitative
analysis offered insights into the geopolitical implications of these investments. Analysis Global trends and patterns of the Chinese FDI in the metal sector Trends in
global Chinese FDI Between 2005 and 2024, cumulated Chinese FDI amounted to some 2.5 trillion USD. Total FDI saw a significant increase from 2013 to the peak
year 2017 (see Fig. 2). After 2017, total FDI decreases with a significant drop after Covid-19 pandemic in 2020. Since 2020, they have remained relatively stable at
around 90 billion USD per year. According to the data provided by the AEI, the share of the metals sector was around 10% of the total FDI over the period, amounting
to around 230 billion USD. The metals sector finds third place after energy (35%) and transport (19%) as shown on Fig. 3. Taken together, the energy, transport and
metals sector account for 63% average of total Chinese FDI over the studied period. From a mineral supply security perspective, these investments can
be seen as complementary because they enable to set up the infrastructure that sustains the global

mineral supply chains. The FDI in the metals sector were spread across 65 countries. Their cumulative evolution
reveals that they grew relatively steadily from 2005 to 2024. The start of the BRI accompanies former FDI deployment and does not correlate with an absolute
change of the global volume of FDI in the metal sector. Geographic repartition of the FDI in the metal sector Paying attention to the geographic distribution of the
FDI in the metals sector, it becomes evident that China tends to prioritize investments in major mining countries. Over the entire time frame, the largest volumes of
FDI have been directed toward countries with a significant share in either the production or the reserves of one or more specific minerals. Based on the data of the
US Geological Survey, here are the current mineral profiles of the four countries with the highest volumes of Chinese FDI: Australia: is the world’s first producer of
iron ore, bauxite and lithium (USGS 2025). Chinese companies (Sinosteel, China Minmetals) mostly signed agreements on iron ore projects, and to a lesser extent, on
bauxite (Chinalco) and other metals, including lithium. For instance, the company Chengdu Tianqi acquired in 2012 the lithium hydroxide refinery of Kwinana located
in Western Australia, which provides “high-quality products to downstream customers alongside plants based in China” (Chengdu Tianqi 2025). Indonesia: is the
leading producer of nickel and has the largest nickel reserves in 2024 (USGS 2025). High grade nickel is largely used in NMC batteries for cathode chemistry using
nickel, manganese, cobalt and lithium (Harper et al. 2019; IEA 2022). Indonesia has attracted investments from over twenty Chinese companies, with deals exceeding
1 billion USD (for instance by: China Nickel Resources, and Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt) focusing on laterite-nickel ore operations with associated by-products, mainly
copper (Cu) and cobalt (Co). Through successive policies restricting nickel ore exports from 2009 to 2019, the Indonesian government compelled Chinese firms to
invest in the Indonesian downstream projects, especially refining and smelting activities (Camba et al. 2022). This is exemplified by the numerous investments of
Chinese firms in ferronickel industries in the Central Sulawesi Province, notably in the Morowali industrial park: Eternal Tsingshan Group successively invested 500
million USD in 2015 and 400 million USD in 2018. Large investments were also made by Jiangsu Delong, Shandong, and several other Chinese companies in the steel
industry. The Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC): is the world's leading cobalt producer and holds around half of the world’s cobalt reserves and the world’s
second-largest copper producer with significant reserves (USGS 2025). Cobalt has been used for superalloys but is also a key material for low-nickel NMC batteries
(IEA 2022). China Molybdenum has already invested some 4.4 billion USD in the country, and other companies such as China Nonferrous Metal Mining, Zijin Mining
and Zhejiang Huayou Cobalt have operations there, especially on the large the large deposits near the Kolwezi and Tenke Fugurume deposits. Peru: is the world's
third-largest copper producer and first in terms of reserves, it is also the first producer of arsenic and the second producer of silver (USGS 2025). Copper is a
keystone component of electricity-based technologies as a result of its unique electrical conductivity properties and relatively low cost. Among the Chinese
companies present in the country, some have made major investments in copper operations, such as China Minmetals, which is the leader with nearly 7 billion USD
in investments already made, and Chinalco with around 3 billion USD. The distinction between pre-BRI (2005–2013) and BRI period (2014–2024) shows a significant
structural change in the geographic distribution of the FDI in the metal sectors. During the period from 2005 to 2013, Australia was the leading destination capturing
a substantial 33% share over the period amounting to approximately 35 billion USD, as shown on the Fig. 4. These investments were primarily directed toward
mining activities in iron ore, and to a lesser extent, on bauxite, copper and other metals. Chinese FDI flowed to a lesser extent into other mining countries: the DRC
(5%), Brazil (5%), Russia (5%) Peru (5%) and more. A significant shift has occurred since 2014 in the geographical distribution of these investments. While Australia
was the first destination for FDI in the period 2005–2013, it lost its leading place during the period 2014–2024. We observed an eightfold reduction in the amount of
FDI (from 35.2 billion USD to 4.2 billion USD) and its share dropped from 33 to 4% in the last period. The three first recipients since 2014 have been Indonesia (16%),
the DRC (10%), Peru (9%), followed by Canada (4%), Guinea (4%) and others. FDI flows to Indonesia increased fivefold, rising from 3.66 to 20.6 billion USD. Similarly,
investments flows to DRC and Peru more than doubled during this period. Several other countries, including Guinea, Canada, Chile, and Argentina, also experienced
substantial increases in share and absolute value. The panel of recipients in the second period is thus more diversified and focused on countries offering mineral
opportunities in specific metals that are critical to new technologies manufacturing. Indeed, this change in the geographic distribution between the two periods
(2005–2013 and 2014–2023) corresponds to a shift from base and steel metals to battery raw materials and advanced materials. This shift aligns with China's
industrial transition from an economic growth strategy focused on hard infrastructure development to one driven by high-tech innovation. A group of countries
accounts for a relatively smaller share of total Chinese FDI in the metal sector (approximately 4–5%) compared to the main recipients mentioned above. Although
these countries are not among the top destinations, their mineral profile exhibit similarities, particularly in terms of specific metal opportunities related to
production and reserves: Canada: accounts for a significant share of global production and reserves for various metals, including nickel, copper, cobalt and iron ore
(USGS 2025), and the country hosts many multinational mining companies. Several acquisitions were made by Chinese companies: Zijin acquired the Canadian
company Continental Gold for 1 billion USD in 2019 (which is the leading large-scale gold company in Colombia, notably with the Buriticá project). Zijin also acquired
the Canadian company Nevsun Resources Ltd for around 1.5 billion USD in 2018. Guinea: ranks third in bauxite production and possesses the world’s largest reserves
of this mineral, which is essential for aluminium production (USGS 2025). The firms: Chinalco, Shandong Weiqiao and China Nonferrous (among others) are Beijing’s
spearheads in Guinea, with more than 4 billion USD already invested in the country's. Chile: is the world’s leading producer of copper and holds the largest copper
reserves (USGS 2025). Additionally, it is also the second-largest producer of lithium and accounts for 33% of global reserves. In 2018, Chengdu Tianqi acquired 24%
stakes in the company SQM, one of the largest lithium producers owned by the Canadian company Nutrien, for more than 4 billion USD (AEI 2025). These results
highlight a more diversified allocation of investments toward a broader range of countries with specific market share on advanced materials during 2014–2024,
compared to the 2005–2013 period. The analysis of this dataset suggests that Chinese firms have diversified their investments by targeting countries with significant
market shares in specific minerals crucial for supporting China's high-tech industries development. Interlinked investments in the transport, energy and metal sectors
If this quantitative analysis has focused on the metal sector so far, these targeted investments must be understood as part of
larger investment frameworks aimed at reinforcing interdependence links. In the long run, these links
will either be necessary for the extraction and export of mineral resources to China or increase the
influence of Chinese firms in the recipient countries, thereby enhancing their likelihood of accessing
major opportunities in the metal sector. Indeed, mineral deposits development requires developing
many sectors beyond the metal sector: roads to access mineral deposits in remote areas, power plants
to supply the infrastructures, labour force, etc. The Chinese FDI in the metal sector must therefore be
understood as the piece of a larger puzzle, which is time consistent with the investments in other
sectors, especially energy and transport as shown on Fig. 5. This multi-sectoral approach is a key feature of the global Chinese supply security
strategy, with energy, transport, and metal accounting for around two-third of total FDI over the studied period. This integrated vision of

resources exploitation enables China to present itself as an unmissable strategic partner which will
help to modernise the overall infrastructure networks, ultimately ensuring the minerals to find their
way toward the Chinese downstream sector. In the next section, we provide a coupled quantitative–qualitative analysis of this
phenomenon in Peru. Peru was the third largest receiver of Chinese FDI in the metal sector between 2014–2024 and is one of the largest mineral producers in South
America. The choice of Peru is consistent in the context of geopolitical influence: Latin America falls within the U.S. sphere of influence under the Monroe Doctrine
and is a key arena for geoeconomic competition between the U.S. and China, unlike the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Indonesia (which has officially joined
the BRICS in January 2025). At least two, and possibly three competing visions currently shape Peru’s geopolitical identity: an American vision (aligned with the
Monroe Doctrine and trans-American ideals), a Trans-Pacific vision (with ties to East Asia, including China and Japan), and a European/Hispanic heritage (economic
ties often steaming from historical and cultural proximity) (Chonn Ching and Camba 2023; Guerra-Barón 2022; Kellner and Wintgens 2021; Regalado-Pezúa and
Macedo 2022). Within this framework, the next section provides factual insights into projects that reflect this Trans-Pacific vision, materialized through Chinese
foreign direct investment (FDI) in key metal, energy, and transport infrastructures, and highlights the political significance of these projects for both parties. Chinese
interlinked investments in critical infrastructure: the case of Peru South America is home to several giant mining countries,
including Peru, Brazil, and Chile. Our analysis of the AEI dataset shows that Peru became a primary
destination for Chinese FDI in the metal sector after 2013. Significant investments were made,
particularly in large copper mining projects such as the Rio Blanco, Toromocho, and Las Bambas deposits, as well as in critical transport
(e.g., Chancay port) and energy infrastructures (e.g., Chaglla dam and Lima’s electricity producers). These acquisitions reflect growing cooperation dynamics between
China and Peru, embodied by the visit of Xi Jinping to Peru in November 2024, for the inauguration of the large-capacity Chancay Port on the west-Pacific coastline,
in which the Chinese firm COSCO is the first shareholder (60%). We found a substantial increase in exports of copper ores and concentrates (HS code 260300) from
Peru to China between 2003 and 2022, as shown on Fig. 6. Exports grew both in absolute weight (0.387 Mt in 2003 versus 5.834 Mt in 2022) and in share (37% in
2003 versus 77% in 2022) with the most significant increases occurring after 2010. The trend reflects a growing supply of copper concentrates toward China over the
studied period, which coincides with the Chinese FDI increases in Peru and the growing needs induced by the development of China's domestic high-tech industry.
Transport: the port of Chancay Located on the Peruvian Pacific coastline, the Port of Chancay was constructed by the public Chinese firm COSCO Shipping (60%) in
collaboration with the Peruvian company Volcan (40%) (COSCO 2024). The Chancay port is part of the BRI framework, aiming to enhance transport opportunities and
trade in South America. COSCO successively invested USD 230 million and USD 780 million in 2019 and 2020 (AEI 2025). The Port of Chancay boasts an advantageous
geographic location, situated approximately 60 km north of Lima. A 1.8-km tunnel connects the port to the Pan-American Highway, facilitating transportation of
commodities, including mineral commodities, from the hinterland to the coastline. The port has a substantial export capacity, with a throughput of 6 million tons of
bulk cargo, and 160,000 vehicles annually and has multi-purpose storage and logistics areas. The company announced that the transit time from Peru to Shanghai by
container has been reduced to 23 days (COSCO 2025). The construction of the port generated 8,000 direct employment opportunities in Peru according to the
company, representing a significant social dependence to this infrastructure regarding labour. The port was inaugurated on November 14, 2024, by Chinese President
Xi Jinping and Peruvian President Dina Boluarte, reflecting the political significance of this project for both parties. Energy: Chaglla dam and Lima's electricity
producers In recent years, Chinese SOEs also acquired key infrastructures of Peru's energy sector. In 2018, China Three Gorges Corporation acquired the Chaglla dam,
which is Peru's third-largest hydroelectric dam, from the Brazilian group Odebrecht for USD 1.39 billion (AEI 2025). With an installed capacity of 456 MW, the Chaglla
hydroelectric dam supplies steady and pilotable power to Peru, meeting approximately 5% of the country's electricity production (China Three Gorges Corporation
2024a). Additionally, China Three Gorges Project Corporation and China Southern Power Grid International separately acquired two firms that generate and supply
electricity to Lima. In 2019, China Yangtze Power International, a subsidiary of China Three Gorges Project Corporation, purchased Luz del Sur for USD 3.59 billion
and in 2023, China Southern Power Grid International acquired Enel for USD 2.9 billion (China Three Gorges Corporation 2024b). These infrastructures are critical to
Peru’s energy security. Metal: copper mines on target Peru was the world's second-largest copper producer and first in terms of reserves in 2024. It was also the first
producer of arsenic and the second producer of silver (USGS 2025). Significant investments have been made in Peru's mining sector by Chinese firms. Our
quantitative analysis shows that a first deal occurred in 2007 when Zijin Mining, in partnership with China Nonferrous Metal Mining and Xiamen C&D, acquired the
Rio Blanco copper-molybdenum mine from the Peruvian company Moterrico Metals (Zijin Mining 2022). Another significant deal occurred in 2007, when the Chinese
public company Chinalco acquired Peru Copper Inc. for around USD 800 million. Chinalco made substantial further investments focusing on the large copper mining
project Toromocho (Chinalco 2025). Investments were made in 2008 and 2018 to develop the Toromocho mining project, reaching a total of USD 3.5 billion (Daly
2018). Currently, Toromocho’s copper concentrate processing capacity is estimated around 157 kt per day corresponding to a refined copper output to 300 kt per
year (1.5% of world's total). The largest investment took place in April 2014 when China Minmetals, Guoxin, and CITIC acquired the Las Bambas copper mine for USD
6.95 billion. This acquisition was the largest overseas deal ever made by a Chinese company in the metal sector abroad at that time. Since then, the mine has
produced over 2.4 million metric tons of copper from copper concentrates (China Minmetals 2014; China Minmetals 2024). These projects contributed
to the expansion of Peru’s copper ore and concentrates production in the last twenty years, which has
mostly been exported to China, as revealed by our analysis of the dataset from Resources Trade Earth presented on Fig. 6. This trend confirms that
these investments were coherent with a growing supply of copper ores and concentrates to the Chinese domestic
refineries and downstream industries. Overall, our quantitative and qualitative analysis suggests that
the linkages induced by Chinese firms'(mostly state-owned) acquisition of Peru's critical infrastructure
through FDI are consistent with the securitization strategy of the supply of raw materials (copper in this
case) needed to modernize the Chinese high-tech manufacturing sector. More broadly, these acquisitions reflect China's
growing economic and political influence in South America on mineral resources, aligning with similar FDI patterns observed in Argentina and Chile, though these are
not explored in this paper.

China has established dominance over the global rare-earth supply chain, from mining and refining to
magnet production, giving China significant economic and geopolitical leverage The US and its allies are
highly dependent on China for critical rare-earth materials, creating supply chain vulnerabilities,
economic risks, and potential threats to national security Efforts by the US to diversify supply chains and reduce reliance on China are underway but face
challenges including environmental regulations, technical barriers, and cost inefficiencies Rare earths are a group of 17 metals: the 15 lanthanides, along with
scandium, and yttrium. These metals possess unique magnetic and electronic properties, making them essential
for manufacturing powerful magnets used in a wide range of technology-driven products, including
smartphones, electric vehicles, and F-35 fighter jets. Given their use cases, global demand for these
minerals is expected to increase by more than 60% by 2040, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA).1 Despite their name,
rare earths are not rare in geological terms; in fact, elements like cerium and neodymium are more abundant in the Earth’s crust than gold or silver. However, they
are considered “rare” because they are seldom found in concentrated, economically viable deposits, making them
difficult and costly to mine and refine. Certain rare earths are often extracted as byproducts of radioactive mining
operations for uranium and thorium. Although large rare-earth deposits do exist in developed countries like the US and Australia, production
remains limited due to environmental concerns and technical challenges.Such limitations have
provided China, a largely centrally planned economy, with significant advantages. By leveraging
decades of government subsidies and strategic investment to support both mining and downstream
processing, China has amassed enormous control over the global rare-earth value chain, spanning
production, separation, and magnet fabrication. Although rare-earth elements play a critical role in
both military and industrial applications, the global market for these materials remains relatively small, with annual sales totaling less than
$4 billion.2 Nevertheless, their ubiquitous application has allowed China to wield outsized influence on the global stage. For the US and its allies, this dependence on
a single supplier for such essential resources poses significant risks, including supply chain vulnerabilities, economic instability, and threats to national security. Even
official data only partially reflects the extent of the US’s reliance on China, in our view. According to the IEA, while China accounted for “only”
about 70% of global rare-earth extraction in 2024, it controls more than 90% of the downstream value
chain, including oxide separation, metal refining, and magnet production.3 Notably, China holds a
monopoly on the separation of two rare-earth elements—dysprosium and terbium—critical for
manufacturing permanent magnets capable of withstanding high temperatures. These magnets are key components in F-35 jet actuators, Tesla’s humanoid
robots, and Virginia-class submarines. In the spring of 2025, Corporate America learned this lesson the hard way. After President Trump
announced reciprocal tariffs on China, Beijing retaliated in April by restricting exports of seven critical
elements, as well as magnets made with them. The situation escalated further in October when
additional bans on more metals were imposed, along with the introduction of extraterritorial licensing
mandates. The abrupt disruption in the supply of rare-earth magnets led to the temporary closure of
Ford’s Chicago factory for weeks in June and left many US companies scrambling to secure supplies.
However, tensions paused with the Kuala Lumpur Accord late that month, as both sides agreed to suspend the fresh strangulations for one year. While the ceasefire
halts the immediate crisis, the rare-earth saga remains unsolved. In this paper, we will examine the origin of China’s dominance in rare earths, its economic and
strategic impacts, and the broader implications for investments. CHINA’S VAST DISCOVERY China’s rise to dominance in the rare-earth
market began with massive overproduction, flooding global markets with cheap products and driving
out competitors. Over time, Beijing shifted to a more strategic approach: They focused on conserving domestic resources and consolidating industry
power, ultimately enabling them to wield rare earths as a geopolitical weapon. In the 1950s, while exploring the Bayan Obo mine in the Inner Mongolia Autonomous
Region, Chinese geologists made a groundbreaking discovery of vast reserves of rare-earth elements. Initially believed to be an iron deposit, Bayan Obo was revealed
to contain the world’s largest rare-earth reserves, exceeding 100 million metric tons. This discovery marked a pivotal moment for China, prompting then-leader Deng

Xiaoping to famously declare in 1992, “The Middle East has oil; China has rare earths.” Supported by strong policy and government
resources, production and operations at the Bayan Obo mine accelerated rapidly.5 In 1994, as part of sweeping economic reforms, China
introduced a value-added tax (VAT) policy that offered rare-earth exporters rebates of up to 17%. These generous VAT rebates, combined with relaxed environmental
regulations and low labor costs, created powerful incentives for domestic producers to ramp up output. The policy not only benefited large state-owned enterprises
(SOEs) but also encouraged smaller and unregulated producers to exploit the tax rebates, further driving production.6 As a result, China’s rare-earth
production surged by nearly 500% from 1985 to 1995, exceeding the rest of the world combined.7 THE
RETREAT BY THE WEST China’s dominance in rare-earth production was also aided by past policy shifts
