Identity is made by machinic enslavement. They fail to understand the core questions of identity Galloway & Culp 2016
Alexander R. Galloway, Professor of Media, Culture, and Communication at New York University, and Andrew Culp, Visiting Assistant Professor of Rhetoric Studies at Whitman College, “Ending the World as We Know It: An Interview with Andrew Culp,” boundary 2, June 29, 2016. https://www.boundary2.org/2016/06/ending-the-world-as-we-know-it-an-interview-with-andrew-culp/, accessed December 7th, 2025. //Brashear
Deleuze and Guattari are certainly not allergic to technology. My favorite quote actually comes from the Foucault book in which Deleuze says that “<mark>technology is social before it is technical</mark>” (6). The lesson we can draw from this is that <mark>every social formation draws out</mark> different capacities <mark>from</mark> any given <mark>tech</mark>nology. <mark>An easy example is</mark> from the <mark>nomads</mark> Deleuze loved so much. <mark>Anarcho-primitivists</mark> <mark>speculate that humans learn oppression with</mark> the domestication of animals and settled <mark>agriculture</mark> during the Neolithic Revolution. Diverging from the narrative, Deleuze celebrates the horse people of the Eurasian steppe described by Arnold Toynbee. Threatened by forces that would require them to change their habitat, Toynbee says, they instead chose to change their habits. <mark>The</mark> subsequent <mark>domestication of the horse did not sew the seeds of the state</mark>, which was actually done by those who migrated from the steppes after the last Ice Age to begin wet rice cultivation in alluvial valleys (for more, see James C Scott’s The Art of Not Being Governed). <mark>On the contrary</mark>, the new relationship between men and horses <mark>allowed</mark> <mark>nomadism to achieve a higher speed</mark>, which was <mark>necessary</mark> <mark>to evade</mark> the raiding-and-trading used by padi-<mark>states</mark> to secure the massive foreign labor needed for rice farming. This is why the nomad is “he who does not move” and not a migrant (A Thousand Plateaus, 381). Accelerationism attempts to overcome the capitalist opposition of human and machine through the demand for full automation. As such, it peddles in technological Proudhonism that believes one can select what is good about technology and just delete what is bad. The Marxist retort is that development proceeds by its bad side. So instead of flashy things like self-driving cars, the real dot-communist question is: how will Amazon automate the tedious, low-paying jobs that computers are no good at? What happens to the data entry clerks, abusive-content managers, or help desk technicians? Until it figures out who will empty the recycle bin, accelerationism is only a socialism of the creative class. The machinic is more than just machines–it approaches technology as a question of organization. The term is first used by Guattari in a 1968 paper titled “Machine and Structure” that he presented to Lacan’s Freudian School of Paris, a paper that would jumpstart his collaboration with Deleuze. He argues for favoring machine to structure. <mark>Structures transform parts</mark> of a whole <mark>by</mark> exchanging or <mark>substituting particularities</mark> <mark>so that every part shares in a general form</mark> (in other words, the production of isomorphism). An easy political example is the Leninist Party, which mediates the particularized private interests to form them into the general will of a class. <mark>Machines</mark> instead <mark>treat the relationship</mark> between things <mark>as a problem of communication</mark>. <mark>The result is</mark> the “<mark>control</mark> and communication” of Norbert Wiener’s cybernetics, which connects distinct things in a circuit instead of <mark>implanting a general logic</mark>. The word “machine” never really caught on but the concept has made inroads in the social sciences, where actor-network theory, game theory, behaviorism, systems theory, and other cybernetic approaches have gained acceptance. <mark>Structure or machine</mark>, each <mark>engenders a different type of subjectivity</mark>, <mark>and</mark> each realizes <mark>a different model of communication</mark>. The two are found in A Thousand Plateaus, where Deleuze and Guattari note two different types of state subject formation: social subjection and machinic enslavement (456-460). While it only takes up a few short pages, the distinction is essential to Bernard Stiegler’s work and has been expertly elaborated by Maurizio Lazzarato in the book Signs and Machines. We are all familiar with molar social subjection synonymous with “<mark>agency</mark>”–it <mark>is</mark> the power that results from <mark>individuals bridging the gap between themselves and broader structures</mark> of representation, social roles, and institutional demands. This subjectivity is well outlined by Lacanians and other theorists of the linguistic turn (Virno, Rancière, Butler, Agamben). <mark>Missing from their accounts is machinic enslavement</mark>, which <mark>treats people as</mark> simply <mark>cogs in the machine</mark>. Such <mark>subjectivity is largely overlooked because it bypasses existential questions of</mark> recognition or self-<mark>identity</mark>. This is <mark>because machinic enslavement operates at the level of </mark>the infra-social or <mark>pre-individual</mark> through the molecular operators of unindividuated affects, sensations, desires not assigned to a subject. Offering a concrete example, Deleuze and Guattari reference Mumford’s megamachines of surplus societies that create huge landworks by treating humans as mere constituent parts. <mark>Capitalism revived the megamachine</mark> in the sixteenth century, and more recently, <mark>we have entered</mark> the “third age” of <mark>enslavement</mark> marked <mark>by</mark> the development of cybernetic and <mark>info</mark>rmational <mark>machines</mark>. In place of the pyramids are technical machines that use humans at places in technical circuits where computers are incapable or too costly, e.g. Amazon’s Mechanical Turk. I should also clarify that not all machines are bad. Rather, <mark>Dark Deleuze</mark> <mark>only trusts</mark> one kind of machine, <mark>the war machine</mark>. And war machines <mark>follow a single trajectory–a line of flight out of this world</mark>. A major task of the war machine conveniently aligns with my politics of techno-anarchism: to blow apart the networks of communication created by the state.

Capitalism relies on their revolutionary movements to continue advancing the system Culp 2016
Andrew Culp, Visiting Assistant Professor of Emerging Media and Communication, University of Texas at Dallas, Dark Deleuze, University of Minnesota Press, 2016, pp. 33–34. https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/andrew-culp-dark-deleuze, accessed December 7th, 2025. //Brashear
<mark>The schizo is dead!</mark> Long live the schizo! <mark>Schizo culture appealed to a society seized by postwar consumer boredom</mark>. “Can’t <mark>we produce</mark> something other than toasters and cars? How about <mark>free speech</mark>, free school, <mark>free love</mark>, free verse!” It is no exaggeration to say that the events of May 1968 were sparked by a Situationist intolerance for boredom (“boredom is always counter-revolutionary,” says Guy Debord; “Bad Old Days Will End,” 36). In the time since the 1972 publication of AntiOedipus, <mark>capitalism has embraced its schizophrenia through neoliberalism</mark>. <mark>The schizo has become the paraphilic obsession</mark> of Nietzsche’s last man. Its flood of more and more objects has subjects able to muster less and less desire, as seen in the Japanese Lost Decade of stagflation, when a torrent of perversions coincided with a suicide epidemic. The dominant feelings today are probably anxiety or depression (Plan C, “We Are All Very Anxious”). They are expressed as vulnerability in the pervasiveness of trauma, as a constant low-level distress, and through a generalization of contingency. <mark>Demonstrating the significance of this shift</mark>: “<mark>go play outside</mark>” <mark>is a breath of fresh air to the bored but fails</mark> to make the depressive budge. <mark>Neoliberalism turns</mark> the depressive into the paranoiac <mark>through a program of exposure</mark>, which <mark>unfolds the subject to</mark> reveal new surfaces to <mark>penetrate</mark>. Despite this, the negative project of the process of schizophrenia (“collapsing a filthy drainage pipe”) is as necessary as ever (AO, 341). But just as Lenin declared the revolutionary affirmation “All the power to the Soviets!” counterrevolutionary after a certain time, it is time to retire the slogan “Liberate the flows!” <mark>Militant discussions</mark> <mark>of</mark> <mark>infrastructure</mark>, <mark>blockage</mark>, <mark>and interruption are refreshing</mark>—<mark>since</mark> the first “free” <mark>laborers threw a shoe in the machine</mark>, <mark>sabotage has been an important tactic of resistance</mark>. <mark>But</mark> with the elliptical dynamics of <mark>capitalism</mark>, which <mark>poses</mark> <mark>its own limits only to overcome</mark> them <mark>for a profit</mark>, <mark>interruptions</mark> <mark>cannot</mark> <mark>be an end unto themselves</mark> (230–31). Every economic system is “a system of interruptions” that works by breaking down (36–37, 151, 192). One needs to look behind the old social democratic criticism of productivism, “even pollution, cigarettes, prisons, logging, napalm, and nuclear warheads are counted in the Gross Domestic Production,” to see why (Kennedy, Remarks at the University of Kansas). Antiproduction, which prevents specific realizations of value in a systematic way, is “at the heart of production itself, and conditioning this production” (235). Potlatch and ritualized warfare are indigenous means of antiproduction that prevent the hoarding that could lead to despotism (Maus, The Gift; Clastres, Society against the State). Aristocratic glorious expenditure made sure that everything was owed to the king (Bataille, “Notion of Expenditure”). Marx reminds us that <mark>capitalists</mark> <mark>dip</mark> <mark>into</mark> their own <mark>capital</mark> stock <mark>at the expense of expanded reproduction</mark>, but <mark>wasting</mark> <mark>money on</mark> the “<mark>political–military–industrial complex</mark>” <mark>guarantees</mark> <mark>the smooth advance of the system as a whole</mark> (235). What interruption is revolutionary? The mold was set by Marx, who proposed “expropriating the expropriators” (Capital, chapter 32). “Direct action at the point of production” would intervene in the apparatus of capture where the earth, activity, and objects are first coded by the state as territory, work, and money or decoded by capitalism as flows of land, labor, and capital (TP, 437–60). But if “societies are determined by their mode of anti-production (and not a mode of production),” then action should be taken at the points of capitalist antiproduction (D, 135). Extending this line of argumentation, the avant-garde taunts the world with a claim: “<mark>cap</mark>italism <mark>defeated traditional societies because</mark> <mark>it</mark> <mark>was</mark> <mark>more exciting</mark> than they were, <mark>but</mark> now there is <mark>something more exciting than capitalism</mark>, itself: <mark>its destruction</mark>” (Bernadette Corporation, Get Rid of Yourself). Though this position is condemned by Leninists as infantile leftism, it is the realization of Deleuze and Guattari’s critique of therapy culture—clinicians say that one matures out of the depressive position by learning an ambivalent balance of love and hate, which helps delay gratification (Joseph, “Projective Identification,” 99). But is that not the alienation of the worker from the fruits of his labor, Deleuze and Guattari protest, the fundamental separation of a desiring subject from her means of satisfaction (AO, 70–75)? Think of an old German rock song, “Macht Kaputt, Was Euch Kaputt Macht” by Ton Steine Scherben, an anarchocommunist band connected to the squatter scene and the Red Army Faction (before it went underground). As cheap as it sounds, perhaps the <mark>cure for depressive disinterest is the thrill of “destroying what destroys you.”</mark>
Refuse the 1AC as a thought war machine and conspire against this world. Radical pessimism renders the future unintelligible and inculcates a doomsday conspiracy to destroy the world. Culp 2016
Andrew Culp, Visiting Assistant Professor of Emerging Media and Communication, University of Texas at Dallas, Dark Deleuze, University of Minnesota Press, 2016, pp. 19–20. https://theanarchistlibrary.org/library/andrew-culp-dark-deleuze, accessed December 7th, 2025. //Brashear

<mark>The conspiracy against this world will be</mark> known <mark>through</mark> its <mark>war machines</mark>. <mark>A war machine is</mark> itself “<mark>a pure form of exteriority</mark>” <mark>that</mark> “<mark>explains nothing</mark>,” but there are plenty of stories to tell about them (TP, 354, 427). They are the heroes of A Thousand Plateaus—Kleist’s skull-crushing war machine, the migratory war machine that the Vandals used to sack Rome, the gun that Black Panther George Jackson grabs on the run, and the queer war machine that excretes a thousand tiny sexes. “<mark>Each time there is an operation against the state</mark>—insubordination, rioting, guerilla warfare, or revolution as an act—it can be said that <mark>a war machine has revived</mark>” (386). War machines are also the greatest villains of A Thousand Plateaus, making all other dangers “pale by comparison” (231)—<mark>there is</mark> the <mark>constant state appropriation of the war machine</mark> <mark>that subordinates war to its own aims</mark> (418), the folly of the commercial war machine (15), <mark>the paranoia of the fascist war machine</mark> (not the state army of totalitarianism) (230–31), <mark>and</mark>, worst of them all, <mark>the</mark> “worldwide <mark>war machine” of capitalism</mark>, “whose organization exceeds the State apparatus and passes into energy, military–industrial, and multinational complexes” to wage peace on the whole world (387, 419–21, 467). “<mark>Make thought a war machine</mark>,” Deleuze and Guattari insist. “Place thought in an immediate relation with the outside, with the forces of the outside” (TP, 376–77). <mark>Two important inventions follow: speed and secrecy</mark>. These are the affects of the war machine, <mark>its weapons of war</mark>, <mark>which “transpierce the body like arrows</mark>” (356, 394). <mark>The resulting violence is not</mark> so vulgar as <mark>to encourage</mark> blow-by-blow bloodletting or a once-and-for-all <mark>immediate killing but institutes an economy of violence whose hatred is unlimited and therefore durable</mark>. <mark>The war machine</mark> engages in war along two poles: one <mark>forms a line of destruction</mark> “prolongable to the limits of the universe,” <mark>while the other draws a line of flight</mark> for the “composition of a smooth space and of the movement of people in that space” (422). Deleuze and Guattari would prefer to promote the connectivist line by saying they “make war only on the condition that they simultaneously create something else” (423). But today, that path leads to collusion with capitalism’s drive toward creative destruction (Schumpeter, Capitalism, Socialism, Democracy, 87). This is certainly not lost on those in Silicon Valley who spread the mantra of “disruptive innovation.” We can thus take heed of Deleuze and Guattari’s warning against treating terms as having “an irresistible revolutionary calling” (387). It is time to accept Nietzsche’s invitation to philosophize with a hammer, rendered here in the voice of Krishna: “<mark>I am become Time, the destroyer of worlds</mark>.” <mark>We must find an appetite for destruction</mark> that does not betray Deleuze and Guattari’s “abolitionist dream.” This takes the “progressive, anxiety-ridden revelation” that <mark>destroying worlds is</mark> just another way of “<mark>smashing capitalism</mark>, of redefining socialism, of constituting <mark>a war machine capable of countering the world war machine</mark> by other means” (385, 417, 372). Make the whole world stand still. Indeed, it may be the only way to think the present in any significant sense. To be clear: the suspension of the world is not a hunt for its conditions of reproduction or a meditative “rhapsody of sensations” (DR, 56). It is thought that treats the world as if struck by an unspecified disaster, where the best friends you have left are your own ideas. This is not the banal disaster movie, whose ambitions are usually limited to teaching us what are the bare essentials to survive. Writing the disaster is how we break free from the stifling perpetual present, for the present carries with itself a suffocating urgency. The present imposes material limits. To it, the past and the future are the empty form of time, and they must endure the complications of having a body to become part of the present (LS, 146–47, 165). <mark>The past and the future exist</mark> in their own right only <mark>through representation</mark>—the former in history as the present memorialization of things passed and <mark>the latter</mark> in the yet to come <mark>as the projection of an image of the present</mark> (147). Such re-presentation is why the future appears with the distinct impression that “we have seen it all before” (Flaxman, Fabulation of Philosophy, 392). The productivist sees the event of thought as an eminently practical reorientation toward the present achieved while generating a new image of the future (WP, 58). In contrast, <mark>those learning to hate the world</mark> must <mark>short-circuit the “here and now” to play out the scene differently</mark>. While still being in this world, they turn away from it. This is the life of characters so agitated that they force the world to stand still—Dostoyevsky’s Idiot, the head of Kurosawa’s seven samurai (TR, 317–18). <mark>Against bleating urgency that</mark> “there a fire, there’s fire . . . I’ve got to go,” they insist that <mark>everything could burn to the ground but nothing happens</mark>, <mark>because</mark> <mark>one must seek out a more urgent problem!</mark>

Asante ’25 [Richard Asante; Associate Professor of Comparative Politics, Development and African Studies at the University of Ghana, Legon; Joseph Asunka; CEO at Afrobarometer, a Pan-Africa Survey Research Organization; Abosede Omowumi Babatunde; Professor at the Centre for Peace and Strategic Studies, University of Ilorin, Nigeria; Joshua Eisenman; Professor of Politics at the Keough School of Global Affairs, University of Notre Dame; Francis Egu Lansana; Forward-Thinking Development Enthusiast with Technical Knowledge in Natural Resource Governance; Sanusha Naidu; Senior Research Associate with the Institute for Global Dialogue; Ogbonnaya Igwe; Chairperson of the Environmental Monitoring and Assessment Research Group; Youmanli Ouoba; Professor of Economics at the University of Thomas SANKARA; Boukary Sangare; Malian Anthropologist and Independent Consultant; October 2025; "Chinese mining in West Africa Responding to the environmental and social impacts"; Keough School of Global Affairs; https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/chinese-mining-in-west-africa.pdf; accessed 12-04-2025] leon
The environmental impacts of Chinese mining activities in the region are complex, evolving, and difficult to fully identify due to the blurred lines between formal and informal mining operations. While industrial-scale mining tends to be confined to specific areas and is somewhat better regulated, <mark>illegal</mark> small-scale <mark>mining operations</mark> are <mark>poorly monitored</mark> despite their environmental and social harms. Chinese actors’ deployment of heavy-duty machinery in small-scale mining has significantly damaged ecosystems, especially forests and watersheds. Ultimately, both large- and small-scale mining have become major sources of environmental degradation, enabled by noncompliance with mining regulations, weak regulatory enforcement, and a lack of accountability and prosecutions for legal violations in the mining sector.
The impacts of these activities are not only ecological but also social. The scale of Chinese mining operations with heavy machinery drive land dispossessions and displacement across the region.1 Chinese operators in illegal mining often convert farmland, forest, and rivers into mining sites—sometimes forcibly or through collusion with government, traditional leaders, and private landowners—eroding long-standing livelihoods rooted in agriculture and fishing.2 The loss of land for farming bananas, rice, potatoes, and other traditional crops undermines food security, fuels social tension, and stokes conflicts between local communities and Chinese miners.3
Weak regulatory enforcement in the mining sector stems from both systemic and political failures. Monitoring and evaluation mechanisms are underdeveloped, and existing legal frameworks fail to address the complex challenges posed by the involvement of Chinese nationals throughout the formal and informal mining sectors. Critically, there is a lack of political will to enforce existing laws—due in large part to the complicity of state and local authorities. High-level government officials, security personnel, and <mark>local</mark> leader<mark>s</mark> often <mark>benefit from illegal mining</mark> operations <mark>through bribery schemes</mark>, unlawful permits and licensing, <mark>and</mark> even <mark>ownership of mining equipment and operations</mark>.4 These activities undermine law enforcement, shielding both local and Chinese violators from prosecution. Compounding the problem is the widespread failure of Chinese mining companies to honor their corporate social responsibility (CSR) agreements. Although mining laws in many West African countries require community engagement and CSR implementation, Chinese company compliance is often absent or minimal, leading to public resentment and straining diplomatic relations between China and countries such as Ghana, Sierra Leone, Mali, and Nigeria.5 These problems, detailed in the following section, underscore the urgent need for national and regional policy responses that address regulatory gaps, strengthen enforcement mechanisms, and promote transparency and accountability in the mining sector.

Evidence The environmental impacts of Chinese mining activities are broad and far reaching, encompassing land degradation, ecosystem destruction, landslides, pollution deforestation, biodiversity loss, desertification, and exacerbating climate change. Chinese miners’ consistent flouting of environmental standards and safety measures has severely damaged forest reserves, farmlands, and water resources. Chinese mining in West Africa is commonly tied to illegal small-scale artisanal operations carried out in collusion with local and transnational actors, including criminal gangs.6 These activities have profound consequences across the region. Deforestation and soil erosion are commonplace across West African mining sites, and pollution of marine and freshwater bodies occurs at multiple stages of mining exploration. Small-scale gold mining causes extensive land degradation and harms water quality.7 In Nigeria, Liberia, Mali, and Ghana, Chinese financing has enabled local miners to excavate deeper with bulldozers and use harmful chemicals more extensively.8 Local mining workers and artisanal miners are exposed to hazardous mercury, cyanide, arsenic, and fluoride, often due to the lack of proper protective gear.9 Wastewater from mining containing these and other toxic heavy metals pollutes soil, groundwater, and rivers in Ghana, Burkina Faso, Nigeria, and Mali, where landslides at abandoned Chinese-owned artisanal mines have resulted in fatalities.10 In Nigeria, lead poisoning in mining sites in Zamfara State in 2010 resulted in about four hundred children falling ill or dying.11 Wastewater discharge onto farmlands from mining reduces crop yields and introduces toxins into the food chain, affecting human and animal health.12 Many mining communities have reported disproportionate cases of cancer, respiratory infections, waterborne diseases, reproductive disorders, skin disorders, asthma, spontaneous abortions, and birth defects.13 In coastal communities where mining is widespread, dredging boats discharge oil, fuel, and chemicals, obstruct riverbeds, cause erosion, and deform watercourses. Chinese mining operations have also released ballast water containing invasive species and toxic waste into marine ecosystems.14 Harmful runoff in mining sites poisons fish and reduces the size and quality of local stocks. Meanwhile, loss of farmland worsens poverty and undermines food security. In Ghana, the majority of small-scale “galamsey” gold mining sites are run by Chinese nationals, with more than fifty thousand Chinese nationals entering the country between 2008 and 2013 to engage in illegal gold mining. According to the Mankurom Cocoa Cooperative Farmers Association, small-scale gold mining has destroyed more than 100,000 acres of cocoa farmland.15 In Nigeria, mining-related disruptions have intensified farmer-herder conflicts due to declining access to water and grazing land.16 Chinese artisanal and illegal mining has also accelerated deforestation.17 In Ghana, bauxite mining in the Atewa Forest has devastated 5,000 hectares.18 In Nigeria, mining in forest zones has resulted in significant habitat loss. From 1975 to 2005, Bukuru, Plateau state, lost 63 percent of its forested area due to mining.19 These forests filter pollution and ensure a steady supply of water to important inland rivers such as the Falémé River (Senegal and Mali), Bagoé River (Côte d’Ivoire and Mali), Tano-Bia Basin (Ghana and Côte d’Ivoire), and the Volta Basin (Benin, Burkina Faso, Côte d’Ivoire, Ghana, Mali, and Togo).20

Chinese involvement in mining across West Africa is linked to organized crime and the proliferation of weapons.21 Transnational <mark>criminal networks linked to Chinese mining</mark> have destabilized mining regions and eroded trust in both local and national governance.22 Illegal mining has also become a funding source for armed groups and terrorists, such as Boko Haram and bandits in the Nigerian state of Zamfara.23 Illicit gold mining in the Central Sahel countries—Burkina Faso, Mali, and Niger—has been linked to transnational organized crime and instability. The illicit trade in Sierra Leonean diamonds and Malian gold has been tied to criminal syndicates and terrorism.24
While Chinese involvement in mining has supported some job creation, infrastructure development, and technology transfer in mining communities, these benefits are typically accompanied by poor working conditions, low wages, environmental hazards, and labor-rights violations.25 Even when communities negotiate infrastructure projects, the benefits rarely offset the environmental and social damage.26 Numerous cases of inadequate compensation, unpaid royalties, and poor infrastructure have fueled community tensions and intracommunity disputes.27 In Niger, for example, top Chinese oil executives were expelled for failing to adhere to the mining code requiring them to use local subcontractors and laborers for extraction.
Governance weaknesses exacerbate these challenges. Environmental and natural resource agencies across West Africa are underfunded, lack the independence needed to enforce regulations, and are plagued with corruption.28 Overlapping mandates and poor interagency communication hinder enforcement, while lax licensing systems allow illegal Chinese operations to flourish. Weak coordination between federal, provincial, and local authorities further enables illegal mining to thrive.29 Many countries also lack clear procedures governing the entire process from mineral exploration to mine decommissioning.30 In Nigeria, Mali, Côte d’Ivoire, and Liberia, small-scale mining licenses are often misused for large-scale operations and many Chinese firms operate without proper registration or licenses.31 In Ghana, where artisanal mining is restricted to nationals, Chinese actors are <mark>heavily involved through local intermediaries</mark>.32 Governments frequently issue licenses without consulting or compensating local landowners.33 In Nigeria and Ghana, vague and controversial land laws allow governments to appropriate land regardless of prior ownership, often keeping marginalized communities from receiving the economic benefits of mineral extraction.34

**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanisms—no magnitude, probability, timeframe, or scope comparisons between the kritik’s “machinic enslavement” impacts and the substantive contention’s conflict/environmental harms. There is no card that says “machinic enslavement outweighs because it structures all future violence” or “systemic conflict in Africa outweighs because X.” A filling card would be a tag like “Systemic violence of machinic enslavement precedes and magnitudes all discrete conflicts — Culp 2016 p. 385” placed at the top of the 2NC overview to frame the judge’s ballot.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards preempt likely Pro positions (e.g., “China’s reduction solves resource-curse conflict,” “Pro’s solvency mechanism works,” “Pro’s framework is util”). The kritik cards (Galloway & Culp, Culp 2016 pp. 33–34, Culp 2016 pp. 19–20) are purely descriptive/philosophical and do not interact with a specific Pro advocacy. An offensive spike would be a card tagged “Pro’s ‘substantial reduction’ rhetoric accelerates machinic enslavement by legitimizing state-managed extraction — Culp 2016 p. 230” placed in the 1NC or 2NC overview.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines answer anticipated responses such as “alt solves,” “reject the alt,” “framework: weigh the plan,” or “China can reduce extraction legally.” The Asante ’25 evidence describes status-quo problems but does not say “even if China reduces *legal* extraction, illegal channels persist” or “Pro’s model fails because enforcement is captured.” A defensive spike would be a card tagged “Reduction without enforcement reform expands illegal channels — Asante ’25 pp. 4–5” placed as a sub-point under Contention 1.

### Evidence quality
**Mixed — Galloway & Culp 2016; Culp 2016 (book); Asante ’25.**  
- *Philosophy cards:* Galloway & Culp 2016 (interview in *boundary 2*) and Culp 2016 (*Dark Deleuze*, Univ. Minnesota Press) are primary theoretical sources by the architects of “Dark Deleuze” / machinic enslavement — high author qualification for a kritik, but zero recency (9 years old) and no empirical dataset.  
- *Substantive card:* Asante ’25 (Atlantic Council report, Oct. 2025) is highly recent, multi-authored by professors/NGO heads, and cites specific events (Zamfara 2010, Atewa Forest, 50k+ Chinese nationals 2008–13). It wins an evidence-comparison tiebreak on the *empirical* layer but cannot be weighed against the kritik without embedded weighing (see above).

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case is pinned to two rigid, non-interchangeable paths: (1) the kritik (machinic enslavement/war machine) and (2) a single substantive scenario — Chinese illegal mining → conflict/masculine violence in West Africa. No second impact scenario (e.g., South America, Indo-Pacific), no alternative link (e.g., “legal extraction also fuels enslavement”), and no “even-if” fallback. A flexible case would add a second contention tagged “Chinese extraction in Latin America replicates machinic enslavement via debt-trap infrastructure — [card]” or an internal link card “Legal extraction licenses enable illegal expansion — Asante ’25 p. 32.”

### Warrant depth
**Partial.**  
- *Kritik cards:* Each tagline is a direct quote from the text (e.g., “Structure or machine, each engenders a different type of subjectivity”), so the warrant *is* the quoted passage — but the passages are dense theory, not explained in debate-accessible language. No analytic gloss translates “machinic enslavement operates at the infra-social” into a debate warrant.  
- *Asante ’25:* Taglines like “Chinese miners’ consistent flouting of environmental standards… severely damaged forest reserves” are claims; the card supplies empirical warrants (specific countries, chemicals, hectare counts). This half meets the standard; the kritik half does not.

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** The kritik (Con should “refuse the 1AC as a thought war machine” and “conspire against this world” via radical pessimism) and the substantive contention (Con defends *status-quo* Chinese extraction because reduction would worsen illegal channels) are contradictory. The kritik rejects all state-managed “solutions” and calls for destruction of the world-order; the substantive contention implicitly defends the current order as *less bad* than Pro’s reduction. No framing card reconciles “vote Con to accelerate the war machine” with “vote Con to avoid worse conflict in Africa.” A consistency card would be tagged “Radical pessimism doesn’t require defending the SQ — it rejects Pro’s reformist ontology — Culp 2016 p. 372” in the 2NC overview.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 3/10** — The case has one high-quality empirical card (Asante ’25) but lacks every strategic architectural element: no weighing, no spikes, no collapse options, no internal consistency between its two advocacies, and the kritik half is unwarranted for debate without analytic translation.
