Background: Afghanistan’s Rare Earth Elements (REEs) reserves have become a central point of geopolitical competition following the Taliban’s return to power in 2021. This study investigates the complex interplay between China’s growing dominance in Afghanistan’s mining sector, the failure of Western containment approaches, and the Taliban’s unconventional governance of these critical resources. Drawing on satellite imagery analysis, blockchain tracking of cryptocurrency transactions, and interviews with industry experts, the research uncovers how China has secured preferential access to REEs deposits through a “security-for-minerals” bargain with the Taliban. The study documents the Taliban’s paradoxical approach to resource governance, combining bureaucratic pragmatism with ideological constraints, resulting in revenue capture (15 % local allocations) and notable leakage (42 % lost to corruption). Findings highlight the emergence of REEs smuggling as a key funding source for regional militant groups, with an estimated $19 million annually flowing to ISIS-K Khorasan. The research contributes to theoretical debates on the “resource curse” in Islamic governance contexts and the evolving effectiveness of sanctions in an era of digital financialization. Practical implications include recommendations for tiered sanctions regimes and regional monitoring mechanisms to curb conflict financing while maintaining pressure for responsible mining practices. This study offers critical insights into how fragile states’ mineral wealth reshapes 21st-century geopolitical competition.
1. Introduction
The global transition to renewable energy and advanced technology has triggered an unprecedented scramble for rare earth elements (REEs) – those seventeen chemically similar minerals including neodymium, dysprosium, and lanthanum essential for everything from electric vehicle batteries to precision-guided munitions. As global demand for REEs surges, control over their supply chains is reshaping international alliances as well as national economy and security policies [1]. As nations race to secure these critical resources, Afghanistan has emerged as an unlikely but pivotal player in this new great game of mineral geopolitics. Afghanistan is considered as Saudi Arabia of rare earth elements specifically lithium. Afghanistan's mineral resources, which include lithium deposits that rival Bolivia's "lithium triangle," are valued between $1 and $3 trillion by the U.S. Geological Survey [2,3]. Yet this potential bounty remains largely untapped, caught between competing geopolitical interests, a pariah regime, and the persistent specter of conflict. This study examines Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals as both a strategic prize and a potential curse, offering new insights into resource politics in fragile states and the evolving dynamics of global mineral competition. The importance of this research stems from three converging global trends. First, the accelerating energy transition has dramatically increased demand for REEs, with global consumption projected to grow 300–1000 % by 2040 depending on the mineral [4]. Second, growing U.S.-China tensions have turned rare earth supply chains into a national security issue, particularly given China’s current dominance (controlling 80 % of global REEs production and 90 % of processing capacity). 2. Literature review
Afghanistan's unexplored mineral wealth, estimated to be worth between $1 and $3 trillion [2], has attracted more and more attention from around the world. These deposits are strategically important for advanced defense and green transition technologies, especially those involving lithium, neodymium, and dysprosium [7]. But their systematic development has been thwarted by the nation's tumultuous political past and ongoing security issues. Prospects for extraction were made more difficult by the Taliban's 2021 comeback, as the government attempts to profit from resources while negotiating sanctions and international isolation [8]. While Western companies are still apprehensive, China has become the most active foreign actor, using its dominance in the global supply chain to negotiate mining contracts in Afghanistan. This has heightened worries about Beijing's capacity to bolster its hold on vital minerals in weaker nations. Afghanistan's resources are viewed strategically by regional actors as well. India aims to balance China despite diplomatic and logistical challenges, while Pakistan portrays Afghan minerals as essential to the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor [9]. Similar to their neighbors in Central Asia, Iran looks for economic opportunities but is cautious about Taliban ideology spreading. Afghanistan's mineral industry is intertwined with overlapping geopolitical rivalries and precarious governance structures, as this regional competition highlights. The strategic importance of REEs cannot be overstated in today’s technology-driven world. These minerals are indispensable for manufacturing high-performance magnets, batteries, wind turbines, and military hardware, including missile guidance systems and stealth technologies. China currently dominates the global rare earth supply chain, controlling approximately 80 % of production and 90 % of processing capacity [10]. This near-monopoly has raised alarms in Western capitals, particularly as tensions between the U.S. and China escalate over trade, technology, and military supremacy. The growing demand for green energy solutions has further heightened the scramble for rare earths, as countries seek to secure supplies for their renewable energy transitions. Afghanistan’s untapped deposits, therefore represent a potential alternative source that could reduce reliance on China. However, exploiting these resources is fraught with challenges, including Afghanistan’s lack of infrastructure, ongoing insurgency, and the geopolitical maneuvering of global powers seeking to control or influence access to these critical minerals.
Afghanistan’s mineral wealth has been known for decades, but decades of conflict have prevented large-scale extraction. Soviet geologists first mapped the country’s mineral potential in the 1980s, and subsequent surveys by the U.S. Geological Survey and the Pentagon confirmed significant deposits of rare earth elements, lithium, copper, and iron ore [6]. Among the most notable sites are the Hajigak iron ore mine, which also contains rare earth elements worth an estimated $336 billion, and the Khanneshin carbonatite complex in Helmand Province, which is rich in lanthanum and cerium. Lithium deposits in Nuristan and Herat have also drawn comparisons to major reserves in Bolivia and Chile (Discovery [11]). Without using metaphorical analogies, geological surveys demonstrate Afghanistan's potential as a vital mineral supplier by confirming substantial lithium, neodymium, and rare earth deposits. Despite this potential, Afghanistan’s mining sector remains underdeveloped due to insecurity, corruption, and a lack of investment. The <mark>Taliban</mark>’s takeover in 2021 has added another layer of uncertainty, as the group <mark>seeks to monetize </mark>these <mark>resources while facing international sanctions</mark> and limited recognition. <mark>China has emerged as the most active</mark> foreign <mark>player</mark>, securing rights to the Mes Aynak copper mine and expressing interest in rare earth projects. The Mes Aynak ore body is located about 30 km southeast of Kabul and contains copper and cobalt deposits worth an estimated $102 billion [12]. China invested $630 million in non-financial direct investment in Afghanistan at the end of 2020, primarily in the area of mining (Global [13]). However, <mark>Western companies remain wary</mark> of engaging with the Taliban regime, leaving Afghanistan’s mineral sector in a state of suspended animation, caught between geopolitical rivalries and internal instability. The competition over Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals is a microcosm of the broader great-power rivalry between the United States and China. For China, securing access to these resources aligns with its Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and its long-term strategy to dominate global supply chains [15]. Chinese state-owned enterprises have already made significant investments in Afghanistan’s mining sector, viewing the country as a potential supplier of critical minerals to fuel China’s high-tech industries. However, Beijing faces significant risks, including security threats from groups like ISIS-K, which have targeted Chinese interests in the region. Additionally, international sanctions on the Taliban complicate financial transactions and deter broader investment. The United States, meanwhile, has adopted a more cautious approach, focusing on diversifying its rare earth supply chains through partnerships with allies like Australia and Canada rather than engaging directly with Afghanistan under Taliban rule. The mining sector in Afghanistan is controlled by the Taliban, and it is unclear where revenues end up [7]. Washington has also invested in research to develop alternative materials and recycling technologies to reduce its dependence on rare earths altogether. Russia, though not a major consumer of these minerals, has sought to position itself as a mediator in Afghanistan, leveraging its historical ties and security partnerships to maintain influence in the region. The recent visit to Kabul by Sergei Shoigu, secretary of Russia’s National Security Council and former defense minister, sends a clear signal to the West about which bloc has the most influence in the country [16]. This great-power competition underscores how Afghanistan’s mineral wealth has become entangled in broader geopolitical struggles, with each actor pursuing strategies that reflect their national interests and risk tolerance.
Regional dynamics further complicate the picture, as neighboring states jockey for influence over Afghanistan’s resources. <mark>Pakistan</mark>, which shares a long and porous border with Afghanistan, <mark>views the country’s mineral wealth as</mark> both <mark>an</mark> economic <mark>opportunity</mark> and a potential source <mark>of instability</mark> [9]. A stable Afghanistan is essential for the successful development of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor [17]. The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) could serve as a conduit for transporting Afghan minerals to global markets, but security concerns and political tensions with the Taliban regime pose significant challenges. India, meanwhile, has sought to counterbalance Chinese influence in Afghanistan but faces logistical and diplomatic hurdles, particularly given its strained relations with Pakistan. Iran, which has cultural and economic ties to western Afghanistan, may also seek to leverage its position to gain access to mining projects, particularly in regions with large Shia populations. Iran also secured chromite through barter in exchange for fuel imports (Discovery [11]). Central Asian states like Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, while they could potentially serve as transit routes for Afghan minerals, remain wary of the Taliban’s ideological alignment with militant groups in their own countries. These regional rivalries and alliances create a complex web of interests that will shape how Afghanistan’s mineral resources are developed or whether they remain untapped due to persistent instability and competing agendas.
The economic potential of Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals is immense, but so are the governance challenges that could prevent the country from capitalizing on this wealth. The “resource curse” theory, which posits that countries rich in natural resources often suffer from corruption, conflict, and economic stagnation, looms large over Afghanistan. The Taliban’s lack of international recognition, coupled with their limited administrative capacity, raises serious questions about whether they can manage the mining sector transparently and equitably. Corruption has long plagued Afghanistan’s extractive industries, with reports of illegal mining, smuggling, and revenue diversion under previous governments [18]. The <mark>Taliban’s reliance on mining revenues to fund their regime</mark> could exacerbate these issues, particularly, if they prioritize short-term gains over sustainable development. Additionally, unregulated mining poses significant environmental risks, including water contamination and habitat destruction, which <mark>could</mark> provoke local opposition and further <mark>destabilize the country</mark>. Because of the possibility of acid leaching, soil erosion, and toxic tailings contaminating essential water systems, environmental risks are especially serious. Such outcomes highlight the need for sustainable extraction frameworks and are comparable to those seen in mining regions of the DRC and Myanmar with lax regulations. Without robust governance mechanisms and international oversight, Afghanistan’s mineral wealth may fail to translate into broad-based economic growth, instead <mark>fueling conflict</mark> and inequality.

3.3. Limitations and mitigations This study further acknowledges several methodological and contextual limitations inherent in researching rare earth elements extraction in Afghanistan. A critical constraint stems from the Taliban regime’s opacity, which restricts access to reliable mining production data, revenue figures, and official policies [20]. Government reports from the pre-2021 era may be outdated, while current Taliban disclosures lack independent verification. To mitigate this, the study cross-references multiple sources, including satellite imagery analysis by groups like Global Witness and confidential industry estimates from mining consultants. Security risks present another major limitation, as physical fieldwork in mining regions like Helmand remains hazardous due to insurgent activity and political sensitivities [21]. This constraint affects the direct observation of extraction practices and community impacts. As a workaround, the study relies on remote sensing data, verified local media reports, and interviews with NGO workers who maintain a limited operational presence. However, these secondary sources may miss nuanced on-ground realities. The research also faces potential selection bias in expert interviews. Many interviewees – particularly Western analysts and former Afghan officials – may harbor inherent skepticism toward Taliban governance [22]. Conversely, Taliban-affiliated sources might exaggerate the regime’s administrative capabilities. To balance these perspectives, the study includes input from neutral third-party actors like UN monitors and incorporates academic literature from Chinese and Russian scholars to diversify geopolitical viewpoints. Temporal limitations affect the study’s findings, given Afghanistan’s rapidly evolving political landscape. The Taliban’s mineral policies and international engagement strategies may shift abruptly due to economic pressures or factional disputes [23]. To address this, the research framework includes quarterly updates from monitoring groups like the Afghanistan Analysts Network, ensuring findings remain relevant despite fluid conditions. Lastly, the comparative case analysis carries inherent limitations as no perfect analogies exist for Afghanistan’s unique combination of Islamic governance, a sanctions regime, and mineral wealth. While the DRC and Myanmar provide useful parallels, differences in cultural, geographic, and historical contexts necessitate cautious interpretation [24]. The DRC illustrates how institutional weakness enabled warlord capture of mining rents, while Myanmar shows how sanctions fractured formal markets, fueling illicit extraction. By contrast, Afghanistan’s theocratic hybrid model differs by embedding religious authority in mining governance. The study explicitly acknowledges these disparities during analysis to avoid overgeneralization. Through these mitigation strategies – source triangulation, diversified sampling, real-time monitoring, and contextual awareness – the research maintains rigor despite Afghanistan’s challenging research environment. Future studies could enhance validity by incorporating advanced geospatial analysis or a partnership with Islamic scholars to interpret Taliban governance frameworks more accurately.
4. Research findings
This study reveals a complex interplay of geopolitical interests, economic ambitions, and governance challenges surrounding Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals resources. Three significant findings emerge from the research, each highlighting different dimensions of this multifaceted issue. First, the study demonstrates China’s strategic dominance in Afghanistan’s REMs sector [25]. Through state-backed investments and diplomatic maneuvering, China has secured preferential access to key mining sites, particularly lithium and rare earth deposits in Helmand and Nuristan provinces [26]. Our analysis of mining contracts shows that <mark>Chinese corporation’s control</mark> approximately <mark>78 % of</mark> current <mark>extraction projects</mark>, often through arrangements that exchange infrastructure development for mineral rights [27,28]. This dominance reflects China’s broader strategy to secure critical minerals supply chains, particularly for its growing electric vehicle and renewable energy industries. However, the research also identifies significant challenges in these operations, including security risks from local insurgent groups and growing resentment among Afghan communities who perceive limited benefits from these mining activities [29]. Second, the study uncovers the limitations of Western engagement strategies. Despite recognizing the strategic importance of Afghanistan’s REM resources, the United States and the European Union have struggled to develop effective policies since the Taliban’s return to power. <mark>Sanctions</mark> intended <mark>to pressure the Taliban</mark> regime have <mark>inadvertently strengthened China</mark>’s position <mark>by eliminating Western competitors from the Afghan mining sector</mark> [30]. Interviews with industry experts reveal that current U.S. efforts to develop alternative REM sources in Africa and South America will require at least 5–7 years to offset potential Afghan supplies. This finding suggests a significant gap in Western critical minerals strategy that could have long-term implications for technical and military competitiveness. Third, the research presents surprising insights into the Taliban’s governance of mineral resources. Contrary to expectations of chaotic exploitation, the Taliban has implemented a relatively structured approach to REEs management, establishing a dedicated Ministry of Mines staffed by both ideological loyalties and retained technical experts from the previous government. The regime has developed a revenue distribution system that allocates approximately 15 % of mining income to local communities, though enforcement remains inconsistent across provinces [31]. But there are significant human costs hidden behind this revenue allocation. Artisanal sites provide evidence of child involvement in risky extraction, forced labor practices, and hazardous working conditions that frequently result in injuries. Communities also report contaminated water sources and displacement, demonstrating how resource capture is accompanied by human exploitation. However, the study also identifies serious limitations in the Taliban’s capacity to oversee large-scale mining operations, including a lack of technical expertise in rare earth processing and vulnerability to corruption among regional commanders [7].
The research also highlights emerging security concerns related to REEs extraction. Approximately 23 % of artisanal mining sites in eastern Afghanistan show evidence of control by armed groups, including affiliates of Islamic State-Khorasan [32]. These groups reportedly generate between $15–20 million annually from illegal REEs extraction, <mark>creating</mark> a new <mark>funding</mark> stream <mark>that could sustain insurgent activities</mark>. Satellite imagery verified the physical growth of uncontrolled extraction areas in eastern Afghanistan, supporting these findings. Illicit REEs trade was directly linked to insurgent financing networks through parallel blockchain tracking, which revealed recurrent transfers through 17 anonymized wallets processing an estimated $19 million annually. This combination of digital forensics and remote sensing offers a validated basis for estimating the scope and methods of smuggling. This finding suggests that the geopolitics of Afghan REEs extends beyond great power competition to include non-state actors with destabilizing potential. Comparative analysis with other resource-rich conflict zones yields important insights. While Afghanistan shares characteristics with the Democratic Republic of Congo regarding the “resource curse,” its unique combination of Islamic governance, geopolitical significance, and international isolation creates distinct challenges [33]. The Taliban’s ideological constraints, for instance, limit partnerships with Western technical experts who could improve environmental and labor standards, while the country’s landlocked geography complicates export routes amid regional tensions. These findings collectively suggest that Afghanistan’s REEs resources are becoming a new focal point of geopolitical competition with far-reaching implications [34]. The research indicates that current approaches by all major stakeholders – China, the West, and the Taliban – contain significant flaws that may lead to suboptimal outcomes for both regional stability and global supply chain security [35]. These findings further contribute to broader discussions about resource governance in fragile states and the geopolitics of energy transition minerals.

Background Since 1996, conflict in eastern DRC has led to approximately six million deaths. The First Congo War (1996–1997) began in the wake of the 1994 Rwandan Genocide, during which ethnic Hutu extremists killed an estimated one million minority ethnic Tutsis and moderate Hutus in Rwanda (DRC’s neighbor to the east). During and following the genocide, nearly two million Hutu refugees crossed the Congolese border, mostly settling in refugee camps in the North Kivu and South Kivu provinces. A small subset of those Rwandans who entered DRC were Hutu extremists who began organizing militias within the Congo. Pressure intensified as Tutsi militias organized against the Hutu groups and as foreign powers began taking sides. Following the Rwandan Patriotic Front’s (RPF) victory against the genocidal Rwandan government, the new Tutsi-led government began its involvement in DRC (then known as The Republic of Zaire). Rwandan troops, under the leadership of President Paul Kagame, and Congo-based Tutsi militias with Rwandan backing launched an invasion of Zaire, which was ruled at the time by Dictator Mobutu Sese Seko. Kigali justified both efforts by arguing that Hutu groups in eastern DRC were still a threat to their Tutsi population and that the Mobutu regime was harboring Hutu extremists who had fled across the border. Rwanda waged the First Congo War against Zaire with the help of other African states (most significantly Uganda, but also Angola and Burundi), who had their own security concerns related to Mobutu’s support of rebel groups across the continent. The Rwandan coalition’s invasion was coordinated with the help of Zaire’s then-opposition leader, Laurent Kabila. Thousands died; some casualties were former Hutu militants and members of armed groups, but many were refugees and non-combatant Congolese in North and South Kivu, in eastern DRC. Methods of warfare were brutal, especially those employed by Rwandan soldiers and Tutsi groups. When Mobutu fled Kinshasa, the Kabila-Kagame coalition won the First Congo War in 1997. Kabila was installed as president of Zaire and changed the country’s name back to The Democratic Republic of Congo. In 1998, the Second Congo War broke out following the deterioration of relations between Kigali and Kinshasa. To diminish the impression that Rwanda held undue influence over the Congolese government, Kabila denied claims that Rwanda had been responsible for winning the war and placing him in power. Kabila also began removing ethnic Tutsis from his government and took measures to weaken Rwanda’s military presence in eastern DRC. By the late 1990s, it was becoming clear to the world that targeted campaigns against Hutu populations during the First Congo War (led mainly by Kagame’s army) amounted to war crimes. This growing international consensus reflected poorly on the fledgling Kabila regime. In a reversal of alliances, Kabila ordered all foreign troops out of the Congo and allowed Hutu armed groups to organize at the border once again. Rwanda responded by invading in 1998. Kigali’s stated aim was to create a zone in the DRC-Rwanda borderlands controlled by its own troops to create more distance from Hutu groups in eastern DRC. Congolese forces supported by Angola (which also reversed alliances following the ascent of Laurent Kabila), Namibia, and Zimbabwe fought the Rwandan, Ugandan, and Burundi militaries, as well as various rebel groups supported by Kigali and Kampala. Amidst the chaos of war, Laurent Kabila was assassinated in a 2001 coup attempt planned by his aides and guards. Those involved were imprisoned, and Kabila’s son, Joseph Kabila, took power. The Second Congo War was formally brought to a close under the junior Kabila in 2002. While estimates vary greatly, the death toll of the Second Congo War and the associated humanitarian disaster may have reached over three million people by 2004. Between 2002 and 2003, Rwanda, Uganda, and DRC began implementing peace agreements that authorized a transitional government in Kinshasa led by Joseph Kabila. Despite these agreements, the establishment of truth and reconciliation commissions, and a renewed UN peacekeeping force, unrest and clashes persisted in eastern DRC. Joseph Kabila was formally inaugurated following a long-awaited popular election in 2006. One of the most prominent rebel groups to emerge in the early 2000s was the March 23 Movement (M23), made up primarily of ethnic Tutsis. Could <mark>a</mark> Sino-U.S. <mark>Proxy War Escalate</mark>?
Scholars often compare the danger of interstate war between the United States and China today to the Peloponnesian War in ancient Greece. Just as the rise of Athens in the fifth century BCE provoked fear in Sparta, triggering the Peloponnesian War, so too today, the economic and military growth of China could spark alarm in the United States and heighten the risk of conflict — what Graham Allison calls the “<mark>Thucydides Trap</mark>.”164 It’s notable that the Peloponnesian War began when <mark>outside powers meddled in</mark> a foreign <mark>civil war</mark>. In 435 BCE, according to Thucydides, the city-state of Epidamnus fell prey to “internal conflicts lasting many years.”165 Competing factions appealed to outside actors for aid, which ultimately transformed a local civil war into a broader conflict between Athens and Sparta. In turn, the campaign between democratic Athens and oligarchic Sparta deepened domestic schisms throughout the Greek world, sparking further brutal civil wars, the erosion of norms, and the desecration of religious sites.166 “Civil strife inflicted many a terrible blow on the cities,” wrote Thucydides, “as always does and always will happen while human nature remains what it is.”167
Could a Sino-U.S. proxy war <mark>intensify into</mark> a larger conflict — or even an <mark>interstate war</mark> like in ancient Greece?168 The barriers to interstate war between the United States and China will likely prevent a proxy conflict from escalating to a full-blown conventional showdown. For one thing, most foreign civil wars do not threaten the core interests of the great powers. In addition, indirect intervention is often deniable, such that one, or both, great powers may prefer to ignore the other side’s intervention precisely to control the risk of a crisis spiraling into a broader, unwanted war.169
Nevertheless, low-level proxy war could potentially escalate in unexpected ways. Scholars have found that foreign backing for rebels is correlated with a heightened chance of militarized interstate disputes.170 Patrons often end up providing more aid in foreign civil wars than initially planned because of <mark>overconfidence</mark> about the allied faction’s capabilities. What begins as a minor proxy war can evolve into a much more dangerous situation if <mark>one patron</mark> decides to step up its involvement and <mark>intervene directly</mark> with ground forces.
A powerful driver of escalation is simply an aversion to losing. Psychologists have found that “losing hurts twice as bad as winning feels good,” and in the face of potential loss, <mark>actors are willing to gamble</mark> with an increased commitment in the hope of getting back to even.171 The Vietnam War illustrates the potential for a small-scale proxy conflict to intensify when neither side is willing to accept defeat. Another example is Cuban involvement in Angola. In the 1960s and 1970s, Havana began giving aid to the communist-aligned People’s Movement for the Liberation of Angola (MPLA) in the form of a relatively small training mission. This escalated into a large-scale and direct intervention with tens of thousands of Cuban ground troops deployed to check South African intervention and ward off the potential defeat of the MPLA.172 In the context of a potential U.S.-Chinese proxy war, <mark>Washington and Beijing may</mark> originally envision modestly backing a friendly regime or rebel group. However, if either country’s surrogate faces defeat, the patron may increase its support, including <mark>send</mark>ing in ground or air <mark>forces to</mark> avoid a strategic, moral, or reputational loss.173
Ignorance could <mark>also spur unanticipated escalation</mark>. In recent decades, the United States has struggled to manipulate foreign civil wars in Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, and elsewhere due to a lack of knowledge about local cultures, ethnic tensions, and languages. China is even more likely to blunder out of ignorance because Beijing’s diplomatic corps is not sufficiently trained for complex civil conflicts and China lacks a network of non-governmental organizations. China and the United States may end up <mark>stumbling in the fog</mark> of proxy war. Intervention in civil conflicts is often complex and covert, making it difficult to accurately perceive a patron’s degree of involvement, resolve, or influence over surrogates. This uncertainty <mark>can encourage</mark> a <mark>rival</mark> patron <mark>to</mark> engage in worst-case-scenario thinking and <mark>misperceive</mark> — <mark>and</mark> perhaps <mark>overreact</mark> to — the adversary’s involvement. In 2017, a U.S. jet shot down a Syrian aircraft that attacked the rebel Syrian Democratic Forces. Russia responded by suspending deconfliction protocols designed to avoid escalation, illustrating how intervention can evolve in unexpected and dangerous ways.174
In addition, surrogate forces may pursue an agenda that deviates from the patron’s preferences and potentially escalates the conflict.175 The Obama administration struggled to convince the Shia-dominated Nouri al Maliki government in Iraq to reach out to Iraqi Sunnis, worsening the Iraqi civil war.176 In such a situation, the local regime’s dependence on the United States does not translate into U.S. control because Washington cannot credibly threaten to end support: A collapse of the government would also be a loss for U.S. interests.177 Surrogates may also escalate a civil war by mistake, worsening an already tense situation or drawing global condemnation. In 2014, Ukrainian rebels shot down Malaysia Airlines Flight 17, using a surface-to-air missile provided, and subsequently recovered, by Russia. Ukrainian rebels may have fired the missile in error, believing the target to be a military aircraft.178 Similar events in a Sino-U.S. proxy war could spur a <mark>retaliatory response</mark>, particularly given loss aversion, cultural ignorance, and the fog of proxy war.
A U.S.-Chinese proxy war could also deepen the intrastate conflict itself. Scholars have found that external support tends to <mark>exacerbate civil wars</mark>.179 Take, for example, recent proxy wars in Libya, Syria, and Yemen, which have often <mark>spilled across borders</mark>. Conclusion
In 2007, Robert Gates said that unconventional wars were “the ones most likely to be fought in the years ahead.”181 This holds true for military competition between the United States and China. Strategic doctrine in both countries downplays intervention in foreign civil wars. And yet, <mark>any</mark> future military <mark>rivalry</mark> between China and America <mark>is likely to take</mark> the <mark>form of proxy</mark> war because of the systemwide dynamics that inhibit interstate war. A modern <mark>great power war</mark> could see nuclear weapons, bioweapons, autonomous weapons, and other destructive new technologies deployed on an unprecedented scale.
It would probably be the most destructive event in history, shattering our world. It could even threaten us with extinction.

We’ve come perilously close to just this kind of catastrophe before.¶ On October 27, 1962 — near the peak of the Cuban Missile Crisis — an American U-2 reconnaissance plane set out on a routine mission to the Arctic to collect data on Soviet nuclear tests. But, while flying near the North Pole, with the stars obscured by the northern lights, the pilot made a navigation error and strayed into Soviet airspace.1¶ Soviet commanders sent fighter jets to intercept the American plane. The jets were picked up by American radar operators and nuclear-armed F-102 fighters took off to protect the U-2.¶ Fortunately, the reconnaissance pilot realised his error with enough time to correct course before the Soviet and American fighters met. But the intrusion enraged Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev, who was already on high alert amidst the crisis in Cuba.¶ “What is this, a provocation?” Khrushchev wrote to US President John F. Kennedy. “One of your planes violates our frontier during this anxious time when everything has been put into combat readiness.”¶ If the U-2’s path had strayed further west, or the Soviet fighters had been fast enough to intercept it, this incident could have played out quite differently. Both the United States and the USSR had thousands of nuclear missiles ready to fire. Instead of a nearly-forgotten anecdote, the U-2 incident could have been a trigger for war, like the assassination of Franz Ferdinand.

Competition among the world’s most powerful countries shapes our world today. And whether it’s through future incidents like the lost U-2, or something else entirely, it’s plausible that it could escalate and lead to a major, devastating war.
Is there anything you can do to help avoid such a terrible outcome? It is, of course, difficult to imagine how any one individual can hope to influence such world-historical events. Even the most powerful world leaders often fail to predict the global consequences of their decisions.
But I think the likelihood and severity of great power war makes this among the most pressing problems of our time — and that some solutions could be impactful enough that working on them may be one of the highest-impact things to do with your career.
By taking action, I think we can create a future where the threat of great power war is a distant memory rather than an ever-present danger.

Deep-sea mining
Deep-sea mining involves the extraction of critical minerals – large deposits of which are in international waters – from the ocean floor. Debates on the topic intersect with those on clean energy (critical minerals required for the transition to green energy are found in the ocean floor), ocean health, deep-sea research, emerging technologies, the use of autonomous vehicles on the ocean floor, undersea intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance (ISR), and governance of the domain. There is currently no agreed framework regulating the commercial extraction of critical minerals from the ocean floor, though it is overseen by the International Seabed Authority (ISA) established under the UN Convention on the Law of the Seas (UNCLOS). There are also no restrictions on the exploration and extraction of seabed minerals in areas within a state’s sovereign jurisdiction.
The potential impacts of increasing commercial activity in the underwater domain – in terms of the economic advantage states might gain, or the longer-term consequences for priorities in underwater warfare – are something of an unknown. Given the unknown impacts, ongoing debates and lack of a common legal framework, many states support a moratorium on deep-sea mining. Canada, Mexico, New Zealand, Peru, Switzerland and the United Kingdom have supported the call for a moratorium. 25 countries have adopted a precautionary pause, while France has adopted a ban.
China, India, Japan, Russia and South Korea are among the countries that have not supported a moratorium and have signalled their intention to pursue deep-sea mining. So far, the ISA has awarded exploration contracts to 22 contractors, including three Chinese, two Russian and two Japanese companies. As shown by the China–Cook Islands MoU, countries with strategic interests in deep-sea mining have begun to forge ahead, in cooperation with like-minded partners when possible.
