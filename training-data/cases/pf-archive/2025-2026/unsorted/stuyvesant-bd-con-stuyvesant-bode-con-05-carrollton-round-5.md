Juana Lozano’s phone buzzes with more than a dozen
alerts each day: police stopping a truck with
construction workers, dark-windowed vans near a
shopping mall, a mother asking if it’s safe to drive
down a certain road. Lozano says the alerts are part
of her routine. She’s a community leader in Central
Florida and a member of a Telegram chat group called
“ICE Orlando y sus alrededores” (“ICE Orlando and
its surroundings”). The group has more than 500
users sharing warnings about police and immigration
authorities. It helps Lozano avoid trouble spots when
she occasionally drives mothers and others who have
no safe way to get around. “Most people in this group
are looking for safety, for themselves or others,”
said Lozano. “People stay in touch, and it’s the
community that shares and confirms the information.”
Across the country, a growing number of immigrants
and others at risk of arrest due to their legal status
are turning to mobile apps, and encrypted networks to
avoid encounters with law enforcement. Tools like
Coquí, StopICE.net, SignalSafe, PeopleOverPapers,
and ICEBlock are becoming lifelines for vulnerable
communities. These platforms offer real-time alerts,
maps, and crowdsourced tips that help users track

immigration activity nearby. For community activists
and developers these tools are seen as a way to
stand up for the rights and dignity of the most
vulnerable. “We agree that the situation is delicate,”
said Isaret Jeffers, a community activist who
defends the rights of immigrants and farmworkers in
Hillsborough and Pinellas counties. “That’s why those
apps, hotlines, and chat groups are important.” Coquí,
named after a small frog known for its loud warning
sound, shares a live map where users can pin, report
and see alerts about immigration activity. Markers
appear within 10 miles. Coquí sends alerts to users
who are within 3 miles of a reported marker. It uses
encryption for extra security. The location is never
saved or linked to the user’s name or identity. The
app has over 10,000 users.

government’s larger push to amass as much
information as possible about all of our lives. By

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case presents multiple distinct impact scenarios (immigrant safety, ICE abuse/surveillance, torture in detention, economic harm to US/Mexico) but contains no explicit comparative weighing mechanism — no "magnitude," "probability," "timeframe," or "scope" tags, no "weighing: X outweighs Y" arguments, and no pre-written framework for the back half to extend. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Weighing — Structural violence of deportation machine outweighs speculative security benefits — magnitude (millions affected) + irreversibility (family separation/death) + probability (empirically ongoing)" and would be placed as a standalone contention or as a tag on the Payan '25 evidence.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards preempt likely Aff positions such as "encryption enables terrorism/child exploitation," "lawful access prevents going dark," "judicial oversight prevents abuse," or "deportations target criminals only." The Petti '25 and US OLP '22 cards describe government *actions* and *justifications* but do not spike them — they function as link evidence, not preemption. An offensive spike would be a card tagged "Spike — 'Going dark' narrative empirically false: encryption backdoors create systemic vulnerability exploited by adversaries — see SolarWinds, Salt Typhoon" or "Spike — Judicial oversight fails: FISA court approval rate >99%, rubber-stamp for surveillance" placed before or within the surveillance contention.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines preempting specific Aff responses. The case does not include blocks like "No solvency — backdoors break encryption for everyone, not just ICE targets," "Turn — backdoors enable authoritarian surveillance the Aff claims to prevent," or "Non-unique — ICE already accesses data via third-party doctrine, subpoenas, Cellebrite." A defensive spike card would be tagged "Frontline — Aff solvency fails: lawful access mandates create universal keys that foreign intelligence/criminals exploit — see EU/UK backdoor debates" and placed in the contention addressing the mechanism of "lawful access."

### Evidence quality
**Tag: "And, deportations hurt Mexico's and the U.S. economy." — Payan '25 (Tony Payan, José Iván Rodríguez-Sánchez, March 26, 2025, Rice University's Baker Institute for Public Policy)** — This is the highest-quality evidence in the doc: recent (2025), institutional (Baker Institute), specific quantitative claims (GDP reduction 2.6–6.2%, $315B cost, sectoral job loss estimates), and author-identified as policy researchers. The Bier '25 (Cato) card is also strong on author quals (Director of Immigration Studies, cited by SCOTUS) and nonpublic data access, but the Payan card covers broader economic impacts with more metrics.

### Collapse flexibility
**Tag: "It's torture targeted at marginalized groups." — Lee et al. '24 (Physicians for Human Rights, Feb 2024)** — This card creates an independent impact scenario (torture/human rights violation via solitary confinement) that does not rely on the encryption-access link or the economic link. Similarly, the Chavez '25 card (encrypted apps as lifelines) and Payan '25 card (economic devastation) provide three distinct terminal impacts (safety, rights, economy) with different link chains, giving the 2NR multiple collapse options.

### Warrant depth
**Tag: "L: To do this, ICE wants access to encrypted communications" — Petti '25 (Matthew Petti, Reason, Sept 25, 2025)** — The tag claims ICE *wants* access to encrypted comms, but the card text only describes ICE buying Cellebrite/Paragon tools to hack *locked phones in physical possession* ("break into a locked phone in their physical possession") and redacted justifications. It does not warrant that ICE seeks *mandated backdoors* or *lawful access to encrypted communications in transit* — the specific mechanism the resolution debates. The warrant gap: phone-cracking tools ≠ encryption backdoor mandate. A proper warrant would cite FBI/ICE testimony advocating for "lawful access" legislation (e.g., EARN IT Act, LAED Act) or DOJ "going dark" reports explicitly calling for mandated decryption.

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** The case contains a framing tension: Contention 1 (Chavez '25, AD '25) argues encryption protects immigrants *from* ICE surveillance, implying the status quo (strong encryption) is good for immigrants. Contention 2 (Petti '25, US OLP '22) argues ICE *is already breaking* encryption via hacking tools and wants more access. These are not contradictory on face, but the case never resolves whether the Aff's "lawful access" proposal *changes* anything given ICE already has Cellebrite/Paragon — i.e., uniqueness/non-uniqueness is unaddressed. A consistency card would be tagged "Framing — Lawful access mandate expands ICE surveillance from targeted phone-hacking to dragnet interception — qualitative escalation, not status quo" to unify the contentions.
