the accounts were involved in drug production, exporting, importing, sampling,reselling, and payments
among others. Since drug trafficking is prohibited by Dutch (and international) law, these activities need
to be shielded from the Dutch authorities. This consequently leads to covert networks, criminal networks
who want to avoid detection (Diviák, 2020; Morselli et al., 2007). Covert networks are usually positioned
on a secrecy / efficiency continuum (Baker and Faulkner, 1993; Morselli et al., 2007; Oliver, 2014). This
means that actors in a network at one end of the spectrum place an emphasis on secrecy. Bosses shield
their lines of communication and are therefore not immediately available to make quick decisions.
Network actors at the other end of the spectrum who give preference to efficiency, attach more
importance to quickly achieving their goals instead of protecting their identities. These differences
theoretically manifest themselves in different scores, e.g. a high density versus a low density, centralized
versus decentralized structures, and core-periphery structures versus other types. Oliver (2014),
however, indicates that the hypothesized differences are not reflected in her meta-analysis. Moreover,
what one study regards as a high score for efficiency, another study interprets as indicative of security
(Oliver, 2014). In the PGP dataset, however, there is less ambivalence about the secrecy or efficiency
debate. Looking back atthe accounts who exchanged messages, it can be argued that although they were
involved in covert activities (they are involved in large-scale drug trafficking, after all), their
communication about these activities was anything but veiled. The accounts openly exchanged detailed
information about drug quantities, drug routes, fake companies, fake cargo, corrupt customs officials or
personnel in private firms, arrival dates, prices, etc. It is therefore perhaps more apt to talk of overt
covert networks. The use of encrypted communication devices apparently led to a (mistaken)trustin
secrecy. Theoretically, this would suggest that all accounts should score relatively high on efficiency
instead of secrecy. It would also explain why a small-world effect likely occurred. Type of Users A PGP
dataset also has another peculiarity that needs to be taken into account. Where the secrecy / efficiency

continuum is relevant in determining the position of its central leaders, the encrypted communication
devices were used by people who already had gained positions of some importance in the illicit drug
market. At least, that is what the message threads indicate. Nearly every account was involved in the
production, brokering, or trafficking of illegal drugs on the wholesale level. Although the message
threads sometimes mentioned that subordinates or front men were used to specifically create distance
from law enforcement, these lower ranking associates did not partake in the encrypted communication
network.16 There were therefore no peripheral players who acted as a buffer for the core. Centrality
scores in a PGP network thus do not translate to tracking potential ringleaders, but tell us something
about positions of influence in a criminal network of like-minded peers. In this sense, the data are almost
the mirror opposite of those in Agresta et al. (2016). They noted that crime bosses did not occupy central
positions in their SNA based on wiretaps because the more important mobsters were careful to avoid or
minimise telephone communication. The core-periphery shape that the synthetic drug network
resembles thus is not the result of any form of shielding or the creation of buffers, but the outcome of
more or less active accounts in the giant component. Centrality Scores Some SNA centrality scores
appear to be less suitable because of the large number of accounts involved in the trafficking of synthetic
drugs (and other illegal drugs). For example, closeness, a well-established centrality measure that
calculates the distance between nodes in a network to reach other nodes, proved to be of little use to a
PGP dataset. Hundreds of accounts were found to be assigned similar high scores for closeness. This
means that an intervention solely based on closeness in the overall network makes little sense.
Closeness was therefore set aside. However, even the use of algorithms like PageRank and HITS which
track down ‘big linkers’ in large datasets, is not without its problems. That is because it needs to be kept
in mind that encrypted communication datasets are inherently different from public datasets. PageRank
and HITS are tooled towards the latter kind of dataset, of course. Ordinary users in public, legal
environments strive to gain top rankings as it literally pays dividends to become the most popular site or
influencer. However, this is absolutely not the goal of encrypted communication users. None of the
message threads even remotely indicate that an account holder attempted to become the primus inter
pares within a technological network based on PGP messages. It is just a communication device which
lacks any kind of system to collect or dispense any sort of positive ratings or recommendations. The
accounts in the network openly communicated about their illegal activities, but this was still a closed
activity to all others not invited to partake. A primary goal for using encrypted communication is to
lessen the risk of detection by the authorities, and perhaps criminal rivals as well. Becoming well-known
would defeat the purpose of it all.17 With these reservations in mind, SNA can be applied to encrypted
communication datasets. A combination of scores, like degree and betweenness, result in finding
accounts that take up influential positions in the network. Moreover, the nature of the data not only
makes it possible to track who messaged whom, but also about what. Because the content of the
messages is included in the original dataset, there is an in-built opportunity to check centrality scores’
validity, so to speak, or to add certain attributes like language or types of drugs. Conclusion This study

and significantly increased the capacities of their high-tech crime units in order to target hardened
communications remotely and access their contents. These efforts need to be supported and further

the contents captured, but to deconflict and coordinate subsequent judicial investigations. Two

investigations. Efforts in this area need to be sustained. In addition to national efforts, local investigation
services also have an important role to play against encrypted criminal communication services. Some

acknowledges this idea, while making it compulsory to declare to a competent public authority the

authorities investigating against criminal encrypted communication systems. However, not all countries
have adopted laws and regulations of this type. Moreover, general provisions for the access to
encrypted contents vary significantly from one country to another. While some allow the hacking of a
suspect’s phone, others prohibit investigators’ use of a PIN code that would have been seized during a
house search, for example.1 The targeting of criminally dedicated communication services has been

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case presents raw evidence cards without any pre-written impact calculus, magnitude comparisons, or probability framing. No card explicitly weighs the "going dark" problem against encryption benefits or establishes a hierarchy of impacts (e.g., lives saved vs. privacy lost). A weighing card would carry a tag like **"Weighing — Magnitude: Lawful access prevents mass-casualty events that outweigh speculative privacy harms"** citing a RAND or Congressional Budget Office cost-benefit analysis, placed as a standalone "Framework/Weighing" block before Contention 1.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No card proactively attacks the Con's core positions — e.g., that backdoors create systemic vulnerability, that criminals will switch to open-source encryption, or that lawful access regimes have historically been abused. An offensive spike would be a card tagged **"Spike — Backdoor Fallacy: Mandated access doesn't require weakening encryption protocols; client-side scanning / lawful hacking alternatives solve"** citing a 2023 Berkman Klein Center or Carnegie Endowment study, placed in Contention 1 or a dedicated "Preempts" section.

A defensive spike would carry a tag like **"Frontline — No Systemic Weakening: Lawful access frameworks (e.g., UK IPA, Australian TOLA) target specific accounts with warrants, not bulk protocol backdoors"** citing the 2024 UK Investigatory Powers Act independent review or a 2023 Lawfare analysis, placed at the end of Contention 1.

### Evidence quality
**ABSENT:** Most evidence lacks author qualifications, institutional affiliation, or methodological transparency. The Doffman (Forbes, 2025) and Levine (ABC, 2024) cards are journalistic summaries without primary sourcing; Soudjin (ResearchGate, 2022) is a non-peer-reviewed working paper; Rajic (CSIS blog, 2024) is an associate fellow's blog post; ABS (2025) is raw statistical data without analytical warrant; Faure (Police Chief, 2025) is an op-ed by a law-enforcement official; Europol (2025) and Australian Federal Police (2021) are self-reported agency press releases; Gaskell (Cybernews, 2021) is a tech-news aggregation. No card comes from a peer-reviewed journal, government accountability office, or independent cryptographic expert. A replacement card would be a 2023–2024 **NIST or ENISA technical report on lawful access architectures** with named PhD authors, or a **Congressional Research Service legal analysis** of encryption mandates.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** All evidence funnels into a single link chain: "criminals use encryption → law enforcement cannot investigate → therefore lawful access needed." There is no alternative impact scenario (e.g., child exploitation, terrorism financing, ransomware critical infrastructure, foreign intelligence) that could be independently extended if the drug-trafficking link is defeated. A flexibility card would be tagged **"Alt Impact — CSAM: End-to-end encryption blocks detection of child sexual abuse material; lawful access enables client-side scanning that preserves privacy for lawful users"** citing a 2024 NCMEC or Thorn annual report, placed as a second contention or "Flex Impact" module.

### Warrant depth
**ABSENT:** Every card is a claim+cite with no explanatory warrant connecting the evidence to the tagline. Example: the Doffman card asserts "FBI wants access… so does Europe" but never explains *why* that access solves investigations without breaking encryption. The Soudjin card describes PGP network topology but does not warrant how lawful access would dismantle it. The Faure card lists operational needs but does not explain the technical mechanism for real-time access that avoids systemic risk. A warranted card would include a parenthetical warrant: **"(Warrant: Client-side scanning on device compares hashes to CSAM database before encryption; only flagged content is revealed to law enforcement — preserves E2EE for all other comms)"** citing a 2023 USENIX Security or IEEE S&P paper.

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** The case mixes mutually contradictory framings. Doffman/Levine cards cite FBI/UK/EU demands for "lawful access" (implying legislative mandates), while Faure/Europol cards celebrate *hacking* encrypted networks (EncroChat/SkyECC) — a law-enforcement *offensive* capability that does not require a backdoor mandate. The ABS card shows Australian crime *decreasing* to record lows (2023–24), undercutting the "going dark" crisis narrative. The Soudjin card shows criminals *already* operating openly on encrypted apps, suggesting detection is possible without new laws. No unified framing (legislative mandate? technical standard? international treaty?) ties the cards together. A consistency fix would insert a **framing card** at the top: **"Framework: The Pro advocates a narrow statutory framework for court-ordered, target-specific decryption assistance — not protocol backdoors — modeled on UK IPA s.253 / Australian TOLA TAN/TCN regime"** citing the 2024 UK IPA independent reviewer report.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 2/10** — The case is a raw evidence dump with no strategic architecture: no weighing, no spikes, low-quality sources, single-link collapse, zero warrants, and internal contradictions that Con can exploit in crossfire and rebuttal.
