A. Revised Test
This Article <mark>propose</mark>s that <mark>the President</mark> may <mark>use force</mark> without direct congressional approval if she seeks <mark>to protect a</mark>n important <mark>national interest recognized</mark> by Congress that will not rise to the nature, scope, or duration of the war. A President determines what constitutes a war based on the circumstances <mark>and</mark> any parameters established by Congress. Although the revised test retains the two prongs of the OLC’s test, it includes additional limiting principles.
First, “important national interests” must be <mark>defined by Congress</mark>, not the President. Instead of national interests deriving from those reasonably recognized by the President, there must be a statutory authority authorizing force to defend the interests. This prong is highly formalistic, wherein the President must find a statute passed <mark>through</mark> bicameralism and presentment that identifies the national interest, justifying their decision to use military force.
Congress can recognize an important national interest either <mark>explicit</mark>ly <mark>or implicit</mark>ly. Explicit recognition would include a clear statement of the national interest and approval for using military force to protect it.339 An excellent example of explicit recognition is the Taiwan Relations Act.340 This Act recognizes Taiwan’s strategic value and provides an ambiguous security guarantee, wherein intervention in Taiwan is left to the President’s discretion.341 Explicit recognition thus requires Congress to state that the subject is an important national interest and that defending this interest justifies military intervention.
Implicit recognition occurs when Congress passes a law to achieve a specific foreign policy goal.342 By passing the law, Congress recognized that the goal was an important national interest. Still, implicit <mark>authorization</mark> must come from a bill in the context of foreign policy or the military. It cannot be from a domestic law irrelevant to national security or foreign policy. Additionally, military intervention must be necessary to achieve that goal recognized by the law.
The American intervention in Somalia illustrates an implied recognition of a national interest. The OLC justified American military intervention in Somalia as implicitly authorized by the Horn of Africa Recovery and Food Security Act passed by Congress.343 This act authorized emergency aid.344 Still, military intervention was the only way to distribute this aid.345 Congress’s law implicitly recognized getting humanitarian aid to Somalia as an important national interest. <mark>Thus</mark>, <mark>Congress must pass</mark> a <mark>law</mark> <mark>recognizing</mark> an important national <mark>interest and</mark> either <mark>explicitly approve</mark> military <mark>force or consider it necessary to protect</mark> that recognized <mark>interest</mark>.
Second, the President can use military force without explicit congressional approval only when the intervention constitutes a war under Congress’s “declare war” power. This test accepts that the original conception of war encompassing all conflict initiation is untenable and accepts the delineation between limited conflicts and expansive wars. The OLC’s proposed “nature, scope, and duration” parameters can guide the President and their advisers to analyze what constitutes a war. Still, Congress retains the power to override the President’s standard for what constitutes a war.
Therefore, defining what constitutes a war will be treated as a concurrent power between the Executive and the Legislature. Although concurrent powers leave deference to the President, it does not foreclose Congress from defining what constitutes a war. Congress is incentivized to assert its authority to interpret the meaning of war, which supersedes presidential discretion. Still, congressional silence will not prevent the President from intervening. The President’s discretion in interpreting what constitutes a war is proportional to the amount of clarification Congress gives.
To illustrate how this test would operate, consider President Biden’s recent air campaign against the Houthi militant group in Yemen. The Houthis, a Yemeni militant group, continue to attack commercial shipping in the Gulf of Aden.346 These attacks threatened American ships and global trade passing through the Suez Canal.347 In response, President Biden ordered strikes against Houthi positions.348 In a letter to Congress, Biden claimed that the strikes were “consistent with my responsibility to protect United States citizens both at home and abroad and in furtherance of United States national security and foreign policy interests, pursuant to my constitutional authority as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive and to conduct United States foreign relations.”349
Both prongs must be met for the President to be justified in using military force. If there is a failure to justify intervention through a national interest recognized by Congress or if the conflict is assessed to reach the level of war, then the President cannot use force without congressional approval.
Under this test, President Biden’s claim to inherent powers would be insufficient. Instead, Congress would need to preemptively pass legislation that identifies global shipping routes as a vital interest that the President may use appropriate force to defend. Recognizing an important national interest does not need to be highly specific to shipping in the Gulf of Aden. Congress can provide a broader authorization. The key is that Congress must recognize global shipping as an important national interest. Alternatively, the President could look through legislation that identifies global shipping as a vital interest and can only be enforced by using military force against Houthi positions.
Then, President Biden would need to determine whether the strikes would likely reach a constitutional war’s nature, scope, and duration. This would be based on President Biden’s determination unless Congress passed legislation establishing guidelines for when a conflict reaches the scope of war. Once both prongs are met, then the strikes would be permissible.
Thus, the test shifts the determination of an important national interest away from the President’s discretion. Still, by allowing for implicit authorization, the President retains the flexibility to make the strikes. The test adds at least one further step that guides the executives’ decisions about what is in the national interest.
B. Why the Revised Test
The revised test maintains the OLC’s general proposal but includes limiting principles for each prong. Still, it is imperative to recognize why these alterations effectively balance the President’s pragmatic needs and the Constitution’s prophylactic protections. Ultimately, the revised test achieves this balance because it creates a constitutionally permissible framework, preserves Congress’s essential functions of determining long-term policy goals, grants the President flexibility to respond to threats to important national interests, and creates a judicially enforceable standard.
1. Constitutionally Permissible
First, the proposed OLC test revisions avoid reliance on problematic constitutional arguments. The OLC relied on inherent presidential powers and historical gloss to justify the President’s power to intervene without congressional approval.350 Both these justifications are contrary to the Constitution’s spirit. Inherent presidential powers are incompatible with the principle of limited and enumerated powers that undergird the Constitution. Historical gloss undermines the concept of a written Constitution. Just because the branches have acted unconstitutionally does not allow them to assume power through adverse possession. The danger of accepting these arguments is that they justify the President’s further encroachment on the legislature since they can act without statutory authority in those cases. If the President can justify acting on their own in war, what is to stop this extension into other domestic affairs, such as legislating without congressional approval? The proposed test forgoes the need to rely on unconstitutional arguments and thus avoids establishing dangerous precedents that could further expand the imperial presidency.
If Congress must define national interests, then <mark>military intervention</mark> by the President <mark>becomes an act of executing</mark> the <mark>law rather than</mark> acting on inherent <mark>power</mark>s. Requiring the President to only act on national interests recognized by Congress ensures that the President only executes a conflict <mark>via the Commander in Chief</mark>, Take Care, and Executive Power <mark>clauses</mark>. The President does not need to rely on questionable arguments of residual powers in foreign affairs, but is granted that discretion by Congress.351 Thus, the OLC only needs to rely on the textually defined role of the President and not stretch the understanding of presidential powers to include inherent powers that would expand the authority of the President and undermine the essential checks and balances of our federal government.
The Court’s holding in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. et al. v. Sawyer352supports the revised test. In this case, from the Korean War, the Court rejected Truman’s assertion of inherent powers to break a steel mill strike.353 The Court held that the President lacked the power from Congress or inherent powers to act domestically.354 Justice Jackson’s concurrence in establishing three zones of presidential power became the framework that the Court utilized when analyzing actions by the President.355 Zone 1 is when the President acts on Congress’s express or implied authorization.356 Zone 2 is when the President acts on independent powers from the Constitution, where concurrent authority exists with Congress, and the President acts independently.357 Zone 3 is when the President is at odds with Congress and can only act when given explicit power in the Constitution.358
Applying the Jacksonian framework, the revised test moves the debate of presidential war powers from a Jacksonian zone two or three to a zone one, where the President and Congress agree. Zone one powers do not need inherent powers; thus, this test does not need to rely on them. Therefore, the revised test brings presidential war powers into line with the Jacksonian framework and ensures that they are within the bounds of the powers granted to the President.
Congressionalist scholars could criticize this revision as impermissibly delegating legislative authority to the Executive, as Congress does to administrative agencies.359 In doing so, this test would greatly expand presidential power. Although delegation is not ideal, the current war powers structure grants the President broader discretion than if Congress proactively defined it. By requiring that the statute be explicit or implicit with context and necessity limitations, it ensures that there is always an intelligible principle. Moreover, the revision ensures that Congress at least considered the potential for conflict and could deliberate on the importance of the interests. The revised test is not the ideal construction of war powers, but it better comports with presidential power than the current model.
Furthermore, the first prong requiring Congress to define a national interest properly is compatible with the legislature’s role in the Constitution’s War Powers Scheme. The text of the Constitution gives Congress the power to “declare war.”360 Importantly, the Constitution does not define how Congress “declare[s] war.” Nothing prevents Congress from implicitly or preemptively authorizing the President to engage in future conflicts. The early Supreme Court agreed with this conclusion in Bas v. Tingy, where the Court stated that Congress could pass legislation that implicitly authorizes “war.”361 Thus, it is within Congress’s power to preemptively authorize through explicit or implicit statutes what national interests permit the President to use force to defend.
Next, the second prong avoids using historical gloss to justify a narrow interpretation of the meaning of war. Since the statutory authorization grants the President the ability to utilize force, there is no need to seek a narrow definition of war justified by historical practice. Instead, the second prong is more a recognition that Congress cannot delegate in advance authorization for a large-scale conflict. This is because the “declare war” power needs to be retained to some extent, and the more significant the conflict, the more likely Congress needs to give direct and close-in-time authorization.362 Defining what constitutes war is thus a Jacksonian Zone 2 concurrent power, wherein the President can determine what constitutes a war unless Congress passes a statute on the issue.
Additionally, the second prong limits the extent to which Congress can delegate military affairs. A fair concern with these revisions is that Congress is withdrawing from the declarations of large-scale wars through delegation. However, this is where the second prong creates a limitation. Congress cannot cede from its explicitly granted powers since they are not privileges of the legislature but part of a broader scheme to defend against tyranny. Thus, by including the nature, scope, and duration test, Congress cannot delegate large-scale wars but can define the Constitution’s war powers through the state. Absent this definition by Congress, the President can be guided by their interpretation, which the OLC provides. Effectively, it prevents Congress from delegating largescale conflicts without explicit authorization.
Treating the definition of war as a concurrent power is compatible with the Supreme Court’s understanding of presidential and congressional war powers. In the Quasi-War case, Little v. Barreme, the Court found that the specificity of Congress’s statutes limited the President’s discretion in enforcing an authorization to seize vessels.363 Congress’s explicit statutory direction on what ships the Navy could seize superseded the President’s interpretation.364 Still, the Court recognized that the less direction Congress granted, the greater discretion the President had.365
In this case, evaluating what constitutes a war through a “nature, scope, and duration” analysis can be considered reasonable. It accounts for the fact that the more intensive the fighting is, the larger the conflict covers, the longer the conflict lasts, and the greater the likelihood it is a war that requires congressional authorization. Still, <mark>if Congress disagrees with</mark> the <mark>President’s parameters</mark>, <mark>it can provide</mark> greater <mark>specificity by</mark> passing <mark>new</mark> laws and <mark>regulation</mark>s.
These arguments illustrate that although the revised test takes a pragmatic and living constitutionalist approach, it remains bound by the Constitution’s text. In doing so, it avoids assertions of inherent presidential powers or historical gloss. Thus, the proposed revision to the OLC’s test solves the challenges to its suspect constitutionality.
2. Preserves the Role of Congress
Second, the proposed revisions to the OLC’s test preserve Congress’s role in guiding long-term strategic decisions. While many scholars agree that Congress should have some role in initiating conflict, the proposals revolve around how Congress writes and passes military authorizations or increases Congress’s supervisory powers.366 The first fails because it is impractical, as illustrated by the previous sections.367 The second would be similar to the War Powers Resolution, which does not preserve Congress’s role as the first mover in a conflict. The proposed test provides a more practical approach by giving Congress the exclusive power to define national interests. In taking a formalist approach to defining national interests, Congress retains a role in defining the longterm goals of American foreign policy and military intervention.
Likewise, the revised test guides the President on what constitutes recognition of a national interest by Congress. One option is for the statute to clearly state the national interest and explicitly permit military force to defend it. Alternatively, Congress can give implicit authorization if the law defines a goal or policy that requires military intervention. Notably, because presidents rely on authority in statutes rooted in national security or foreign policy, the test avoids concerns that presidents will use irrelevant statutes to justify their military actions. Additionally, domestic laws unrelated to foreign policy cannot meet the requirement for defining a national interest.
For an example of the national interest model, consider the case underlying the Curtiss-Wright decision. Although the Court’s dicta argues for inherent presidential powers in foreign affairs, the facts of the case illustrate Congress defining a national interest that the President can achieve.368 The case arose from a law allowing the President to control the flow of arms into the Chaco region to achieve peace.369 Congress gave the President statutory authority and direction to make decisions to achieve the stated goal of peace in the region.370 The law did not mandate specific sanctions or military intervention, but defined the policy goal and granted discretion to the President in achieving this goal.371
Critics of the revised test could argue that permitting implicit authorization allows the President to use statutes that do not intend to grant the President initiatory war powers to justify their actions.372 This concern about presidential manipulation is valid, as many statutes likely provide some language that could be interpreted as implicit recognition of a national interest. Moreover, the AUMF following 9/11 has been repeatedly manipulated by the President to permit interventions in conflicts increasingly tangential to the War on Terror.373
These criticisms are valid. While the test does not provide a silver bullet to prevent a President from acting in bad faith, it still provides more legal definition to identify when that President is exceeding his authority. The law <mark>can constrain</mark> the <mark>President by creating norms</mark> internalized in the executive branch and assist Congress and the populace in <mark>identify</mark>ing <mark>when they exceed</mark> their <mark>authority</mark>.374
Still, the additional context <mark>and</mark> necessity requirements <mark>alleviate</mark> some of these <mark>circumvention</mark> concerns. To avoid misuse, presidents could not use domestic laws or irrelevant statutes to justify military intervention because the test requires the law to be in the context of national security or foreign policy. Moreover, the requirement that military intervention be essential to achieve the law’s goals prevents Congress from considering, or should have considered, that passing the law might necessitate military force. Additionally, <mark>it can permit</mark> the President to establish <mark>doctrines of deterrence regarding nucl</mark>ear weapons and autonomous systems to avoid later challenges to their <mark>deployment</mark> by the public. Although not infallible, these <mark>limit</mark>ing principle<mark>s prevent blatant</mark> statutory <mark>manipulation</mark> by the President.
By retaining Congress’s role as the initiator of conflict, the public can debate the interests that justify using force abroad.375 Congress’s <mark>openness</mark> in its decision-making <mark>ensures</mark> that the <mark>public has</mark> a greater opportunity to <mark>weigh in</mark> on the conflict. Even if the issue does not maintain significant political salience, the ability for public discourse as to what constitutes important national interests warrants the force to defend.
As for the second prong, treating it as a concurrent power offers Congress the option but not the obligation to define what constitutes a war. Doing so incentivizes Congress to assert its authority, if it wishes, to limit the President’s power. Similarly, Justice Jackson’s concurrence utilized this institutionalist approach in its Category Two powers.376 Where Congress has not spoken, the President has greater discretion. Still, if <mark>Congress</mark> decides to define a Constitutional war, they can. Thus, the nature, scope, and duration can be legitimate guiding principles for the President without congressional action.
3. Pragmatic <mark>Respect</mark> for the <mark>Need for</mark> Presidential <mark>Flexibility</mark>
Third, the proposed test provides the President with flexibility in using force. Although the President cannot define a national interest unilaterally, she can still pursue limited military incursions. <mark>The President can</mark> still <mark>make rapid decisions to defend</mark> vital <mark>interests</mark>, only losing their overreach into long-term strategic markers.377 Moreover, if the President recognizes risks <mark>or</mark> needs, they can <mark>persuade Congress to</mark> pass new legislation that <mark>preemptively recognize</mark>s a national interest that might require using military force.
Additionally, the second prong defers to presidential expertise in determining what interventions will rise to the level of war. Even if Congress were to assert its power and set forth a standard defining what constitutes a war, the President would still have latitude in determining which military interventions would meet that standard. Thus, the President retains significant power to assess situations and determine the most appropriate response within the confines of the statutes passed by Congress.
Moreover, setting <mark>clear standards</mark> to guide the President’s decisionmaking can <mark>ensure</mark> greater <mark>legitimacy and support</mark> when conflict is pursued. Presidents can avoid arguments that their intervention is not constitutional and ensure that Congress does not oppose the conflict for lack of voice or authorization.378 The specific parameters for explicit or implicit recognition further reduce ambiguity. In doing so, the President can focus more on implementing foreign policy and less on the legitimacy of that power.379
4. Judicially Enforceable
Lastly, the revised test creates a judicially enforceable standard for determining if an action is within the national interest. Democratic Institutions and the Credibility of Threats A central question among constitutional war powers scholars is whether robust checks—especially congressional ones—on presidential use of force lead to “sound” policy decision-making. Congressionalists typically argue that legislative control over war decisions promotes more thorough and valuable deliberation, including more accurate weighing of consequences and gauging of political support for military action.166 Presidentialists usually counter that the executive branch has better information and therefore better ability to discern the dangers of action or inaction, and that quick and decisive military moves are often required to deal with security crises.167 If we are interested in these sorts of functional arguments—and we should be—then reframing the inquiry to include threatened force prompts critical questions as to whether such checks also contribute to or detract from effective deterrence and coercive diplomacy and therefore positively or negatively affect the likelihood of achieving aims without resort to war. Here, recent political science provides some reason for optimism, though the scholarship in this area is neither well-developed nor conclusive. To be sure, “soundness” of policy with respect to force is heavily laden with normative assumptions about war and the appropriate role for the United States in the broader international security system, so it is difficult to assess the merits and disadvantages of constitutional allocations in the abstract. The importance of credibility to strategies of threatened force adds important new dimensions to this debate. On the one hand, one might intuitively expect that robust democratic checks would generally be ill-suited for coercive threats and negotiations—that institutional centralization and secrecy of decision-making might better equip non-democracies to wield threats of force in support of foreign policy ambitions. They have concluded that the institutionalization of political contestation and some diffusion of decision-making power in democracies of the kind described in the previous Section make threats especially credible and effective in resolving international crises without actual resort to armed conflict. Recent arguments in effect turn some old claims about the strategic disabilities of democracies on their heads: whereas it used to be generally thought that democracies are ineffective in wielding threats because they are poor at keeping secrets and their decision-making is constrained by internal political pressures, a new wave of political science accepts this basic description but argues that these democratic features are really strategic virtues.171 Rationalist models of crisis bargaining between states assume that because war is risky and costly, states will be better off if they can resolve their disputes through bargaining rather than by enduring the costs and uncertainties of armed conflict.172 Effective bargaining during such disputes—that which resolves the crisis without a resort to force—depends largely on states’ perceptions of their adversary’s capacity to wage an effective military campaign and its willingness to resort to force to obtain a favorable outcome. A state targeted with a threat of force, for example, will be less willing to resist the adversary’s demands if it believes that the adversary intends to wage and is capable of waging an effective military campaign to achieve its ends. If a state perceives that the threat from the adversary is credible, that state has less incentive to resist such demands if doing so will escalate into armed conflict. The accuracy of such perceptions, however, is often compromised by informational asymmetries that arise from private information about an adversary’s relative military capabilities and resolve that prevents states from correctly assessing each other’s intentions, as well as by the incentives states have to misrepresent their willingness to fight—that is, to bluff.173 Informational asymmetries increase the potential for misperception and thereby make war more likely; war, consequentially, can be thought of in these cases as a “bargaining failure.”174 Some political scientists have argued in recent decades—contrary to previously common wisdom—that features and <mark>constraints</mark> of democracies make them better suited than non-democracies to <mark>credibly signal</mark> their <mark>resolve</mark> when they threaten force. An adversary that understands these political vulnerabilities is thereby more likely to perceive a threat that a democratic leader does issue as highly credible, in turn making it more likely that the adversary will yield.177 Other scholars have recently pointed to the special role of legislative bodies in signaling and threatening force. Kenneth Schultz, for instance, argues that the open nature of competition within democratic societies ensures that the interplay of opposing parties in legislative bodies over the use of force is observable not just to their domestic publics but to foreign actors; this inherent transparency within democracies—magnified by legislative processes—provides more information to adversaries regarding the unity of domestic opponents around a government’s military and foreign policy decisions.178 Political opposition parties can undermine the credibility of some threats by the President to use force if they publicly voice their opposition in committee hearings, public statements, or through other institutional mechanisms. Furthermore, legislative processes—such as debates and hearings—make it difficult to conceal or misrepresent preferences about war and peace. Faced with such institutional constraints, Presidents will incline to be more selective about making such threats and avoid being undermined in that way.179 This restraining effect on the ability of governments to issue threats in turn makes those threats that the government does issue more credible because observers will likely assume that the President would not issue it if he anticipated strong political opposition. Especially when legislative members of the opposition party publicly support an executive’s threat to use force during a crisis, their visible support lends additional credibility to the government’s threat by confirming that domestic political conditions favor the use of force should it be necessary.180 The credibility-enhancing effects of legislative constraints on threats are disputed. Some studies question the assumptions underpinning theories of audience costs—specifically the idea that democratic leaders suffer domestic political costs to failing to make good on their threats, and therefore that their threats are especially credible181—and others question whether the empirical data support claims that democracies have credibility advantages in making threats.182 Other scholars dispute the likelihood that leaders will really be punished politically for backing down, especially if the threat was not explicit and unambiguous or if they have good policy reasons for doing so.183 Additionally, even if transparency in democratic institutions allows domestic dissent from threats of force to be visible to foreign audiences, it is not clear that adversaries interpret these mechanisms as political scientists expect in their models of strategic interaction, in light of various common problems of misperception in international relations.184 These disputes are not just between competing theoretical models but also over the links between any of the models and real-world political behavior by states.
