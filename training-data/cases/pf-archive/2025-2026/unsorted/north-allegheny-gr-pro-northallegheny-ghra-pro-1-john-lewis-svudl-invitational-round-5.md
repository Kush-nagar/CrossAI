Dr. Melvin R.J. Soudjin 22, Senior Researcher at the National Unit Information Provision Service, “When encryption fails: a glimpse behind the curtain of synthetic drug trafficking networks,” ResearchGate, 6/xx/22, https://tinyurl.com/ytbf2w4k, tristan
Based on the content of the messages, the accounts were involved in drug production, exporting, importing, sampling, reselling, and payments among others. Since drug trafficking is prohibited by Dutch (and international) law, these activities need to be shielded from the Dutch authorities. This consequently leads to covert networks, criminal networks who want to avoid detection (Diviák, 2020; Morselli et al., 2007). Covert <mark>networks</mark> are usually <mark>positioned on</mark> a <mark>secrecy</mark> / efficiency continuum (Baker and Faulkner, 1993; Morselli et al., 2007; Oliver, 2014). This means that actors in a network at one end of the spectrum place an emphasis on secrecy. Bosses shield their lines of communication and are therefore not immediately available to make quick decisions. Network actors at the other end of the spectrum who give preference to efficiency, attach more importance to quickly achieving their goals instead of protecting their identities. These differences theoretically manifest themselves in different scores, e.g. a high density versus a low density, centralized versus decentralized structures, and core-periphery structures versus other types. Oliver (2014), however, indicates that the hypothesized differences are not reflected in her meta-analysis. Moreover, what one study regards as a high score for efficiency, another study interprets as indicative of security (Oliver, 2014).
In the PGP dataset, however, there is less ambivalence about the secrecy or efficiency debate. Looking back at the accounts who exchanged messages, it can be argued that although they were involved in covert activities (they are involved in large-scale drug trafficking, after all), their communication about these activities was anything <mark>but</mark> veiled. The <mark>accounts openly exchanged detailed info</mark>rmation <mark>about</mark> drug quantities, drug <mark>routes</mark>, <mark>fake companies</mark>, fake <mark>cargo</mark>, <mark>corrupt</mark> customs <mark>officials</mark> or personnel in private firms, <mark>arrival dates</mark>, prices, etc. It is therefore perhaps more apt to talk of overt covert networks. The <mark>use of encrypted communication</mark> devices apparently <mark>led to</mark> a (<mark>mistaken</mark>) <mark>trust</mark> in secrecy. Theoretically, this would suggest that all accounts should score relatively high on efficiency instead of secrecy. It would also explain why a small-world effect likely occurred.
Type of Users
A PGP dataset also has another peculiarity that needs to be taken into account. Where the secrecy / efficiency continuum is relevant in determining the position of its central leaders, the encrypted communication devices were used by people who already had gained positions of some importance in the illicit drug market. At least, that is what the message threads indicate. Nearly every account was involved in the production, brokering, or trafficking of illegal drugs on the wholesale level. Although the message threads sometimes mentioned that subordinates or front men were used to specifically create distance from law enforcement, these lower ranking associates did not partake in the encrypted communication network.16 There were therefore no peripheral players who acted as a buffer for the core. Centrality scores in a PGP network thus do not translate to tracking potential ringleaders, but tell us something about positions of influence in a criminal network of like-minded peers. In this sense, the data are almost the mirror opposite of those in Agresta et al. (2016). They noted that crime bosses did not occupy central positions in their SNA based on wiretaps because the more important mobsters were careful to avoid or minimise telephone communication. The core-periphery shape that the synthetic drug network resembles thus is not the result of any form of shielding or the creation of buffers, but the outcome of more or less active accounts in the giant component.
Centrality Scores
Some SNA centrality scores appear to be less suitable because of the large number of accounts involved in the trafficking of synthetic drugs (and other illegal drugs). For example, closeness, a well-established centrality measure that calculates the distance between nodes in a network to reach other nodes, proved to be of little use to a PGP dataset. Hundreds of accounts were found to be assigned similar high scores for closeness. This means that an intervention solely based on closeness in the overall network makes little sense. Closeness was therefore set aside.
However, even the use of algorithms like PageRank and HITS which track down ‘big linkers’ in large datasets, is not without its problems. That is because it needs to be kept in mind that encrypted communication datasets are inherently different from public datasets. PageRank and HITS are tooled towards the latter kind of dataset, of course. Ordinary users in public, legal environments strive to gain top rankings as it literally pays dividends to become the most popular site or influencer. However, this is absolutely not the goal of encrypted communication users. None of the message threads even remotely indicate that an account holder attempted to become the primus inter pares within a technological network based on PGP messages. It is just a communication device which lacks any kind of system to collect or dispense any sort of positive ratings or recommendations. The accounts in the network openly communicated about their illegal activities, but this was still a closed activity to all others not invited to partake. A primary goal for using encrypted communication is to lessen the risk of detection by the authorities, and perhaps criminal rivals as well. Becoming well-known would defeat the purpose of it all.17
With these reservations in mind, SNA can be applied to encrypted communication datasets. A combination of scores, like degree and betweenness, result in finding accounts that take up influential positions in the network. Moreover, the nature of the data not only makes it possible to track who messaged whom, but also about what. Because the content of the messages is included in the original dataset, there is an in-built opportunity to check centrality scores’ validity, so to speak, or to add certain attributes like language or types of drugs.
Conclusion
This study explored the possibilities of using SNA in regard to message threads sent by unknown users of <mark>encrypted</mark> tele<mark>communication</mark> devices to gain a better understanding of the structural characteristics and <mark>central position</mark>s <mark>of</mark> synthetic <mark>drug networks</mark>. With the focus on Dutch-speaking accounts that communicated at least once about synthetic drugs, thousands of relevant accounts were found. From a meso level perspective, three findings stand out.
Firstly, the majority of the accounts (58%) within the total synthetic drug market is involved in poly-drug trafficking. Within the giant component, the percentage of poly-drug trafficking rises to 66%, or even to 81% in the case of accounts with the highest centrality scores. It is noteworthy that these percentages are based on a large sample. After all, the total size of the network encompasses 4,158 accounts (sub-question 1). What is more, the account holders were not run of the mill subjects. Every account was involved in drug trafficking on a wholesale level. Centrality scores therefore did not translate to tracking potential ringleaders, but only tell us something about positions of influence in a criminal network of like-minded peers (sub-question 5).
Secondly, the core of the network is quite large. About <mark>three quarters of</mark> all the <mark>accounts</mark> were <mark>situated in</mark> a <mark>giant component</mark>. This giant component should not be confused with the existence of one big <mark>criminal</mark> organisation, but a network in which three quarters of its total population i<mark>s</mark> inter<mark>connected through dozens of subgroups</mark> that fracture in even smaller networks. The Louvain and Girvan-Newman methods showed that the accounts which existed in a giant component could be divided into 46 communities (sub-question 3). Again, the 46 communities are a theoretical construct, and should not be seen as proof of the existence of 46 criminal groups. Otherwise each average criminal group would consist, of 66 members (3,019: 46), an improbably high number considering that Dutch drug trafficking investigations usually do not exceed 25 suspects. If we would hazard a guess, we would posit that hundreds of smaller criminal groups collaborate with each other within the overall synthetic drug network. Such numbers correspond with a recent study which put the average size of organised crime groups in the UK at 5.4 members, while its largest group only consisted of 21 members (Niezink and Campano, 2022). These numbers are also in line with findings from the US and Canada (Bouchard and Morselli, 2014).

Walker 20 [Summer Walker; Head of Security and Rights Initiatives for the Global Initiative, 2020, "Transformative Technologies: How digital is changing the landscape of organized crime," https://globalinitiative.net/analysis/cyber-tech-organized-crime/, DOA: 10-29-2025] shaan
How <mark>the growth of digital technologies has changed</mark> market <mark>dynamics</mark>
While the emergence of online markets on both the surface and the dark web, together with the growing availability of a range of digital and information communication technologies, has not transformed the underlying infrastructure of the international drug-trafficking trade, it has catalyzed a number of shifts in market dynamics.
Many <mark>drug-trafficking groups exploit</mark> technology and <mark>apps to communicate</mark> – El Mencho, of <mark>the Sinaloa cartel</mark>, <mark>communicates with</mark> his senior traffickers by <mark>WhatsApp</mark> group.32 With <mark>end-to-end encryption</mark>, and easily installed, this is a simple and direct way in which to command horizontally structured organizations. WhatsApp is used by dealers and users in many countries as a means through which the various risks related to drug transactions (including arrest and hijacking) are diminished.
<mark>Trafficking networks</mark> are increasingly likely to <mark>rely on online</mark> financial <mark>transactions</mark>, including with the use of payment apps on smartphones, like EcoCash, which is common in Zimbabwe.33 This allows consumers and dealers to avoid carrying cash, reducing the risk of cash being used as evidence in the event of arrest, and of being hijacked.
Turning to darknet markets, these have the greatest effect on the final links in the supply chain, and on consumer markets.
Online markets have transformed the drug market from a network economy, in which <mark>limited advertising and clandestine operations mean transactions</mark> typically <mark>occur through existing networks and</mark> thereby <mark>favour incumbents</mark>, to a conventional market where dealers compete on price, quality and service. This has eroded the incumbent advantage and lowered barriers to entry.34
Social-media platforms are being used to recruit new sellers. Research in the UK found that social media has reportedly been used to advertise the luxurious lifestyles of drug dealers and to groom child dealers as a way of cutting out the middleman, thereby penetrating local markets directly.35 In two separate trafficking arrests in the US, the person arrested claimed to have responded to an advertisement on Facebook for ‘people looking for work’ or ‘an opportunity to make money’, and ended up trafficking illicit drugs for cartels in Mexico.36 Social media is thus able to create new opportunities for transactional relationships between criminal groups and new ad-hoc employees.
Recreational users buy illicit drugs on darknet markets for a combination of reasons related to safety, quality and ease of purchase. The online market allows for <mark>anonymity in</mark> both <mark>sales and purchases</mark>, <mark>reduces direct contact</mark> between players in the market (thus possibly reducing risks associated with open-air drug markets), and in some cases has allowed for enhanced accountability in rating a seller’s product or products – an interesting potential benefit from a public-health perspective. However, the ease with which vendors are able to post fake reviews and information online is poorly understood by users, and this can easily increase a user’s chance of being misinformed.37 Purchases tend to be based on ‘price, details of product, vendor reputation, feedback from other buyers, and available “trip reports” (descriptions of personal experiences with the effects of specific substances)’.38 Some listings are even described as ‘fair trade’ or ‘conflict free’. The prevalence of illicit-drug supply on social-media platforms, which are heavily used by those between the ages of 16 and 24, could result in growing markets among younger drug users.39 Built-in design features on these platforms, including ‘suggested friend’ functions and hashtags to expand reach, can be used by dealers to expand their customer base.
The advantages for sellers on darknet markets are that they can avoid detection and minimize risks, such as ‘arrest and violence, and threat to profits and reputation’.40 As noted above, this may have lowered barriers to entry for small-scale players in the drug-trafficking markets; a hypothesis supported by the limited use of the darknet by larger criminal groups.
According to RAND, both buyers and vendors of illicit drugs on the darknet share a common profile. This has been identified as someone young, male, hailing from English-speaking or West European countries, well-educated, entrepreneurial and with strong IT skills.41 This is, to a significant extent, shaped by the prevalence of darknet markets for final-leg distribution in consumer countries. Some research suggests that online vendors are either established dealers with access to the product, or newcomers who are expanding their base beyond selling to friends.42 There is concern that a younger and more tech-savvy consumer base could access illicit substances more easily, but evidence has not yet revealed this to be a trend.
<mark>Buyers</mark> purchasing drugs on the darknet typically <mark>use</mark> bitcoin or other types of <mark>cryptocurrency to pay</mark> for the product. In some cases, including in the case of the original Silk Road, payments are held in escrow by darknet administrators until the product is received. Then, the purchased substance is mailed through either private or public mail systems. <mark>Products can be delivered to</mark> a number of <mark>anonymous locations</mark>, including anonymous post-office boxes, automated booths or ‘packstations’.
<mark>By collapsing geographical distances</mark>, <mark>online</mark> criminal <mark>markets empower</mark> smaller dealers to supply local markets while trading on the <mark>global drug-trafficking market</mark>. They also rely on small-parcel post, which has a significantly low surveillance rate, thereby further reducing the risk of engagement.43
Reduced operating costs means drug prices are predicted to decrease.44 Online dealers require fewer employees and benefit from lower overheads, as is also the case with licit online businesses.
Legal and enforcement challenges
When trying to tackle operations conducted on the surface web, <mark>law-enforcement</mark> entities often <mark>rely on</mark> the <mark>cooperation of private-sector</mark> e-commerce <mark>platforms</mark> and social-networking sites. However, such <mark>cooperation has always been ad hoc</mark> or at the discretion of the respective platform.
When platforms have cooperated in attempting to mitigate their use by drug-trafficking operators, the speed at which sellers adapt to these changes lessens the impact. For example, in 2017, Facebook disabled certain key search terms – for example, ‘OxyContin’, ‘Xanax’ and ‘fentanyl’ – on its search engine and replaced some search results with adverts for substance-abuse treatment. Unlike a “back door,” which generally refers to an undisclosed vulnerability in an application or device, a front door is a <mark>well-documented</mark> and clear <mark>mech</mark>anism <mark>for</mark> both encrypting and <mark>decrypting data</mark>, whether it be data in motion (communications) or at rest (stored data). <mark>To be secure</mark>, encryption should be <mark>subject to rigorous testing</mark>. Essentially, a front door is the digital equivalent of a big, ingeniously engineered lock on the only entrance to an otherwise secure building. It is a lock that has been tested by every available lock picker and found to be secure, with any identified weaknesses being constantly fixed. Such a lock is always superior to a secret entrance in the rear of a building.
When presented with the option of using the front door versus the back door, law enforcement will always choose the former, and <mark>nobody is suggesting</mark> that <mark>there should be any imperfections</mark> built into the front door lock. The split key model mitigates this risk and allows for the preservation of secure, timely, and efficient front door access to evidence when lawfully authorized; splitting the key to provide that access will align the inherent risk of improper access with what should be demanded of a free society.
IV. Splitting the Key
The use of split key encryption to lock access to data significantly mitigates the concern that a duplicate or escrow key will be abused. An encryption key itself can not only be split into discrete parts and stored separately; it can be encapsulated within other “containers” of encrypted data.46 Each container can be placed in the possession of another entity, requiring cooperation by two or more entities to unlock the series of keys to decrypt the cipher text into plain text47 . Thus, for example, the manufacturer of a device could hold one portion of the key, or a key to an encapsulated container.48 A privacy group could hold another key.49 Only by combining all parts of the key could the cipher text be returned to plain text.50 If and when the government sought the use of the encryption key, it would bear the burden to satisfy both custodians that access is authorized by law, in most cases as the result of a lawful order. Each custodian would then be in a position to assess the legal basis and contest the order if it believed the access to be improper. Thus, only when both custodians validated the legal basis for access, each of which approaching the question with differing interests, would decryption occur. 51 This split key option preserves privacy, security, and the government’s ability to obtain evidence when authorized.

Like CALEA, a statutory obligation along the lines proposed herein will inevitably trigger criticisms and generate concerns.62 <mark>One</mark> obvious <mark>criticism is</mark> that the creation of an escrow key or the maintenance of a duplicate key by a manufacturer would introduce an unacceptable <mark>risk of compromise</mark> for the device.63 This argument presupposes that the risk is significant, that the costs of its exploitation are large, and that the benefit is not worth the risk. <mark>Yet</mark> manufacturers, product <mark>developers</mark>, service providers, and users <mark>constantly introduce such risks</mark>. Nearly every feature or bit of code added to a device introduces a risk, some greater than others. The vulnerabilities that have been introduced to computers by software such as Flash, ActiveX controls, Java, and web browsers are well documented.64 The ubiquitous SQL database, while extremely effective at helping web designers create effective data driven websites, is notorious for its vulnerability to SQL injection attacks.65 Adding microphones to electronic devices opened the door to aural interceptions.66 Similarly, the introduction of cameras has resulted in unauthorized video surveillance of users.67 Consumers accept all of these risks, however, because we, as individual users and as a society, have concluded that they are worth the cost.

When thinking about warrants, it is essential to know that <mark>law enforcement cannot</mark> simply <mark>get a warrant for any reason</mark>. There must be certain rules and guidelines followed. The Fourth Amendment and Its Role in Issuing Warrants
The <mark>Fourth Amendment</mark> of the United States Constitution plays a major role in the way warrants are issued. This amendment was put in place <mark>to protect citizens from unreasonable searches</mark> and seizures. It states that <mark>warrants must be</mark> based on probable cause and that the warrant must describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized.
Probable cause is a term that comes up often when discussing warrants. Probable cause means that there is a reasonable belief, <mark>based on facts</mark>, that a crime has been committed. Law enforcement officers must convince a judge that there is probable cause before a warrant can be issued. This <mark>protects people </mark>from having their <mark>privacy </mark>violated without a good reason.
In order to issue a warrant, a judge must be presented with evidence that shows probable cause. This evidence can include witness statements, physical evidence, or even digital records. However, this does not mean that a warrant can be issued based solely on suspicion or a hunch. There must be something more concrete.
