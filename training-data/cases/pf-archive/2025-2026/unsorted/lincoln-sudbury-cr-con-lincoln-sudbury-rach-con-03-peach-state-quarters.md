Quantum key distribution requires special hardware to produce the encryption keys. And the <mark>laws of physics make it impossible</mark> for a third party <mark>to copy</mark> or store <mark>the key</mark>.
Two types of quantum weirdness make quantum encryption possible: quantum superposition and quantum entanglement. <mark>Here’s how it works</mark>: <mark>the device that produces</mark> quantum <mark>keys</mark> emits pairs of photons, and <mark>sends one photon from each pair to Alice and the other</mark> one <mark>to Bob</mark>. <mark>Each photon is in a superposition of states</mark>—for example, a photon’s polarization can be simultaneously horizontal and vertical—thus <mark>representing a "0" and a "1" at the same time</mark>. <mark>Only</mark> the act of <mark>detecting</mark> the photon <mark>can make it settle</mark> on one of the possible choices.

Once Alice and Bob have their shared encryption key, Alice can then use it to encrypt her message and send it to Bob by any means of communication: <mark>to anyone who intercepts it</mark>, <mark>the</mark> encrypted <mark>message will look like a string of totally random bits</mark>, and <mark>only Bob, who has the key, will be able to decrypt it</mark>.
Trouble is, <mark>there is no way for</mark> the designers of the <mark>quantum encryption device to store a</mark> copy of the <mark>key</mark> or to provide it to a third party: <mark>after the device has sent out</mark> the <mark>photons</mark>, even <mark>the device itself has no way of knowing which of the bits will turn into 1s and which will turn into 0s</mark>.
