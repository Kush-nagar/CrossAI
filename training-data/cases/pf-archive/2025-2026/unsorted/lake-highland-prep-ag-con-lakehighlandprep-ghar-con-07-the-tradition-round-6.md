Authoritarian states known for their extensive surveillance systems include the GDR, the People’s Republic of China, the Soviet Union, and North Korea.Footnote1 In the past few decades, however, established liberal democracies have been increasingly ready to monitor their citizens on a massive scale. One notorious surveillance program run by an alliance of democracies was uncovered by Edward Snowden. The current rise of large-scale government surveillance is widely viewed with great concern, if not outright horror. Critics fear the rise of Orwellian surveillance states and celebrate Edward Snowden as a paragon of civil disobedience.Footnote2 Much of the criticism of government surveillance has revolved around privacy, its meaning and value, and how it is impacted by surveillance. Government surveillance and <mark>the erosion of privacy it is </mark>associated with are being discussed as a cause of distrust and feelings of vulnerability, as a potential source of discrimination and unjust domination, and as <mark>a threat to democracy </mark>and the integrity of the public sphere, to name but a few concerns. At the same time, there remains some ambiguity about the acceptability of surveillance in democracies. For one thing, recent technological advances have made large-scale government surveillance not only feasible and cheap but also, in one sense, less intrusive. Modern government surveillance relies increasingly on technology rather than human spies and informers. Surveillance practices include, for instance, the monitoring of public spaces with CCTV cameras, the automatic interception and retention of Internet and telecommunication traffic, and the use of artificial intelligence to make sense of the huge amounts of data collected. As a result, modern surveillance is characterized by the rarity of actual human access to the large quantities of data collected. While the quantity of data collected is staggering, only a small proportion of them are ever accessed by a human person. Human access to collected surveillance data can be expected to further decrease as artificial intelligence becomes more sophisticated. This has led to a debate about whether modern surveillance even reduces the privacy of those subject to surveillance, with some arguing that modern surveillance, involving little human access to the collected data, tends to leave people’s privacy intact.Footnote4 For another thing, surveillance operations by democracies seem much more acceptable than the kind of surveillance conducted by authoritarian regimes. Democracies are using surveillance mostly for seemingly innocuous purposes, such as combating terrorism and serious crime or, most recently, containing the spread of a deadly virus. They are less inclined to use surveillance to crush legitimate political opposition or to persecute members of stigmatized groups, notable exceptions notwithstanding.Footnote5 There is a significant moral difference, then, between, say, the NSA and the East German Stasi.Footnote6 In sum, when democratic governments conduct large-scale surveillance operations in the pursuit of seemingly innocuous goals, all while limiting human access to the collected data, the case against surveillance is at least not obvious. In fact, some philosophers have expressed wholehearted support for large-scale government surveillance. Noting that it is generally permissible for law enforcement agencies to secure information about past events, one advocate of government surveillance has suggested that “the State should place all of its citizens under surveillance at all times and in all places.”Footnote7 Others have invoked catastrophic risks, such as those posed by biological and nuclear weapons of mass destruction, to justify extensive government surveillance.Footnote8 To be sure, such wholesale endorsements of government surveillance are the exception.Footnote9 But they convey an idea of the ethical ambivalence of government surveillance. My goal in this paper is to contribute a new perspective on what is ethically at stake when democratic governments monitor their citizens and to achieve a better understanding of what is pro tanto objectionable about it.Footnote10 I will proceed by distinguishing three independent concerns that a critic of government surveillance may have. The first concern is that governments diminish citizens’ privacy by collecting large amounts of data. This concern focuses on the loss of privacy brought about by the collection of data as such, that is, irrespective of whether the data will be accessed or used for objectionable purposes. The second concern is that the collected data may be accessed after all, again causing a loss of privacy, though of a different kind. The data may be accessed by government employees or exposed to the public through a hack or a leak. The third concern is that the collected data may be used for objectionable purposes (other than accessing the data). Two of the above introduced concerns revolve around privacy. Zooming in on these two concerns, this paper seeks, first, to shed light on the significance of privacy in the context of surveillance. Engaging with the debate about the meaning of privacy, I will suggest that, whereas access to data is objectionable as such, the privacy loss brought about by the mere collection of data does not constitute an independent reason to object to government surveillance. Second, moving on to the third concern, the paper seeks to achieve a better understanding of problems associated with what surveillance can be used for. I will suggest that one serious and underappreciated problem with surveillance is related to the problem of political legitimacy. For most of human history, natural pandemics have posed the greatest risk of mass global fatalities.37 However, there are some reasons to believe that natural pandemics are very unlikely to cause human extinction. Analysis of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) red list database has shown that of the 833 recorded plant and animal spe- cies extinctions known to have occurred since 1500, less than 4% (31 species) were ascribed to infectious disease.38 None of the mammals and amphibians on this list were globally dispersed, and other factors aside from infectious disease also contributed to their extinction. It therefore seems that our own spe- cies, which is very numerous, globally dispersed, and capable of a rational response to problems, is very unlikely to be killed off by a natural pandemic. One underlying explanation for this is that highly lethal pathogens can kill their hosts before they have a chance to spread, so there is a selective pressure for pathogens not to be highly lethal. Therefore, patho- gens are likely to co-evolve with their hosts rather than kill all possible hosts.39 Recent developments in biotechnology may, how- ever, give people the capability to design pathogens which overcome this trade-off. Some gain-of-func- tion research has demonstrated the feasibility of altering pathogens to create strains with dangerous new features, such as vaccine-resistant smallpox40 and human-transmissible avian flu,41 with the poten- tial to kill millions or even billions of people. For an engineered pathogen to derail humanity’s long-term future, it would probably have to have extremely high fatality rates or destroy reproductive capability (so that it killed or prevented reproduction by all or nearly all of its victims), be extremely infectious (so that it had global reach), and have delayed onset of symptoms (so that we would fail to notice the prob- lem and mount a response in time).42 Making such a pathogen would be close to impossible at present. However, the cost of the technology is falling rapid- ly,43 and adequate expertise and modern laboratories are becoming more available. The United States economy finds itself at a critical juncture, grappling with a monumental surge in overall debt levels that has expanded by an astonishing $4.4 trillion in household debt alone since late 2019, reaching a record $18.59 trillion by September 2025. This unprecedented accumulation, spanning government, household, and corporate sectors, is now casting a long shadow over the Federal Reserve's ability to wield its primary monetary policy tool—interest rates—without risking profound economic instability. As of late 2025, the central bank faces a delicate balancing act: combatting persistent inflationary pressures while simultaneously navigating the immense burden of servicing a national debt that has swelled to over $38 trillion. The implications are immediate and far-reaching. Every percentage point increase in interest rates translates into billions more in debt service costs for the federal government alone, potentially crowding out essential public spending and exacerbating fiscal strain. For American households and businesses, higher borrowing costs threaten to stifle investment, depress consumer spending, and trigger a wave of delinquencies, all of which could plunge the economy into a deeper slowdown. This intricate web of debt is forcing the Federal Reserve to walk a tightrope, where every policy decision carries the potential to either stabilize or destabilize an already fragile financial landscape. The Unprecedented Surge: A Detailed Look at America's Debt Explosion. The $4.4 trillion increase in overall U.S. debt since late 2019 is merely a segment of a larger, more alarming trend, with the national debt alone surging by an astonishing $10.83 trillion, reaching over $38 trillion by October 23, 2025. This rapid accumulation has been fueled by a confluence of factors, primarily the extraordinary government response to the COVID-19 pandemic, coupled with pre-existing fiscal vulnerabilities. The timeline of this debt explosion begins in late 2019, when the national debt stood at $23.2 trillion, and corporate debt was already hovering around $9.5 trillion. The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in early 2020 served as a catalyst for unprecedented federal spending. Major legislative packages, including the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security (CARES) Act, the Consolidated Appropriations Act, and the American Rescue Plan Act, injected trillions into the economy through stimulus checks, unemployment benefits, and business aid. This pushed budget deficits to levels not seen since World War II, causing the national debt to jump to $27.75 trillion by December 2020. In the years that followed, leading up to November 2025, the debt continued its relentless climb, driven by an aging population, rising healthcare costs, and crucially, rapidly escalating interest payments on the existing debt. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projects this trajectory to continue, with debt held by the public potentially reaching 172% of GDP by 2054. Across the sectors, the debt picture is equally stark. Government debt is the primary driver, with federal spending consistently outpacing revenues. As of September 2025, the federal government debt stood at $37.6 trillion, with interest payments alone projected to exceed $1 trillion in 2025. Household debt has also swelled to a record $18.20 trillion by July 2025, a $4.6 trillion increase since 2019. Mortgage debt ($12.80 trillion) constitutes the largest share, fueled by pandemic-era refinancing, but credit card debt has surged to new records, reaching $1.21 trillion by Q2 2025, accompanied by rising delinquency rates. Corporate debt, which was already significant pre-pandemic, saw further growth, driven by a low interest rate environment, though the exact contribution to the overall $4.4 trillion figure is less precisely delineated. Key players in this unfolding drama include the Federal Reserve (The Fed), which responded to the pandemic with massive quantitative easing, becoming the largest holder of U.S. national debt, peaking at over $6 trillion in 2022. Subsequently, to combat inflation that surged to 9% in mid-2022, the Fed embarked on the fastest tightening cycle since the 1980s, aggressively raising the federal funds rate from near zero to above 5% by mid-2023. More recently, by late 2024 and October 2025, the Fed began reducing rates to a target range of 3.75%-4.00% to stimulate a softening economy. The U.S. Treasury is tasked with managing this colossal debt, constantly navigating volatile financial markets and the complexities of debt issuance. Financial institutions, including banks, pension funds, and insurance companies, are also significant stakeholders, holding vast amounts of government and corporate debt, making them highly vulnerable to any fiscal crisis or significant shifts in interest rates. Initial market reactions to the escalating debt burden have been cautious, with concerns about long-term economic impacts, potential inflation, and erosion of purchasing power. Rating agencies have taken notice, with Fitch Ratings downgrading U.S. debt from AAA to AA+ in August 2023, and Moody's following suit in May 2025, lowering the U.S. credit rating from Aaa to Aa1. These downgrades reflect growing investor apprehension about the government's fiscal trajectory. The Fed's aggressive rate hikes, while necessary to tame inflation, led to immediate and substantial increases in borrowing costs across the board, impacting mortgage rates, credit card interest, and corporate loans. This has contributed to market volatility and uncertainty, further complicated by the Fed's ongoing efforts to reduce its massive balance sheet, creating a challenging environment for absorbing the expanding Treasury debt. Corporate Fortunes: Winners and Losers in a Debt-Laden Economy. The intricate dance between escalating debt levels and the Federal Reserve's constrained monetary policy creates a landscape of distinct winners and losers across the corporate spectrum. Public companies, from tech giants to local banks, are now navigating an environment where the Fed's every move on interest rates is amplified by the colossal debt burden, shaping their access to capital, consumer demand, and ultimately, their profitability. In a scenario where the Federal Reserve is compelled to keep interest rates lower than desired to avert a widespread economic contraction—a plausible outcome given the $38 trillion national debt—highly leveraged companies stand to benefit significantly. Lower borrowing costs would ease the burden of servicing their substantial debt, improving cash flow and potentially allowing for refinancing at more favorable terms. This environment would also favor interest-sensitive, capital-intensive sectors.Technology companies, often reliant on cheap capital for research and development and whose valuations are sensitive to discount rates, could thrive. Firms like Microsoft (NASDAQ: MSFT) or Amazon (NASDAQ: AMZN), though well-capitalized, could see continued favorable borrowing conditions for expansion. Similarly, the Real Estate sector, including homebuilders like D.R. Horton (NYSE: DHI) and Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) such as Simon Property Group (NYSE: SPG), would see demand stimulated by lower mortgage rates. Consumer Discretionary companies, ranging from Target (NYSE: TGT) to Ford Motor Company (NYSE: F), would likely experience increased demand as consumers' disposable income is less burdened by debt servicing. Conversely, Financial Institutions like JPMorgan Chase (NYSE: JPM) or Bank of America (NYSE: BAC) might see their net interest margins squeezed in a prolonged low-rate environment, potentially impacting profitability. However, should persistent inflation force the Federal Reserve to raise rates despite the debt concerns, the landscape shifts dramatically, creating a challenging environment for many. Highly leveraged companies would face significantly increased debt service costs, eroding profit margins and elevating default risks. This would particularly impact firms in sectors with high debt-to-equity ratios. Interest-sensitive sectors, especially Technology, would suffer as higher discount rates reduce future earnings valuations, making external funding more expensive and potentially stifling innovation and growth for companies like Meta Platforms (NASDAQ: META) or emerging startups. The Consumer Discretionary sector would also face headwinds as rising borrowing costs for mortgages and credit cards dampen consumer confidence and reduce discretionary spending, hurting retailers and auto manufacturers. The Real Estate and Construction industries would see demand cool as mortgage rates climb, impacting developers and construction firms. In this tightening scenario, Financial Institutions (banks) would initially benefit from wider net interest margins, at least until higher rates lead to reduced loan demand and increased defaults. Consumer Staples companies, such as Procter & Gamble (NYSE: PG) or Coca-Cola (NYSE: KO), which provide essential goods, are typically more resilient and defensive in such environments. Companies with strong balance sheets and low debt, regardless of sector, would also be better positioned to weather the storm, potentially gaining market share as weaker, more indebted competitors struggle. The current environment demands that companies meticulously manage their debt profiles and operational efficiencies, as the Fed's constrained hand could lead to abrupt shifts in market conditions, favoring prudence over excessive leverage. Wider Significance: A Global Economic Quagmire. The staggering rise in U.S. debt and the Federal Reserve's resulting policy constraints are not isolated events; they are deeply intertwined with broader global economic trends and carry significant ripple effects across international markets and policy frameworks. This situation signals a potential shift in the global economic order, challenging long-held assumptions about monetary policy independence and fiscal sustainability. Globally, debt levels have soared past $307 trillion by 2023, largely propelled by public sector borrowing in response to the pandemic. The U.S. debt-to-GDP ratio, exceeding 100%, mirrors a trend seen in many advanced economies, reminiscent of post-World War II levels. This massive global debt burden exacerbates the inflationary pressures that have persisted since 2021. When governments finance deficits through borrowing, it injects demand into the economy, pushing prices upward. More critically, if markets perceive that central banks might be forced to monetize government debt to manage its burden, long-term inflation expectations can become unanchored, a particularly acute risk in emerging markets. This creates a challenging fiscal policy environment where governments worldwide grapple with the dilemma of increasing debt servicing costs crowding out spending on essential services like infrastructure and education, thereby limiting their ability to respond to future crises or invest in long-term growth. The ripple effects of this U.S. debt dilemma are profound and far-reaching. On competitors and partners, the Fed's actions or inactions directly influence global borrowing costs. When the U.S. raises rates, it often leads to higher borrowing costs for other nations, particularly those with dollar-denominated debt, potentially slowing their economic growth. Currency fluctuations, driven by uncertainty in U.S. fiscal and monetary policy, can impact global trade and investment flows, making U.S. exports more or less competitive. Furthermore, periods of U.S. fiscal uncertainty or monetary tightening can trigger capital flight from riskier emerging markets into safer U.S. assets, causing currency depreciations and financial strain in developing economies. On international markets, the persistent U.S. debt concerns pose a long-term threat to the dollar's status as the world's primary reserve currency. While no clear rival currently exists, a loss of confidence could force the U.S. to incur higher borrowing costs and face greater economic constraints, leading to global market volatility and asset price distortions, given the central role of U.S. assets in the global financial system. For the Federal Reserve, the regulatory and policy implications are stark. The rising debt significantly constrains its monetary policy, creating a risk of "fiscal dominance"—where the central bank is implicitly or explicitly pressured to keep interest rates low to manage government borrowing costs, even if it means tolerating higher inflation. This compromises the Fed's independence and its ability to achieve its mandate of price stability. For the U.S. government, the implications are equally pressing. It faces immense pressure to undertake credible fiscal adjustments through politically difficult decisions on tax increases, spending cuts, or both. Failure to address the unsustainable debt trajectory risks a full-blown fiscal crisis, diminished economic growth, and a weakening of the United States' global leadership role. Historical precedents offer sobering lessons. The Post-World War I era saw many nations struggling with high sovereign debt, with some experiencing hyperinflation (Germany) and others resorting to "financial repression" to keep borrowing costs low. A more direct comparison for the U.S. is the period during World War II and up to the 1951 Fed-Treasury Accord, where the Federal Reserve's independence was effectively subordinated to fiscal policy, as it was directed to suppress interest rates to manage wartime debt. The eventual Accord restored the Fed's independence. While the specifics differ, these historical examples underscore the recurring tension between high government debt and independent central bank policy, highlighting the persistent risks of inflation and economic instability when this balance is disrupted. What Comes Next: Navigating a High-Stakes Economic Future. The path forward for the U.S. economy, the Federal Reserve, and global markets is fraught with uncertainty, as the nation grapples with an unprecedented debt burden that fundamentally reshapes policy options. Both short-term maneuvers and long-term structural shifts are anticipated, demanding strategic pivots from businesses and investors alike. In the short term, the Federal Reserve's immediate focus will likely remain on fine-tuning interest rates in response to incoming economic data, particularly labor market trends. This shift away from balance sheet reduction is a dovish signal, potentially putting downward pressure on Treasury yields and supporting risk assets, at least temporarily. However, policy uncertainty remains high, influenced by geopolitical tensions and the ever-present risk of fiscal impasses like government shutdowns, which can disrupt economic data flow crucial for the Fed's decisions. Looking long-term, the specter of "fiscal dominance" looms large over the Fed's independence. With the national debt projected to reach 116% of GDP by 2034 and 172% by 2054, future Fed chairs will face intense political pressure to keep interest rates low to manage the government's soaring borrowing costs. Succumbing to this pressure risks reigniting inflation and potentially pushing long-term rates even higher as investors demand greater compensation for holding depreciating assets. Some experts even suggest the possibility of the Fed re-launching quantitative easing (QE) to create artificial demand for Treasuries, a move that could be seen as a de facto monetization of government debt. This would fundamentally alter the Fed's monetary policy framework, shifting its primary focus from inflation and employment to supporting government financing, with profound implications for price stability. For businesses, strategic adaptation is paramount. Diligent cost management and enhanced operational efficiency will be crucial in an environment of potentially sustained higher interest rates (even with short-term cuts, long-term rates are influenced by supply/demand for debt) and economic uncertainty. Businesses must also prioritize supply chain resilience amidst geopolitical tensions and trade protectionism, which can fuel inflation. Prudent capital allocation and proactive debt management—refinancing when favorable, reducing leverage when possible—will be vital for companies reliant on borrowing. Adapting pricing and product strategies to align with shifts in consumer purchasing power, especially during periods of moderating wage growth or higher inflation, will also be key. Investors will need to navigate increased market volatility with a focus on diversification and quality.
