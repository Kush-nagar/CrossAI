## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case lacks explicit weighing mechanisms embedded within cards—no cards use language like "outweighs on magnitude," "probability first," "timeframe," or "scope" to pre-script comparative impact calculus. While the Eisenhardt block tags "outweighs and turns case" at the top, none of the subsequent cards (Simon 25, Jimenez 24, Caillon 24, Young 25, Minton 18, Shancer 17) extend that weighing with explicit comparative warrants. A card filling this gap would be a 2023–2025 meta-analysis or CBO estimate tagging "Magnitude: state tax revenue from sports betting exceeds $2B annually—outweighs opponent's isolated harm claims on scope and probability" placed as a subpoint under Contention 1.

### Offensive spikes
**Young 25** — "Prediction markets are not new, but they're new in the sports world… In states where sports betting remains illegal, however, platform customers have increasingly begun to use prediction markets to 'invest' in sporting event outcomes… Is this sort of an end around to sports betting?" (Scott Young, 2025, Harvard Law School). This functions as an offensive spike by preempting Pro arguments that legalization expands gambling access: it documents that prohibition merely diverts demand to unregulated prediction markets (Kalshi), reframing the "expansion" narrative as a solvency deficit for Pro's world.

### Defensive spikes
**Minton 18** — "The more the criminals profit by illegal gambling, the more money and incentive they have to attempt to corrupt players and officials… proposals that will make it harder, if not impossible, for legal gambling operators to offer the same types of games, odds, and payouts as the illegal market, ensuring that customers continue to spend their money illegally." (Michelle Minton, 2018, Competitive Enterprise Institute). This preempts the common Pro response that federal regulation improves integrity by arguing that over-regulation drives consumers to illegal markets with *less* oversight, directly frontlining the "integrity" and "consumer protection" arguments Pro will likely run.

**Shancer 17** — "As seen after the demise of online poker in 2006, players will not simply give up and disappear, but rather will find a new source to feed their gambling habits. State laws banning DFS in their jurisdiction only push players to pursue DFS and gambling on the black market." (Joshua Shancer, 2017, DePaul J. Art Tech & IP Law). This serves as a historical defensive spike, using the poker black-market precedent to inoculate against Pro's "ban reduces gambling" claims by empirically establishing demand inelasticity.

### Evidence quality
**Caillon 24** — "The majority of patients achieved middle (74.2%) and long-term (78.9%) recovery… Online gambling did not seem to influence middle- and long-term recovery compared to offline gambling." (Julie Caillon, 2024, PubMed Central, 2-year longitudinal study, n=237). This wins evidence-comparison tiebreaks: author is a hospital research engineer in addictology at a university department (strong quals), 2024 recency, peer-reviewed longitudinal design with multivariate regressions, and a 237-patient sample with registered clinical trial (NCT01248767). Beats typical Pro cards citing advocacy-group surveys or single-year cross-sections.

**Simon 25** — "New York's online sports betting market… collects over $200 million in revenue per quarter… 14 states and D.C. earmark a portion of sports betting revenue toward problem gambling treatment." (Rob Simon, 2025, US Census Bureau). Census Bureau institutional source, 2025 data, quarterly revenue figures across multiple states—high credibility for magnitude claims.

**Young 25** is weaker: Scott Young is a WSJ bestselling author/podcaster, not a gambling policy expert; the Harvard Law School event is a secondary summary of McCann's remarks, not primary research. Would lose a quals tiebreak vs. a 2024 NCSL or GAO report.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case funnels through a single narrative—legal markets generate tax revenue → fund treatment → treatment works (Caillon 24) → prohibition drives users to illegal/prediction markets (Minton 18, Shancer 17, Young 25). No distinct second impact scenario (e.g., consumer protection via regulated operators, job creation, sports integrity via legal monitoring, tribal sovereignty, or state fiscal autonomy). A card filling this gap would be a 2023–2025 AGA or Oxford Economics study tagging "Regulated markets create 200k+ jobs and $40B+ in economic output—independent fiscal impact even if treatment funding fails" placed as a standalone Contention 2.

### Warrant depth
**Caillon 24** — The tagline "Treatment is effective---empirics" receives a full warrant: the card details the 2-year longitudinal design (n=237), bivariate and multivariate logistic regression methodology, specific recovery rates at 1 and 2 years (74.2%, 78.9%), odds ratios for self-efficacy (OR=1.04) and mood disorders (OR=11.18), and the clinical trial registration number. The warrant explains *why* and *how* the conclusion holds.

**Simon 25** — The tagline "Tax revenues lead to gambling addiction treatment" is warranted with specific state examples (NY 51% tax rate, $200M/quarter; OH $39M first quarter; NC $38M; IL graduated rate increase) and the explicit Census Bureau finding that 14 states + DC earmark revenue for treatment.

**Jimenez 24** — The tagline "Treatment is effective---empirics" (re-used) is *not* warranted by this card; Jimenez 24 only documents state earmarking practices, not treatment efficacy. This is a warrant mismatch—the card doesn't support the tag.

**Young 25** — The tagline "People turn to prediction markets" is warranted with a direct quote from McCann describing Kalshi's operation and state lawsuits alleging it's an "end around to sports betting," but lacks data on *volume* of users diverting (no market share, user counts, or dollar figures).

**Minton 18** — The tagline "Federal regulation makes it harder for legal sites to compete, facilitating an exodus to the illegal market" is warranted with the mechanism: regulated operators can't match illegal markets on games/odds/payouts → customers stay legal; if regulation prevents that parity → customers flee to illegal markets. No empirical data, but logical warrant is complete.

**Shancer 17** — The tagline "Players will not give up and disappear, but rather will find a new source… on the black market" is warranted with the 2006 online poker precedent (UIGEA) as historical evidence of demand inelasticity and migration to unregulated sites.

### Internal consistency
The case is internally consistent: Contention 1 (tax revenue → treatment funding → treatment works) and Contention 2 (prohibition → illegal markets/prediction markets → less consumer protection) both support the Con narrative that *legalization with regulation* outperforms *prohibition*. No contention undercuts another. The framing is uniform: "legal markets enable harm reduction; bans enable harm displacement." The only tension is terminological—the case uses "sports betting" and "DFS" and "prediction markets" somewhat interchangeably across cards (Shancer 17 discusses DFS; Young 25 discusses Kalshi/prediction markets; Simon/Jimenez discuss sports betting). This isn't a logical contradiction but creates a slight definitional looseness Pro could exploit on "what exactly is the topic?"
