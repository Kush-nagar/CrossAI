While recent scholarship has pointed to the production and mobilisation of knowledge and nonknowledge in governing borders and migration (Amelung, Scheel, and van Reekum Citation2024; Aradau and Perret Citation2022; Boswell and Chabal Citation2023), there has been considerably less focus on their fluid, that is, co-constitutive, relational and contextual dimensions. For deportation to be more palatable and accepted, despite its inherent structural and procedural violence, EUropean states produce a considerable amount of knowledge (Isakjee et al. Citation2020; Stel Citation2021), as well as nonknowledge regarding the severe consequences, harm and potential death resulting from deportation for the people subject to it. This interwoven process is visible in practices ostensibly designed to protect individual’s safety and right to life, such as pre-deportation medical checks. By generating evidence that a person is ‘safe’ to travel while disregarding inadequacies of the medical checks and the effects deportation has on people’s health and well-being, states obscure and legitimise the violence they enact through deportation. In this way, knowledge and nonknowledge are co-constitutive, operating in specific ways to support the practice of deportation. Non/knowledge is also relational and contextual. It is produced in socio-political environments structured by legal and policy landscapes, where state and other actors’ non/knowledge is generated in reaction to each other, whether to supress and ignore or modify and (re)legitimise their own behaviour and policy goals. In examining the co-constitutive processes of non/knowledge, the multitude of actors involved and its evolving context, we offer a renewed epistemological and methodological framework for understanding contemporary deportation practices. While these fluid dimensions of non/knowledge production cannot necessarily be disentangled empirically, in this paper, we do so to highlight specific aspects and how they manifest in practice.
Building on critical work on epistemic injustice (Aradau and Perret Citation2022; Dittmer Citation2021; Mayblin and Turner Citation2021), we understand deportation as a form of violence and injustice and wish to draw attention to the role of non/knowledge in its maintenance. We see borders not just as sites of state violence, but as political spaces where potential generative struggles can produce alternative subjectivities, agencies and even hope (Brambilla and Jones Citation2020; Perera Citation2007; Rajaram and Grundy-Warr Citation2007) and which require the consideration of alternative knowledge to that of the state. This paper is a call for the de-normalisation of non/knowledge that supports and helps legitimise deportation as a step closer to abolition, which we see as necessary for dismantling the violence and racialised power dynamics that underpin contemporary migration governance in EUrope.
Literature Review
The EUropean state functions as an arena of struggle for competing political interests, informed by various rationalities and actors, including those from state, supra/international, civil society and academic institutions. Its structures and institutions, however, have traditionally favoured the status quo, often co-opting or sidelining radical alternatives (Natter, Norman, and Stel Citation2023, 5). This has been made possible, among other things, by state actors’ central role in knowledge production (Natter, Norman, and Stel Citation2023). Traditionally, the state has been imagined as holding epistemic authority (Foucault Citation1978), a privileged access to authoritative knowledge that is fundamental to its legitimacy (Weber Citation1978), while seeking to render populations legible (Scott Citation1998). More recent scholarship on knowledge production has also drawn attention to state actors’ conditional interest in producing knowledge, with ‘ignorance’ described as a necessary dimension of governance (Boswell and Chabal Citation2023; Gross and McGoey Citation2023; Procter and Schiebinger Citation2008). This scholarship has shaped critical approaches to the production of knowledge, nonknowledge and ignorance in the governance of migration. As Boswell (Citation2023, 2) suggests, ‘[m]aintaining a degree of uncertainty is a useful strategy for handling this tension. Rather than managing undocumented migrants, states find it expedient to manage what is known about them’. Migration is then enacted as an intelligible object of governance through the interplay of knowledge and various types of nonknowledge (Scheel and Ustek-Spilda Citation2019; Tazzioli Citation2020).
The production, purpose and underlying logics of nonknowledge have received significant attention in recent years. Scholars have actively engaged with how the production of knowledge and different kinds of nonknowledge normalise <mark>hegemonic forms of knowing</mark> and marginalise, <mark>suppress</mark> or undervalue <mark>alternatives</mark> (Bradley Citation2023; Canning Citation2018; Procter and Schiebinger Citation2008). This has been variously described <mark>as</mark> ‘<mark>strategic ignorance’</mark> (Borrelli Citation2018; Boswell and Chabal Citation2023; Scheel and Ustek-Spilda Citation2019), ‘<mark>orchestrated invisibility’</mark> (Canning Citation2018), ‘<mark>fragmented’</mark> and ‘<mark>disjointed</mark> knowledge’ (Tazzioli Citation2020), ‘colonial unknowing’ (Bradley Citation2023, drawing on Gayatri Spivak’s ‘sanctioned ignorance’), ‘nonrecording’ (Rozakou Citation2017) and ‘nonknowledge’ (Aradau Citation2017; Aradau and Perret Citation2022). What these conceptualisations have in common is that <mark>not knowing</mark>, partial knowing <mark>or disregarding information</mark> often <mark>serves the interests of actors aiming to control mobility</mark>, whether operating through deliberate, intentional decisions or <mark>unconsciously as part of structural systems sustaining the status quo</mark> (Natter, Norman, and Stel Citation2023). While nonknowledge can also be mobilised in support of the interests of migrantised people (e.g., Borrelli Citation2018, Citation2024; Mann and Mourão Permoser Citation2022), this is arguably rarer when it comes to officials acting in the name of the state. Deportation officers, for instance, may find it expedient to <mark>disregard</mark> available information that is not essential to their task – such as an individual’s <mark>personal circumstances</mark>, <mark>community ties</mark> or what will likely happen to them post-deportation – <mark>to make</mark> their professional <mark>duties</mark> (emotionally and <mark>morally</mark>) <mark>easier</mark>. Nonknowledge production then is not necessarily intentionally deceitful or malicious; it can stem from conscious and unconscious coping mechanisms, everyday practices by bureaucrats as well as the structural configuration of states’ deportation apparatuses (Borrelli Citation2018). Rather than originating from all-powerful actors, the production of nonknowledge can be fragmented, ambivalent and complex, involving heterogeneous actors and technologies (Borrelli Citation2018; Perret Citation2024; Scheel Citation2024; Stel Citation2021).
<mark>Nonknowledge</mark> is <mark>embedded in</mark> structures of reporting and analytics on migration through processes of categorisation and quantification (Bradley Citation2023; Krause Citation2022), including the omission, compression, deflection and sanitisation of statistical <mark>data production</mark> (Rozakou Citation2017; Scheel and Ustek-Spilda Citation2019). This helps <mark>shape governance and legitimise</mark> specific <mark>policies</mark> and practices, such as how to categorise migrants, assess asylum claims and target enforcement strategies. Looking at the ‘deportation gap’, for example, Stephen Scheel (Citation2024) shows how specific statistical knowledge, produced through various sources and types of nonknowledge that stem from known data quality issues, is used to shape migration policy in Germany by <mark>producing narratives of concern</mark> over a perceived increase in the number of people issued deportation orders and those who leave, something he describes as a ‘statistical chimera’.
Nonknowledge through processes of obfuscating, hiding, discrediting and ignoring has also been mobilised to conceal and deny violent border practices, including ‘pushbacks’, physical abuse and death (Davies, Isakjee, and Obradovic-Wochnik Citation2023; Glouftsios Citation2023; Perret Citation2024). By ‘guarding truth claims, silencing unwanted voices, and shutting out perspectives that expose the injustice of the border itself’ (Davies, Isakjee, and Obradovic-Wochnik Citation2023, 169), state actors can better sustain liberal narratives of respecting human rights, dignity and the rule of law despite counterevidence (Isakjee et al. Citation2020; Stel Citation2021). This scholarship has pointed to the consequences of nonknowledge, particularly in creating a lack of accountability for how migrants are treated, in what Vicky Canning (Citation2018, 142) describes as ‘the capacity to orchestrate unaccountability for the harms (including deaths) inflicted on migrants’. Martina Tazzioli (Citation2020), for example, shows how the partial lack of data collection and circulation of data on migrants’ passage through the French-Italian borderlands is due, in part, to local authorities’ ambivalence towards their crossing as well as the will to ‘not govern too much’, facilitating unaccountability towards them and any rights violations. In this way, recording and officially knowing is a ‘double-edged sword’; it increases not only legibility but also culpability and responsibility (Kalir and van Schendel Citation2017, 2). Institutional ambiguity and nonknowledge can therefore maximise discretionary power and obfuscate liability (Stel Citation2020).
Nonknowledge also produces specific subjectivities enacted within colonial hierarchies that largely serve Western preferences and interests (Aradau and Perret Citation2022; Bradley Citation2024). Claudia Aradau and Sarah Perret (Citation2022), for instance, show how ‘errors’ and ‘fakes’, as distinct forms ofnonknowledge, shape different subjectivities within EU asylum practices. When authorities are unable to establish an asylum seeker’s identity this is categorised as an ‘error’, while the asylum seeker’s claim of statelessness or uncertainty about their national belonging is often perceived as a ‘fake’. This <mark>framing portrays migrantised individuals as</mark> inherently <mark>untrustworthy and deceptive while authorities</mark>, though fallible, are <mark>seen as ultimately committed to the pursuit of truth</mark>. These subjectivities inform how non/knowledge is differentially interpreted and conceptualised depending on who it comes from, processes that operate within racialised hierarchies <mark>reinforcing hegemonic power structures</mark> (Aradau and Perret Citation2022). This is also evident in the way systemic violence and ‘<mark>pushbacks’</mark> against migrantised people at EUrope’s borders <mark>are</mark> framed as aberrations, if not <mark>discredited altogether</mark> (BVMN Citation2022; Davies, Isakjee, and Obradovic-Wochnik Citation2023; Glouftsios Citation2023). The abrogation of responsibility is ever more glaring for <mark>EUropean states responsible for</mark> the conditions of <mark>inequality</mark>, as a result of varied <mark>colonial projects</mark>, <mark>imperial capitalism</mark> and warfare that <mark>cause people to move to Europe</mark> (Mayblin and Turner Citation2021). Yet, as we will demonstrate in the context of deportation, <mark>non/knowledge</mark> <mark>operates as a </mark>powerful <mark>governance tool enabling</mark> <mark>EU</mark>ropean <mark>states to make normative claims and discursive commitments to respecting rights</mark>, dignity and the rule of law <mark>while concealing historical</mark> and contemporary <mark>responsibilities towards</mark> <mark>migrant</mark>ised <mark>people</mark>.
While important research has analysed the production, purpose and consequences of knowledge and nonknowledge, less attention has been dedicated to its fluid dimensions, what we call non/knowledge production. This fluidity, we argue, further contributes to normalising hegemonic migration governance practices, such as deportation, by enabling state institutions and actors to respond and adapt to criticism by rights groups and legal frameworks without impeding larger political goals of increasing deportations. Specifically, we add to deportation literature by showing how the violence of deportation processes is maintained, in part, through the continuous production of non/knowledge. We suggest that a focus on improving deportation processes, specifically the institutional practices and enforcement of the forced removal, can give the impression of advancing fundamental and human rights while distracting from the larger harms deportation inflicts on people’s lives and the broader systems of exclusion, racism and state violence it is embedded. This allows for a deflection from both the structural and procedural violence of deportation and allows national police, immigration agencies and FRONTEX to promote narratives of concern and respect for human and fundamental rights while seeking to increase the number of people deported. Our contribution centres specifically on the necessary interplay between knowledge and nonknowledge in helping legitimise this system.
Drawing on this empirical work, we argue for an understanding of knowing and un-knowing as fluid, that is, co-constitutive, relational and contextual, shaped by heterogenous actors and evolving socio-political contexts. Such an understanding builds on recent work highlighting the complex and entangled processes of knowledge and nonknowledge production, and the need to move beyond static or dichotomous understandings of non/knowledge (Jones and Squire Citation2025; Perret Citation2024; Scheel Citation2024; Tazzioli Citation2020). Drawing on ignorance studies and socio-material approaches, Scheel (Citation2024) shows how specific types of knowledge are produced through complex, intertwined and multifarious processes of nonknowledge created through sociotechnical networks of human and non-human actors. In addition to illustrating the interconnectivity of knowledge and nonknowledge, this dispersed and often messy process of nonknowledge production helps avoid critiques of ‘conspiratorial logics’ (Scheel Citation2024, 4). Rather than being simply opposed, knowledge and nonknowledge are frequently entangled and juxtaposed in ways that contribute to the reproduction of dominant rationalities and practices at the EU’s borders (Perret Citation2025), even when they are not clearly linked to specific, intentional decisions to do so (Scheel Citation2024).
Specifically, we contribute to this scholarship by showing non/knowledge production as fluid along three key dimensions: co-constitution, relationality and contextuality. First, we argue that <mark>knowledge and nonknowledge are co-constitutive</mark>; they are mutually interdependent as they <mark>continuously</mark> co-produce and <mark>sustain each other</mark>. <mark>What is known and what</mark> remains <mark>un-known is</mark> therefore <mark>inherently connected and dynamic</mark>. Second, the production of <mark>non/knowledge</mark> is relational in that it <mark>involves</mark> multiple actors, including state agencies, international and non-governmental organisations, civil society, migrants and <mark>researchers</mark>, all of <mark>whom contribute to and contest what is recognised as knowledge</mark>. Third, knowledge and nonknowledge are contextual, <mark>continually redefined </mark>over time, space and socio-political contexts <mark>as</mark> political <mark>interests</mark>, regulations and policy imperatives <mark>evolve</mark>. While these dimensions of fluidity are all dynamic and intrinsically entangled, we attempt to separate them in our empirical section to better highlight how these processes operate in the context of deportation.

Methodology¶ The theoretical perspectives in this paper stem from a collaborative process involving intellectual exchange, in-depth discussions and data sharing, drawing on two of our research projects across multiple EUropean states that ended in 2022 and 2024, respectively. We combine several qualitative methods to capture the complexities of deportation processes. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with officials involved in migration control and individuals subjected to it, providing important insights into both group’s experiences and perspectives. Nicole interviewed 21 officials involved in carrying out or monitoring deportations in Austria, Finland, Greece, the Netherlands, Norway, Romania, Spain, Sweden and FRONTEX. Dorina interviewed 65 people awaiting deportation in Denmark and Norway, including some who had previously experienced deportation. Access to document requestsFootnote2 to FRONTEX also helped gather data on how monitors and the FRONTEX Fundamental Rights Office perceive deportation operations (e.g., Ostrand FOI PAD-2024-00271; PAD-2022-00451) and provided insight into the type of data the Agency does not collect, such as the number of families deported during FRONTEX supported operations (Ostrand FOI, PAD-2024-00273). This was supplemented by document analysis of relevant legal, policy and operational materials, including deportation monitoring reports by CPT, national monitoring bodies and the FRONTEX Fundamental Rights Office (FRO). Together, these methods offer a view of the process of deportation as well as how it is experienced by the various actors involved.¶ We also draw on data collected by the European network, UNITED for Intercultural Action, which has been collecting information on refugee deaths stemming from Europe’s exclusionary and ‘fatal’ policies since 1993. This data, compiled in the ‘UNITED List of Refugee Deaths’, summarises where, when and under what circumstances someone has died. It is amassed through UNITED’s own research, 550 network organisations in 48 countries and from local experts, journalists and researchers in the field of migration (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citationn.d.-b). The list is part of the campaign ‘Fatal Policies of Fortress Europe’, which aims to advocate for change by raising public awareness and denouncing the EU’s ‘exclusionist’ and ‘fatal’ policies, most notably by exposing the ‘human costs of (policymakers’) decisions’ (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citationn.d.-a). According to UNITED, ‘at least 60 000 refugee deaths can be attributed to the “Fatal Policies of Fortress Europe”’ between 1993 and 2023 (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citationn.d.-a).Footnote3 Since 2004, well over 129 of those deaths have been directly related to deportation.Footnote4¶ Our interest in deportation came in no small part due to our own position as migrants in EUropean. As scholars who have, at different times, experienced the realities of being both migrantised and occupying positions of privilege, we are acutely aware of the complex dynamics at play in discussions of deportation. Our personal histories, which have at moments brought us uncomfortably close to the deportation machine, inform our critical stance towards this practice. The experiences of participants in our research projects – particularly those rendered deportable – bring a sharper, more urgent focus to our critique.

We therefore <mark>approach our research as </mark>political <mark>actors enmeshed in the field of non/knowledge</mark> production and <mark>reject the idea that research can</mark> – <mark>or should</mark> – <mark>be neutral</mark>, <mark>detached</mark> <mark>or apolitical</mark>. Building on critical scholarship on migration and border practices (Canning Citation2018; Cházaro Citation2021; De Genova and Mae Peutz Citation2010; Noronha Citation2020; Garelli and Tazzioli Citation2013; Kalir Citation2019; Stierl Citation2022; Tazzioli 2024), we question deportation as a ‘legitimate’ state practice. Considering our research on deportation and its inherent structural and procedural violence, we believe there is a <mark>pressing need for alternative perspectives that challenge the dominant</mark> <mark>state narrative</mark> and reconsider the broader implications of deportation practices. Our aim here, however, is not to present the many arguments for an abolitionist stance; others have eloquently done so (e.g., Cházaro Citation2021; de Noronha Citation2022; Tazzioli Citation2024; Shah Citation2024), but to draw attention to the role of non/knowledge as a governance technique aimed at maintaining and legitimising the status quo. We believe this is an <mark>important step towards</mark> <mark>de-naturalising the</mark> current <mark>system that</mark> sees deportation as inevitable and appropriate, what Cházaro (Citation2021, 1043) <mark>calls its</mark> ‘<mark>legal and political common sense</mark>’.
In the following, we highlight how violence is produced through practices of deportation before describing the way non/knowledge helps obscure and legitimise it.
Deportation on the Non/Knowledge Continuum
Deportation as State Sanctioned Violence

Deportation is a form of state-sanctioned violence – a practice rooted in legal procedures and ‘constantly seen as “normal” and natural because it “is the law”’ (Menjívar and Abrego Citation2012, 1387) – which entails severe consequences for individuals, families, communities and even state actors involved in its implementation (Damsa Citation2023; Noronha Citation2020; Hasselberg Citation2016; Lindberg Citation2022). The rupture caused by deportation for the people subjected to it often occurs with little regard for individual circumstances or the harms it has on their lives and families, as illustrated in our research by the case of Anna, a woman who had lived in Norway for 12 years, had a Norwegian partner and children. Despite her ‘entire life’ being in Norway, Anna faced deportation and a two-year entry ban. She was left ‘at a loss’ about how to start over and cope with ‘forced separation’ from her children (Dorina, fieldnotes). For others, deportation not only means the loss of their families and life opportunities but can also pose serious threats to their lives, as we saw with Noor, the Muslim Moor minority woman deported to Sri Lanka (Dorina, fieldnotes). In the case of Farhad, who was shot by the Taliban after Germany deported him to Afghanistan, deportation was deadly (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citation2024). Tony also died after ‘years of psychological trauma’ and fear of deportation, while Tschianana and her newborn baby could not access the medical care they needed after deportation (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citation2024).¶ Women, and in our research, especially mothers who are sole providers for their children or extended families, like Eva, are particularly affected: ‘All I can do is sit in my cell and cry [waiting to be deported] and think of the things I can’t do for my children’. In the case of Saran, who moved to Norway three times to financially support her son, daughter-in-law, grandson and elderly father, she felt like she had no other options but to return despite being arrested and deported: ‘That’s how it started, right? Money problems, financial problems, again. I was a single mother, and my son was about to start school. So, I had to come back here, because this was my only chance, to come here and work (…) I had no one, no other choice, I had no money’ (Dorina, fieldnotes). Deportation is not an individual, finite incident but has long-lasting effects (Drotbohm and Hasselberg Citation2015; Hasselberg Citation2016).¶ As exemplified above, the devastating consequences of deportation for individuals, families and communities, as well as its structural violence embedded in broader systems of exclusion and racism, have been highlighted by scholars across various disciplines (Canning Citation2018; De Genova and Mae Peutz Citation2010; Noronha Citation2020; Hasselberg Citation2016; Kalir Citation2019). Our contribution to deportation scholarship is to highlight how the process of deportation is also inherently violent and how this violence is actively sustained and legitimised through the production of non/knowledge. By examining how knowledge and nonknowledge are entangled and mobilised, we show how the deportation process inflicts harm while concealing or downplaying that violence in the public sphere.¶ This procedural violence is present in routine and degrading practices, from strip-searchesFootnote5 where individuals are required to remove their clothes in front of state officials and, in some cases, have their genitals inspected (CPT Citation2023b; FRO Citation2024; Germany National Agency for the Prevention of Torture (NPT) Citation2023, 25), to being arrested at home in the middle of the night (European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA) Citation2023, 9; German NPT Citation2023), to the threat and humiliation of being shackled or physically restrained in front of others, including family and young children (FRO Citation2024, 9). The violence of deportation is even felt by the officers responsible for enforcing it and by the monitors who observe and report on deportation operations. It is not unheard of for officials to experience depression, trouble sleeping or even more acute mental health issues requiring them to take medical leave (Nicole, interviews N8, N20). As one officer explained, ‘everybody, I mean everybody, is dreading it. The returnees are sad (…). And the atmosphere is embedded onto the sadness of these people’ (Nicole, interview N20). Making people leave against their will can be emotionally and physically challenging work (CPT Citation2015, 11 and 22). This is especially the case when families with children are involved (Nicole, interviews N8, N11, N12, N16, N17), when officials know an individual’s personal history and are ‘directly confronted with their emotions’ (Nicole, interviews N11, N7) and when individuals have a strong reaction to deportation, like a panic attack, fainting, becoming extremely emotional (Nicole, interviews N18, N20) or engaging in self-harm: ‘(it is hard when there is) a guy who is desperate not to go back and does things. I mean once there was a guy who took out some sharp object and started cutting himself. There was blood all over the place. This is, this is so sad’ (Nicole, interview N14).¶ In EUropean, where liberal values and fundamental rights are often portrayed as foundational (FRA Citationn.d.) and part of the EU’s ‘spiritual and moral heritage’, non/knowledge is crucial for its continued justification (Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU Citation2012). As Nora Stel (Citation2021, 8) argues, drawing on Agnieszka Kubal‘s work (Citation2013), ‘it is exactly when authorities mobilize imaginaries of rights- or law-based governance’, such as in EUrope, ‘that we should step up our scrutiny as critical scholars’. FRONTEX is a clear example as it regularly frames the protection of fundamental rights as ‘unconditional’ and ‘essential’ to its border control and claims to ‘strictly adhere to the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the EU, the EUropean Convention on Human Rights, and relevant instruments of international and human rights law’ (Aas and Gundhus Citation2015; FRONTEX Citationn.d.), despite evidence to the contrary (BVMN Citation2022; Glouftsios Citation2023). Expressions of concern for respecting rights to life, dignity, integrity and nonrefoulment, then, can only be sustained if specific knowledge about the inherent violence in forcibly removing individuals from communities is ignored, including recorded suicides and other deaths from deportation due to persecution, dangerous living conditions or inadequate healthcare in the state of deportation (Fekete Citation2015; UNITED for Intercultural Action Citation2024). As Angelica Cházaro (Citation2021, 1083) rightly observes, violence is not an aberration but an essential feature of deportation.¶ The Fluidity of Non/Knowledge: Co-Constitution, Relationality and Contextuality¶ Despite this violence, deportation remains a ‘legal and political common sense’ – where there is general acceptance of its legitimacy and a consensus that some level of deportation is inevitable and necessary, even among migrant advocates (Cházaro Citation2021, 1043). This ‘common sense’ is enabled, we argue, by the fluidity of non/knowledge. What is known and un-known represents a co-constitutive process, entangled in socio-political contexts, with a multitude of actors seeking to influence how deportation is enforced. This is evident, for example, in the way national police and FRONTEX were forced to modify their practices and procedures following horrific deaths and serious injuries during deportation in the 1990s through mid-2010s, and due to the sustained pressure by rights organisations, the media and public that followed (Ostrand Citationforthcoming). It resulted in Article 8(6) of Return Directive (Citation2008/115/EC; Procter and Schiebinger Citation2008), requiring member states to have monitoring systems, the eventual institutionalisation of ‘forced return monitoring’ in many EUropean states and FRONTEX (FRA Citation2023), as well as dedicated training for officers on human and fundamental rights, new guidelines, revised codes of conduct and the establishment of the FRONTEX Fundamental Rights Officer (FRO) and Consultative Forum on Fundamental Rights (Nicole, interviews N1, N18, N20). As officials saw it, ‘something had to be done, so it was done’ (N14): ‘damning reports’ by rights groups and media made national police and FRONTEX recognise that they needed to invest more in fundamental rights and safeguards (Nicole, interviews N20, N18, N8, N6). In this way, the social-political environment, including sustained pressure by civil society actors, created institutional incentives for national and EU agencies to portray themselves as more effectively protecting fundamental and human rights during deportation, while ignoring and obscuring potential failings. It required the production of new non/knowledge. In the following, we illustrate the fluidity of non/knowledge by focusing specifically on its three dimensions: co-constitutive, relational and contextual.¶ Co-Constitution¶ National agencies and FRONTEX’ ability to maintain narratives of concern and respect for fundamental and human rights during deportation entails the concurrent production of evidence – such as monitoring reports, increased documentation when physical force is used and medical certificates to ensure individuals’ ‘fitness to travel’ (Nicole, interviews N4, N18)Footnote6 – and ignorance – such as discounting documented deaths and severe harms caused by deportation, whether due to suicide, separated families, financial insecurity, persecution, dangerous living conditions or inadequate healthcare in the country of deportation. The fluidity of non/knowledge then renders the possibility for violence, death and renouncing responsibility to exist, whilst maintaining a veneer of humanity and care.¶ One such empirical case is represented by practices designed to better protect individual’s safety and right to life during deportation used by most EUropean statesFootnote7: having medical professionals present during the deportation and conducting medical assessment to ensure individuals are ‘fit-to-travel’, meaning their ‘medical condition (both physical and mental) which allows the person to travel safely’.Footnote8 In the case of FRONTEX, these practices have been required since 2013 (CPT Citation2015, 12) and create an ‘orchestrated unaccountability’ (Canning Citation2018) by insulating the Agency against responsibility:¶ […] for every person (member states) had to do a (medical) check. So, if something was really wrong later (…) (FRONTEX) could revert to the member state and say this person was not fit-to-fly. And then that state was clearly liable for whatever: Lawsuits from their family, from the returnee, from whatever. So, it was clearly, clearly a responsibility of the (national) delegation. Because, you know, at the end as well, health was a very important part of the flight. So, (FRONTEX also) made it compulsory to have a doctor in the flight. (Nicole, interview N18)¶ FRONTEX’s requirement that EUropean states participating in FRONTEX coordinated deportation perform medical checks prior to deportation produces both knowledge and nonknowledge. The ‘fit-to-travel’ certificate provides documentation that individuals are ‘healthy’ enough to fly. Yet, as we shall see, they can conceal considerable information about an individual’s actual health, leaving FRONTEX largely ignorant about whether a person is actually safe to be deported. Such intertwinement of knowing and unknowing allows FRONTEX to demonstrate concern and accountability for individuals’ safety while simultaneously diffusing accountability to states.¶ The aim of ‘fit-to-travel’ checks is to safeguard the ‘wellbeing and health of returnees’ and ‘avoid incidents that could result in the health of returnees being jeopardized’ (International Centre for Migration Policy Development (ICMPD Citation2021, 82). This type of knowledge production, however, is fraught with challenges. In Cyprus, the ‘fit-to-travel’ assessment is based on ‘an evaluation of the personal medical files’, and an exam is only carried out when ‘the medical file reveals a serious medical condition possibly affecting the person’s fitness to travel (CPT Citation2023, 11; Eule et al. Citation2023, 11). Reliance on files requires them to be accurate, complete and reflect relevant medical conditions, something that is far from always the case. During a monitoring visit of Cyprus, CPT found medical notes to be ‘cursory, at times consisting of no more than one single word’, omitting relevant details about injuries, including its size and location, and missing known relevant information, like pre-existing psychiatric illnesses (CPT Citation2023, 11). In fact, CPT found one woman with two separate files where ‘(n)one of the medical information from the first medical file found its way into the second file, and no cross reference was made in either file’ (CPT Citation2023, 11). In another case, CPT witnessed a violent incident that required a trip to the hospital; yet, the following day, there was no reference to the incident in the medical file of the person concerned. Furthermore, the substance of the medical assessment in Cyprus, CPT concluded, doesn’t cover the range of ‘pathologies enumerated in the International Air Transport Association (IATA) flight inadmissibility criteria, or the need to specifically assess the risks associated with possible prolonged use of means of restraint, in particular in confined spaces such as an aircraft. Therefore, completing a basic medical examination upon admission does not automatically mean that the person concerned will be fit-to-fly if they are removed’ (CPT Citation2023, 11).¶ Even when adequate information and safeguards are taken with respect to individuals’ ‘fitness-to-travel’, the narrow focus on their health during the deportation hides the often-considerable nonknowledge and disregard by state agencies on how someone’s health and well-being may be affected by deportation. Noor, who we mentioned before as awaiting deportation to Sri Lanka, suffered from clinical depression and suicidal ideation and had been receiving prescription medication. Upon return to Sri Lanka, she feared she would lose access to potentially life-saving treatment. Her concerns are far from isolated (Dorina, fieldnotes). Other documented cases have shown deportation from Norway and Switzerland led to deaths as the individuals deported were unable to access necessary medical care in Djibouti and Guinea for their pre-existing and serious health conditions (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citation2024). In Germany, a 20-year-old also died due to the lack of medical care for his severe illness, and in Switzerland, a 17-year-old boy committed suicide in a shelter for unaccompanied asylum seekers, fearing deportation back to Afghanistan (UNITED for Intercultural Action Citation2024). Case-law by the European Court of Human Rights has also held that deportation of ‘seriously ill persons’ may violate Article 3 on the prohibition of torture, inhuman and degrading treatment (see inter alia Paposhvili v. Belgium, 2016 and K.S. v. Sweden, 2018; for limits on this see Savran v. Denmark, 2021).¶ These cases expose the significant limitations of ‘fitness-to-travel’ documentation, which fails to capture the long term risks deportees face prior to and upon return. While recorded deaths such as those documented by United for Intercultural Action highlight the gravest consequences, they represent only a small fraction of the harm caused to individuals subjected to deportation. Beyond death, they may suffer from (untreated) depression, anxiety, physical disabilities and other medical conditions that can worsen due to separation from family, financial precarity and inadequate healthcare post-deportation. The narrow focus on health during periods of forced confinement and deportation, when the state is most easily held accountable, belies genuine concern for individuals’ health and well-being. After all, state actors are often aware of the violence caused by deportation, even if they disregard or ignore it. Despite being advised not to pick up individuals and families unannounced in the middle of the night as it can result in trauma, especially for children and individuals who have already experienced trauma, Germany continues to routinely use this practice, regardless of who is affected (FRA Citation2023, 9; Germany NPT Citation2023, 15). In Cyprus, despite persistent allegations of ill treatment and injuries when a deportation is aborted, and CPT’s recommendation that individuals receive a medical examination when this happens, Cypriot authorities have refused to do so, denying that such information is necessary (CPT Citation2023, 8).¶ The inherent violence and degradation embedded in deportation processes cannot be fully mitigated by increased recording or safeguarding measures. Authorities oscillate between knowing and un-knowing, expressing ‘concern’ for a person’s well-being during the deportation, at least in some cases, while showing ignorance prior to deportation and once the individual is returned to their country. Non/knowledge production then serves to help legitimise the deportation process. ‘Fit-to-travel’ certificates, as we have seen, are designed to demonstrate individuals are healthy enough to be deported, but often hide as much as they reveal, neglecting inadequacies of the medical checks and the longer term health risks or concerns once the deportation is complete. This process does not necessarily result from conscious or malicious intent by individuals but operates instead within a broader structural framework where certain facts are emphasised while other information is ignored. It nevertheless largely supports the interests of FRONTEX and national agencies to claim ‘we have done our best’ when it comes to individuals’ rights (Nicole, interview N6).¶ Non/knowledge, as shown here, is necessarily co-constitutive, representing a continuum of interwoven processes that must be examined together. When viewed through the lens of fluidity we see knowledge and nonknowledge sustain each other to create specific narratives. The fluidity of non/knowledge serves as a mechanism that perpetuates harm under the guise of concern. This helps reveal how more knowledge and evidence of state’s ‘concern’ for individuals’ rights cannot necessarily fix a fundamentally flawed system. You cannot remove the indignity and humiliation of strip searches, for example, by recording more, as is often suggested by monitoring bodies: ‘if a strip-search is carried out, the reasons for this should be documented in a clear and comprehensible manner’ and ‘should be carried out as respectfully as possible’ (Germany NPT Citation2023, 25). While such documentation and change in procedures may make state actors and institutions feel better about the violence they are enacting, it does little for the people subjected to it. The interplay of non/knowledge is therefore crucial for understanding how EUropean state actors and institutions can maintain a veneer of legitimacy and liberal self-imagery when deporting individuals and families.¶ Contextuality and Relationality¶ The non/knowledge that sustains these narratives and limited accountability develop within social-political contexts co-produced by a multiplicity of state and non-state actors operating within evolving legal and policy landscapes. As discussed above, fundamental and human rights have, at times, operated as important legitimising frameworks for migration and border governance in EUrope (see also Aas and Gundhus Citation2015; Lemberg-Pedersen Citation2021; Pallister-Wilkins Citation2015). This context has created opportunities for actors seeking to improve the conditions of individuals and families subjected to deportation, such as legal and medical professionals, psychologists, rights organisations and NGOs, to advance reforms by mobilising rights frameworks. One clear example is in the production of ‘good practices’ when it comes to deporting children – what one official called ‘the future in forced returns’ (Nicole, interview N16). Knowledge about what is considered ‘good practices’, which are intended to reduce the trauma and harm caused by deportation for children, are produced through an entanglement of laws, norms, bureaucratic procedures and actors, including enforcement officers, psychologists, fundamental and human rights experts and advocacy groups. A 2020 workshop on children in deportation, for example, involved enforcement officers and monitors from 21 EUropean states, the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA), PICUM, ICMPD and FRONTEX. The aim was to discuss how best to operationalise and implement relevant legal frameworks and guidelines when it comes to monitoring and enforcing deportations involving children, bringing together a range of state and non-state actors, inter/national laws and state procedures to develop knowledge on ‘good practices’.Footnote9¶ These practices are also increasingly viewed as needing to be ‘research-based’ and drawn from professional knowledge (Nicole, interview N21). FRONTEX, for instance, touts the development of its ‘Toolbox for Children in Return’ created with ‘child psychologists and child education experts, as well as in consultation with the Agency’s Fundamental Rights Office and the Consultative Forum (FRONTEX Citation2022). The ostensibly good intentions of protecting children’s rights and reducing trauma do not, however, prevent states from deporting them, nor does it wholly remove the violence of deportation regardless of how ‘good’ the practices are. One monitor emphasised this when explaining why she ‘hates’ flights with families, describing how the only time she had to step away from her role was during a flight with two little boys even though ‘everything was carried out very child friendly, and with a lot of care (…) and also there were people present from social intuitions, and social workers and NGOs’ (Nicole, interview N8). Still, ‘(t)he parents were petrified, and the children were puzzled, and they were trying to plead’ in German with the police about how they were ‘good kids’ and ‘didn’t do something bad’ (idem).¶ The fluidity of non/knowledge when it comes to the violence of deportation is also evident in the use of knowledge from trauma psychology to help inform how to reduce harmful stress when deporting children. In describing one such practice, an official explained how police entering a home should view their actions ‘as a potential crisis for the family’ and try to modify their behaviour to appear less threatening, like wearing civilian clothes (Nicole, interview N21). The irony of the state manufacturing the very trauma it is trying to alleviate requires a certain level of ignorance enabled by the acceptance that some level of deportation is inevitable and appropriate, even for families with children born in the country they are being forcibly removed from. This ‘common sense’ (Cházaro Citation2021) is facilitated by professionals, scholars, and rights organisations, however well intentioned, who mobilise ‘expert’ knowledge and ‘epistemic authority’ in pursuit of making deportation as humane as possible. Such reforms add a veneer of legitimacy and help FRONTEX and national agencies maintain narratives of individuals’ rights and well-being while shielding against critique, allowing them to claim ‘we have done our best’: ‘this was consulted. We have monitors (…) therefore it should be according to (human and fundamental rights) standards’ (Nicole, interview N6).¶ As this example illustrates, influential rights organisations (e.g., FRA, European Council on Refugees and Exiles and Save the Children), professionals, and researchers can play a significant role in shaping non/knowledge surrounding deportation. This has led to changes in know-how on how children and families should be treated during the process, including the provision of child-appropriate food, toys and diapers (Nicole, interviews N8; N10; FRO Citation2022, Citation2023). Yet, such actors who, to varying degrees, accept the inevitability of deportation are largely limited in their ability to seek transformative change, confined to focusing on reforming and adding safeguards to this coercive state practice. Improving the process, rather than questioning the legitimacy of deportation itself, can contribute to the normalisation and technocratic management of deportation, bolstering the legitimacy of the process and rights-respecting institutional narratives (De Genova Citation2002, De Genova and Mae Peutz Citation2010; Kalir Citation2019).¶ Other actors’ knowledge, namely those who reject the legitimacy of deportation on an individual or systemic level, such as migrantised individuals and their families, communities opposing deportation, activist groups, artists and critical scholars, hold little influence within hegemonic migration governance frameworks that rely on institutionalised and state-sanctioned expertise to justify deportation as a legitimate practice. Critical scholars, activists and artists have documented people’s accounts and how their experiences within the deportation regime are systematically marginalised in the production of knowledge (De Genova and Mae Peutz Citation2010; Golash-Boza Citation2017; Ortiz Citation2018). Their contributions remain largely peripheral to policy conversations, as politicians, policymakers and state actors involved in implementation frequently disregard or downplay inconvenient scientific and expert knowledge, despite ‘evidence-based policy making’ being a ‘buzzword in migration policy circles’ (Natter and Welfens Citation2024, 1617). Rather, such epistemic exclusion reinforces a knowledge hierarchy that privileges state-aligned expertise and reflects a broader political environment designed to maintain this violent system by ignoring the accounts of those who directly experience its effects.

Brexit as nostalgia for empire
The 2016 <mark>referendum</mark> on Britain’s EU membership should be understood as <mark>another in a long line of assertions of white entitlement</mark> to the spoils of colonialism. The Leave vote <mark>should not be exceptionalised as an object of study</mark>, or collapsed into the meaningless hashtag that is ‘Brexit’, but <mark>instead understood in</mark> the context of Britain’s colonial history and <mark>ongoing colonial configuration</mark>. Exceptionalising the referendum result <mark>distracts from</mark> the <mark>structural forces underlying it</mark>. The terms on which the referendum debate took place are symptomatic of a Britain struggling to conceive of its place in the world post-Empire. Present in the discourse of some of those arguing for a Leave vote was a tendency to romanticise the days of the British Empire, despite widespread amnesia about the details of Britain’s imperial history. A 2016 poll conducted six months prior to the referendum found that 44 per cent of the British public were proud of Britain’s colonial history and 43 per cent considered the British Empire to have been a good thing. In 2011 David Cameron, then Conservative Prime Minister, stated that ‘Britannia didn’t rule the waves with armbands on’. Before him, former Labour Prime Minister, Tony Blair, stated in 1997 that he valued and honoured British history enormously and considered that the British Empire should neither elicit ‘apology nor hand-wringing’ and that it should be deployed to enhance Britain’s global influence.
The hankering after the halcyon days of empire was expressed in a tabloid headline following the referendum, which read, ‘Now Let’s Make Britain Great Again’. The slogan, popular in the course of Donald Trump’s presidential election campaign, has since been adopted by some of those who backed a Leave vote. The idea of ‘making Britain great again’ captures a yearning for a time when ‘Britannia ruled the waves’ and was defined by its racial and cultural superiority. The vote to leave the EU is not only an expression of nostalgia for empire, it is also the fruit of empire. The legacies of British colonialism have never been addressed, including that of racism. British colonial rule saw the exploitation of peoples, the theft of their land, their subjugation on the basis of a white supremacist racial hierarchy, a system that was maintained through the brutal and systematic violence of colonial authorities.
The British Empire was vehemently resisted by local populations in colonised territories. As Richard Gott writes, there was always ‘resistance to conquest, and rebellion against occupation, often followed by mutiny and revolt – by individuals, groups, armies and entire peoples. At one time or another, the British seizure of distant lands was hindered, halted and even derailed by the vehemence of local opposition’. Priyamvada Gopal has challenged the discourse prevalent in Britain that credits anti-imperial resistance to the liberal imperialist project. This project presents freedom from slavery and imperialism as having flowed from the ‘benevolence of the rulers’ and having been granted to colonies  ‘when they were deemed ready for it’. Gopal’s counter history destroys this narrative, demonstrating instead how enslaved and colonised peoples were the agents of their own resistance and freedom. They also shaped British discourse on liberation, not only through scholarship but also in the form of struggle and insurgency thereby ‘interrogating the tenacious assumption that the most significant conceptions of ‘freedom’ are fundamentally ‘Western’ in provenance’.
Paul Gilroy has argued that imperial nostalgia is sometimes combined with ‘a reluctance to see contemporary British racism as a product of imperial and colonial power’. The prevalence of structural and institutional racism in Britain today made it fertile ground for the effectiveness of the Leave campaign’s rhetoric of ‘taking back control’ and reaching ‘breaking point’. The Leave victory on 23 June 2016 resulted in a renewed level of legitimisation of racism and white supremacy. In Britain, a week prior to the referendum, pro-immigration Labour MP, Jo Cox, was brutally murdered by a man who shouted ‘Britain first’ as he killed her, and who gave his name in court on being charged with her murder as ‘Death to traitors. Freedom for Britain’. In the months following the referendum, racist hate crime increased by 16% across Britain, and peaked at a 58% rise in the week following the vote. Weeks after the referendum, Arkadiusz Jóźwik was beaten to death in Essex, having reportedly been attacked for speaking Polish in the street.
In an article that has proved remarkably prophetic Vron Ware argued in 2008 that ‘societies where whiteness has historically conferred some sort of guarantee of belonging and entitlement present an opportunity for political mobilisation in the name of white supremacy’. The vote to exit the EU signified a shoring up of white entitlement to territory, resources, and to identification as British. Despite the frequent refrain that the 2016 vote to leave the European Union was the result of a disenfranchised ‘white working class’ revolt, Gurminder Bhambra has observed that the vote was disproportionately carried by ‘the propertied, pensioned, well-off, white middle class based in southern England’. According to Danny Dorling, 52% of Leave voters lived in southern England and 59% were middle class. The proportion of people who voted Leave in the lowest social classes was just 24%. The Leave campaign’s violent rhetoric of ‘taking back control’ is thus symptomatic of an imagined victimhood on the part of white nationalists, in part a backlash against the gradual gains made in the realm of civil rights and non-discrimination on the part of minorities living in white hegemonic societies over the past decades. James McDougall and Kim Wagner have pointed out that Enoch Powell’s ‘notorious line about the descendants of the enslaved and colonised gaining “the whip hand over the white man” was an early indication of the fantasy of victimhood that grip’s today’s alt-right and feeds hostility to migration and multiculturalism’.
In reality, colonial conquest historically benefited poor white Britons, as well as the ruling classes. As Danny Dorling and Sally Tomlinson note, poor white Britons ‘were encouraged to believe in their economic, political and racial superiority to the rest of the subjects of empire. This helped deflect attention from their own precarious position in the economic structure’. Further, their wages could rise because there were colonial populations to exploit. Aditya Mukherjee has shown how accumulation by colonial dispossession allowed for the betterment of economic conditions for the entirety of the metropole, including poor white people.
The process of primitive accumulation in capitalism or the initial phase of industrialisation is a painful one as the initial capital for investment has to be raised on the backs of the working class or the peasantry. To the extent that Britain and other metropolitan countries were able to draw surplus from the colonial people, to that extent they did not have to draw it from their own working class and peasantry. That is one reason why colonialism is supra-class, it is not only the metropolitan bourgeoisie exploiting the colonial proletariat but the metropolitan society as a whole benefiting at the cost of the entire colonial people.
Britain’s impending departure from the EU now sees it turning once again to the Commonwealth. It is <mark>no coincidence</mark> that Nigel <mark>Farage</mark>, MEP and former leader of the United Kingdom Independence Party (UKIP), <mark>expressed</mark> a <mark>preference for migrants from India</mark> and Australia <mark>as compared with East Europeans</mark>, and has advocated stronger ties with the Commonwealth. Theresa May, in her speech on the government’s plans for Britain’s departure from the EU, referred to the Commonwealth as being indicative of Britain’s ‘unique and proud global relationships’, and declared it was ‘<mark>time for Britain to</mark> get out into the world and <mark>rediscover its role as a</mark> great, <mark>global</mark>, trading <mark>nation’</mark>. At the same time, paradoxically, spokespersons of the Leave campaign adopted the language of ‘national liberation’ resulting in the reframing of Britain’s voluntary decision to leave the EU, an organisation it willingly chose to join, as ‘an existential struggle between oppressor and oppressed’. In reality, since the Leave vote, Britain’s appetite for imperial mythology and fantasy has grown. Boris Johnson, former Conservative Foreign Secretary and current Prime Minister, claimed in his first major speech following the referendum that, ‘Brexit emphatically does not mean a Britain that turns in on herself’. As he romanticised Britain’s long history of intervention in Afghanistan, he euphemistically descried British colonialism in terms of, ‘astonishing globalism, this wanderlust of aid workers and journalists and traders and diplomats and entrepreneurs’. He wondered whether, ‘the next generation of Brits will be possessed of the same drive, the same curiosity, the same willingness to take risks for far flung peoples and places’. Johnson’s dressed up Britain’s colonial history as being that of a small island nation’s attempt to do good in the world. He promised that a post-Brexit Britain will be ‘more outward-looking and more engaged with the world than ever before.’
Yet, the prospect of Britain retaining its status as the leading global power in the face of the defeat of its empire was presented by British politicians in 1961 as a major reason in favour of joining the EEC. Britain’s profound colonial amnesia and imperial ambition now see it making a drastic manoeuvre away from the EU. Considering <mark>the Leave vote’s</mark> <mark>embodiment of a rejection of anyone considered to be a migrant</mark>, a post-Brexit Britain promises to be a dangerous place for racialised people and those without a secure status. <mark>We urgently need new strategies for organising collectively in the</mark> <mark>service of</mark> anti-racist and <mark>migrant solidarity</mark>.

I. THE CRITIQUE OF COMPARATIVE LAW
In his essay, “The Two Jameses at Play,” Pierre Legrand stages a powerful intervention in comparative legal scholarship.27 He identifies two currents within the discipline: a traditional mainstream, which Legrand associates with scientific positivism and disciplined reporting of legal rules; and a livelier countercurrent, characterized by contextualized study of legal rules, more active interpretation, and interdisciplinary engagement. Legrand is deeply critical of the former; the latter, he embraces and recommends as the path forward for comparative law. His essay, an exceptionally beautiful contribution to legal scholarship, is itself a testament to the generative value of thoughtful interdisciplinary engagement. Though styled primarily as adjudication of a famous disagreement between prominent figures in comparative law, each representing a particular set of methodological commitments, I read Legrand’s essay as an inspired call to imagine a new and emancipated comparative law—one that might provide a home to decolonizing critique, as I suggest in the following section.28
Legrand begins by identifying what has always been the core value of a comparative approach to legal study: by inclining its practitioners towards the foreign and unfamiliar, comparativism becomes a way of suspending “self-centric and self-satisfied normality”; by disenchanting our most sacred institutions and disrupting intellectual routines, comparativism becomes a practice of repositioning oneself in the world and history.29 The comparativist’s commitment to honor difference, to represent the foreign with fidelity, enables her to resist both the “nationalizing and universalizing” impulses that govern so much legal scholarship.30 Comparative law, Legrand writes, “is informed by an engagement with the world’s legal disparateness,” which it regards “as beneficial and indeed as normatively relevant.”31 But Legrand worries that as comparative law scholars become preoccupied with policing what is or is not a proper object of study—what it will or will not recognize to be “the law”—they lose sight of the intellectual and ethical value of comparative methods.
In what is a remarkably impassioned essay, Legrand confesses, “I have long felt profound intellectual discomfort and frustration that my professional existence should have been unfolding within a field that, in the main, has tended somewhat blatantly to keep even the most basic epistemic interrogations very safely out of the ambit of scholarly investigation.”32 His is an effort to revitalize comparative study, “to contribute to the redemption of epistemology within comparative law”33 by insisting upon a heightened self-reflection within the discipline. He urges his colleagues in comparative law to think critically about the conventions of knowledge-production that define the discipline, and the intellectual habits that allow only certain questions to be raised while banishing others.
Legrand asserts that comparative legal scholarship is afflicted by an excessive positivism, characterized by a narrowed focus on authorized legal texts and a committed indifference to almost everything else.34 In his words, according to the reigning orthodoxy, the comparative study of law should “remain squarely set on the rules—on what has been posited by authorized officials as ‘what the law is.’”35 These, in turn, should be represented in as “scientific” a manner as possible. This understanding of the discipline has encouraged comparative <mark>law scholars</mark> to <mark>produce</mark> dutifully descriptive <mark>accounts</mark> of cases and statutes, <mark>uncontaminated by</mark> either “<mark>political</mark> commitment <mark>or personal investment</mark>,” <mark>uncluttered by</mark> references to historical or <mark>cultural context, and free of</mark> authorial <mark>interpretation</mark>, critical or otherwise.36 They regard their task as one of “arrang[ing] the law in the form of an orderly, coherent, and systematic representation of the different rules in force, largely applying them at the behest of the state.”37
Legrand is appropriately skeptical of the positivist’s pretensions to scientific objectivity. Positivism, he argues, can only fulfill its claim to pure objectivity by denaturing its object. In the positivist’s construction, law is made to appear rational and coherent; law is cleansed of its essential ambiguity, indeterminacy, and inconsistency. Positivists attribute to law a “brand of fixity or invariance of meaning that is more readily associated with the Pythagorean theorem or with the laws on thermodynamics” than a product of human creation and contestation.38 By imagining that man-made laws operate as naturally or with the same autonomy as the Pythagorean theorem, positivists invest their own science of legal reasoning with a transcendental quality while effacing the contests and contingencies that have allowed legal reasoning to proceed as a self-authorizing force.
Just as positivists attribute to their law-object an internal purity, they assume that legal objects come with clear external boundaries. In their view, law is clearly distinguishable from not-law.39 As Legrand writes, positivists “regard the legal aspect of an issue as discrete and as crisply detachable from its other dimensions.”40 But here, too, positivist legal discourse maintains its conceptual clarity only by suppressing historical contingencies, cultural contaminations, and so on. As Legrand writes, positivists arrive at their account of “what the law is” through a process of subtraction: they “evacuat[e] . . . all markers of ambivalence [and] censor the world of culture, that is, they subtract from the law cues that, as they approach the matter, would interfere with law as law and would detract from its conceptual merit and practical worthiness as law.”41 But by refusing to look beyond the usual parameters of legal discourse, to explore the conditions that give rise to its knowledge conventions, the positivist fails to acknowledge the contingency of both his object and method of study
The positivism that Legrand describes also participates in a fallacy that has sustained colonial reasoning since its beginning—the Cartesian fallacy through which the knower knows himself to be detached from the known world. The contemporary philosopher, Achille Mbembe, explains that western epistemic traditions, to which positivism belongs, rest on “a division between mind and the world, or between reason and nature as an ontological a priori.” 42 Mbembe explains that these “are traditions in which the knowing subject is enclosed in itself and peeks out at the world of objects and produces supposedly objective knowledge of those objects. The knowing subject is thus able, we are told, to know the world without being part of that world.”43 The problem with this tradition, Mbembe argues, is that “it has become hegemonic.” On the one hand, “it has generated discursive scientific practices and has set up interpretive frames that make it difficult to think outside of these frames”; on the other hand, “it actively represses anything that actually is articulated, thought and envisioned from outside of these frames.”44
For Legrand, too, the most concerning feature of positivism in <mark>legal scholarship</mark> is that it maintains its authority by excluding challenge. It <mark>safeguards</mark> its <mark>rationality</mark> and coherence <mark>by</mark> denying and <mark>devaluing</mark> certain forms of <mark>inquiry</mark>— particularly those that are most disruptive of its internal logic and order. As Legrand writes, “certain <mark>knowledge is banished from</mark> the <mark>sphere of significance</mark>, and some issues are made never to arise, therefore allowing for . . . an ultimately immaculate development of internal heuristic processes generating ultimately immaculate legal results.”45 By insulating itself from outside challenge, by refusing to question its own working assumptions, positivism within legal discourse becomes an entirely self-referencing and self-replicating epistemic regime.46 It replicates itself in law schools by cultivating a particular mindset in new students. As Legrand puts it:
The positivist mind channels the energies of law schools and of law teachers into the career preparation of students in a manner that ensures the solidification and the perpetuation of the existing institutional order through the annual “delivery” of sets of mentally homogenous and socially receptive individuals pliably disposed to apprehend the law as consisting in a set of textual commands requiring but (sophisticated?) technical mastery.47
The students of color who resist sitting for their criminal law exams while police killing continues unabated and unredressed—those students escape Legrand’s description. Those students explain that they did not want to participate in the ideological reproduction of the status quo by mastering rules that, for instance, allow police officers to use deadly force against innocent and unarmed men and women.
But there are many other law students who do fit Legrand’s description. For instance, last year, students at one American law school complained to their criminal law professor that the statement “Black Lives Matter,” worn on her tshirt, “was an inappropriate and unnecessary statement that has no legitimate place within our institution of higher learning.”48 In an anonymous letter that has since been widely circulated, the students explained, “We are here to learn the law”:
This is not a political science class or college. We are a law school. We have undertaken the solemn duty to learn and respect the law. We do not need the mindless actions of our professors to distract and alienate us. Just as our personal beliefs have no place in law exams, your personal beliefs have no place in the classroom.”
The <mark>students’</mark> confidence that law school has nothing to do with politics or personal belief; that they can <mark>learn </mark>criminal <mark>law without having to be distracted by assertions about black humanity</mark>; that as the “solemn” keepers of the profession, they owe nothing to those who challenge its effects—these are all symptoms of the epistemic enclosure of which Legrand warns. History, other people, and conscience—these become someone else’s problem
Notwithstanding its claim to objectivity and ideological innocence, Legrand assures us that positivism is always political.51 There is, of course, a politics to sanctioning indifference by policing disciplinary boundaries. In the United States, as in other contexts, <mark>racial inequality</mark> is <mark>sustained by</mark> historical amnesia and, often, by an <mark>unexamined faith in</mark> the <mark>essential goodness of law</mark>. Positivists promote the view that law has within itself the resources to gradually perfect itself, “through the self-regulatory and teleologically ordained use of the posited.”52 In this sense—though Legrand is perhaps more understated in his account—positivism in legal discourse tends uncomfortably towards authoritarianism. It is more concerned with a respect for governing authority than it is with the expression of governed collectivities.
To redeem the comparative study of law, Legrand argues, comparativists should muddy the pristine waters of the positivist mainstream by engaging in two kinds of dredging—what he calls “enculturation” and interpretation. 53 The comparative law scholar should free himself of the positivist’s demand for certainty and embrace the essential unruliness of legal texts and legal culture. He should abandon the notion that law can be purified of its internal deficits (ambiguities, inconsistencies, and indeterminacies) or cleaved of its external contaminations (of history and culture) and instead recognize law to be “a massively incorporative cultural formation.”
Legal texts, like all other texts, Legrand writes, “exist as intertextual matrices . . . as interfaces where arrays of discursive threads have interlaced to be absorbed and transformed in order to be made to speak legally.”55 The fabric of any legal text consists of “historical configurations enmeshed with traces of political rationalities intertwined with traces of social logics interwoven with traces of philosophical postulates plaited with traces of linguistic orders darned with traces of economic prescriptions interlaced with traces of epistemic assumptions,” and so on.56 While descriptive positivism limits itself to endlessly reinscribing a legal text with a particular significance, encultured interpretation opens the text to re-signification. Legrand reminds us that the meaning of a legal text is never exhausted by its authorized or official interpretation. On the contrary, when confronted with multiplicity of meanings contained in any legal text, the scholar is confronted with an inescapable choice, an ethical judgment. He can either attend to the difference, the newness, or the challenge presented by the trace, and thereby “do justice” to it. Or he can ignore it. But by <mark>refusing to address</mark> the <mark>hidden trace</mark> discovered in a legal text—the trace <mark>of an imperial logic</mark>, for instance, <mark>or</mark> older <mark>racial episteme</mark>—the scholar <mark>participates in</mark> its <mark>silencing and erasure</mark>.

Policy debate is a fiercely competitive speech activity where two teams of two participants argue in front of a judge who then adjudicates one team as the victor, the other the loser, and assigns all four debaters “speaker points” based on individual performance. While policy debate is a global phenomenon practiced in many formats and academic contexts, all of which merit serious scholarly inquiry, I will focus my attention specifically on nationallevel inter-collegiate policy debate in the U.S. This focus encapsulates not only a single game but a community of participants which overlaps with and includes many smaller identity communities.
