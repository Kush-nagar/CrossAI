New report warns the EU and UK approaches to delivering a circular economy are fast-diverging, leaving businesses facing
complex rules and rising costs
Few areas of environmental policy are more challenging than the circular economy. Global emissions may still be rising, but climate action is a priority for governments
all around the world and clean technologies are providing a roadmap towards a net zero emission economy. Nature may be under siege worldwide, but there are international targets to
reverse biodiversity loss and inspiring projects that show how quickly nature recovery can take root. The idea of a more circular economy that brings an end to our throwaway culture may be
popular with the public, but policymakers rarely prioritise the issue, efforts to boost circularity have to reckon with the remarkable convenience of linear buy-use-throw resource
models, and businesses remain wary of changes that could threaten consumer-driven growth plans.
The scale of the challenge is laid bare by soaring levels of waste, stubbornly low recycling rates, and the difficulties - as evidenced this week - of delivering an international agreement on how to
tackle the plastic pollution that provides the most visible proof of the environmental damage wrought by an economy built on profligately linear resource models. Consumers and businesses
may say they are in favour of a circular economy in principle, but in practice weak regulations, engrained behaviours, and a lack of recycling capacity have combined to ensure progress is both
patchy and slow.
In the UK, where recycling rates have flat-lined for much of the last decade, the new Labour government has pledged to revitalise efforts to build a more circular economy and has made some
significant progress over the past 12 months. A timetable for the deposit return scheme (DRS) that was repeatedly delayed under the previous government has been finalised and should now
launch in October 2027. Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) rules were expanded to cover packaging at the start of the year and has already raised over £1bn for investment in new
recycling capacity. Disposable vapes were banned. And just this week e-waste rules were extended to online retailers to ensure they pay their fair share towards electronic recycling services.
Meanwhile, a new Circular Economy Taskforce has been convened and tasked with boosting circularity across six key sectors: agri-food; built environment; chemicals and plastics; electrical and
electronic equipment; textiles; and transport. The Taskforce is now working with these sectors to create a series of specific roadmaps to improve and reform their approach to using and
re-using materials.
These roadmaps will feed into a new Circular Economy Strategy, which will be published for consultation in autumn. Hopes among businesses and campaigners for what will be the first circular
economy strategy for England are high. Think tank Green Alliance today described the upcoming strategy as "a crucial opportunity to rethink how we use resources", as it called on Ministers to
ensure the plan includes a firm target to reduce resource use in the UK economy.
The strategy is expected to provide an overarching vision for how government at both the national and local level can work with businesses and investors to drastically increase
recycling capacity, boost recycling rates, incentivise repair and re-use, catalyse the development of innovative new designs and materials, penalise wasteful behaviours, and improve resource
efficiency.
The government is not without policy ideas and business models to draw upon. Campaigners have repeatedly noted how Wales enjoys some of the highest recycling rates in Europe,
showcasing how clear communication, effective collection services, and sufficient capacity can serve to drive up recycling. Growing numbers of businesses are rolling out new resource efficient
re-use services, sharing economy business models, and products that seek to embed circularity. Earlier this month, the Fabian Society published a blueprint for the new strategy proposing a cap
on repair costs, the introduction of raw material use reduction targets, the abolition of VAT on building refurbishments, investment in critical mineral recycling plants, and a new 'right to repair'
for consumers.
However, according to a new briefing paper today from the influential Aldersgate Group of businesses and the Institute for European Environmental Policy UK (IEEP UK), the strategy will also
have to reckon with a growing challenge for many organisations that dates back to the Brexit referendum. Despite assurances from the previous government to the contrary, recycling,
eco-design, and circular economy rules for the UK and EU are diverging, creating compliance challenges for businesses and threatening to
undermine the UK's wider push to boost circularity.
The report assesses a raft of UK and EU circular economy policies and legislation to determine where divergence has already occurred or is at risk of occurring. It reveals a mixed picture - for
example, the UK arguably has more ambitious rules on plastics and packaging with its recent ban on disposable vapes, planned national DRS, and extended EPR fees - but overall, the EU is
adopting more ambitious targets and policies for boosting resource efficiency and enhancing circularity, underpinned by more demanding eco-design product standards and the
bloc's new 'right to repair'.
Worse still, the areas where the risks of divergence are highest tend to be the areas with the biggest potential for environmental impacts. The EU's ecodesign product
standards are far more wide-ranging than the UK's rules, which are limited to energy-related products. The EU has new rules governing construction products and building resource efficiency,
while the UK Future Homes Standard is primarily focused on energy use and is still yet to be finalised. And Brussels has passed a Critical Raw Materials Act and is pushing a raft of policies to
boost e-waste recycling and re-use.
"Comparatively speaking, EU policy making on circular economy has been generally more ambitious and progressive than the UK since leaving the EU, with legislative divergence opening up
gaps on various fronts," the report states. "The European Union, guided by the European Green Deal, has introduced a Circular Economy Action Plan (CEAP) and a wide-ranging package of legal
and policy measures, tentatively putting its circular economy vision into practice."
The gap may well widen further given the UK's Circular Economy Strategy could quickly be surpassed by a Circular Economy Act from the European Commission, which is expected in
2026 and should "provide further overall focus to EU circular economy and critical raw materials policy and to stimulate the creation of a single market for waste and secondary raw
materials", according to today's report.

The paper acknowledges some of the EU's plans could be derailed by the backlash against environmental policies in some EU member states. But there is also the potential for EU circular
economy policies to be positioned as part of the bloc's efforts boost industrial competitiveness, enhance economic growth, and bolster resource
security, especially for critical raw materials.
The resulting divergence in standards is creating costly headaches for businesses that operate in the UK and EU, as they increasingly have to comply
with two different regulatory regimes. It also threatens to complicate on-going talks to integrate the UK and EU
Emissions Trading Scheme and lower trade barriers between the two markets. Meanwhile, UK consumers are at risk of an influx of lower quality products that
cannot be sold in the EU.
"Delivering a circular economy will mean that we make best use of our resources, strengthen supply chains, and support the delivery of net zero and environmental targets," said Rachel
Solomon Williams, executive director at Aldersgate Group. "However, the UK's current regulatory and fiscal frameworks do not effectively enable circularity and fairly
reward ambition. Regulations are acting as a barrier to offering circular products, and ambitious businesses face competition from those operating with lower standards. As other
markets, such as the EU, make progress, we must collaborate to ensure the UK is not left behind."

clear case of the benefits to the UK for aligning with the higher circular economy standards that the EU has established in the period since Brexit," he said. "This would chime with the

environment.
Solomon Williams said the upcoming Circular Economy Strategy now presents an ideal opportunity to "address potential divergence and reward ambition in the private sector".
The report sets out a raft of recommendations for how this might be done, calling for a renewed effort to identify and mitigate areas where regulatory divergence are proving
challenging, such as ecodesign standards, product passporting, and carbon taxation. It also argues for improved collaboration with the EU to share circular economy data

better ensure the circular economy is incorporated in future trade deals.
More broadly, the report argues the upcoming strategy must address shortfalls in recycling infrastructure and circular economy-related skills, providing businesses with the policy stability and
clear goals they need to invest in new facilities and training. And it urges Ministers to resolve current policy misalignments that are
hampering the adoption of circular economy practices. "Businesses highlighted low regrets actions for the government, with opportunities to resolve situations where
regulation or other policies are limiting progress with circularity," the report states. "For example, public procurement, regulation and policy could better incentivise circular economy actions
higher up in the waste hierarchy, such as repair and re-use, rather than focusing on interventions around end-of-life waste disposal."
For its part, the government is continuing to work on its new strategy with further updates expected in 'due course'. Ministers are expected to consider a wide range of policy proposals,
including for sector-specific interventions and for promoting re-use and enhancing resource efficiency.

Introduction
Recalling the thoughts of Jean Monnet and the main ideas of the Schuman Plan, the long-term target of the Union was to bring European states closer by entwining their economies and industries. It is not without irony that the
complexity of the negations around Brexit proves how successful and far-going European integration has come already. When in June 2016, the people of the United Kingdom (UK) were asked in a referendum whether to remain or
leave the European Union (EU), the majority (51.9%) – with a very small margin – voted in favor to leave the Union. Followed by complex negotiations and massive political turbulences, the UK formally left the EU in February 2020.
However, after four years of Brexit negotiations and uncertainty, a phase of intense trading negotiations started to draw up the future relationship between the EU and UK until the end of the transition phase to find a final
compromise for future cooperation and trade relations. Regardless of the outcome, the departure of Britain triggers severe changes and disturbances on both sides of the Channel. It will open a new chapter of cross-channel
relations not only with long-lasting political and economic consequences but also for the environment and future environmental policy-making in Europe. Doubtless, the departure of Britain will change the face of the United
Kingdom but it will also leave its traces on the European Union, causing distortions in the short but also in the long term.1
The EU is known for its leadership role in global environmental protection efforts, acknowledging that climate change and environmental degradation pose a real
threat to human mankind and also urging to decarbonize production and consumption. In various areas, the EU came forward with some environmental policies that did not exist before or at least not in such a huge region (e.g., acid rain, the ozone layer, climate change, hazardous
chemicals...). Over the past four decades, the EU developed and implemented major environmental concepts and principles that find application in environmental policymaking around the world (i.e., precautionary principle, burden sharing, waste hierarchy, proximity principle for
treatment of waste, etc.). The Union has one of the most mature and coherent environmental frameworks in the world. Policy-making within the Union also helps member states to share best practices. In the phase of policy formation, Member States can learn from each other's
challenges and policy responses. This process allows opting for the best-practice policy to be implemented. In the past, the realm of environmental policy-making served as a driver for advancing integration, as Member States were more willing to hand over policy environment-related
competences to the EU than in other areas. European environmental policy-making is not only more long-term oriented than those of national governments but also allows large-scaled efforts. Furthermore, it is more resistant to failures.2 A major source of the EU’s international influence
derives from its market and regulatory power based on common environmental standards.3 However, participation in the Union is based on compromises, and with the departure of a very powerful entity, the EU will be influenced in several aspects. Britain´s departure is expected to
trigger serious changes to several realms of the Union, such as for the economy, the financial sector, international trade, international relations, but also for environment-related issues (e.g., climate change, renewable energies, circular economy).
In 2015, the EU adopted its first Circular Economy package that should bring the EU economy in line with sustainability. And with the newly proposed EU Green Deal in 2019, the EU set out an ambitious target to achieve a climate-neutral Europe by 2050. And this goal can only be achieved
by rapid progress in establishing a circular economy.
Given that the EU has more experience with enlargement than with reduction, Brexit represents a quite unique phenomenon worth studying. While several countries have joined the Union over the yars, only the UK has left it. This paper seeks to provide answers on how Brexit will affect
the EU environmental policy-making, in particular, it focuses on the consequences in the field of the circular economy. Therefore, the work analyzes and evaluates the consequences of Brexit in terms of establishing the European circular economy in two realms: economy and politics. The
paper seeks to shed light on possible short-term and long-term consequences.
European Union, Circular Economy, and the EU Green Deal
The concept of circular economy (CE) has enjoyed widespread support from across Europe as it combines economic benefits (increased competitiveness, resource efficiency, new jobs, etc.) with environmental benefits (waste reduction, reducing greenhouse gas emissions output, etc.).
Though not lucidly defined yet, the circular economy concept builds on economic, natural, and social capital and redefines growth by focusing on society-wide benefits.4 The concept of CE seeks to design out waste and pollution, decouple economic activities from material demand and
consumption of resources, and keep valuable materials as long as possible in the system.5 Thus, CE aims at a reduction, reuse, recycling and recovery. CE redefines waste as input material for other industries and focuses on promoting the use of secondary raw materials. This not only
includes recycling efforts but includes a view on the overall life-cycle of products. Besides, CE spurs innovation and provides opportunities for new business models and growth.6
In the current linear mode of production, growth is propelled by natural resource extraction that also drives up greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.7 So far, progress towards a circular economy is slow. The global circularity rate stands at about 9.1%. 8 However, with the global population
projected to cross the 8.5 billion mark in 2030, demand for goods and services will necessarily grow. Annually, about 88 billion tonnes of materials are extracted from the ground, and about 2.01 billion tonnes of garbage is disposed of by urban residents worldwide.9 In 2019, emissions
from industrial activities and the burning of fossil fuels will pump an estimated 36.8 billion metric tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere.10 And according to the U.N. World Meteorological Organization, the CO2 concentration levels in the atmosphere hit a new record in 2018
by rising to 407.8 parts per million. 11 Since material management accounts for about two-thirds of worldwide emissions, the establishment of a circular economy also supports climate mitigation and resource efficiency plays directly into climate action. 12 The reuse of materials (e.g.,
cement, steel, aluminum, and plastics) can be central to lower greenhouse gas emissions. Accordingly, improved circular loops play a crucial
role in achieving the goals of the Paris Climate Agreement. If the current path is continued, there is no way to contain
temperature rise to a 1.5ºC above pre-industrial levels as laid out in the UN Paris Agreement.
Based on a report by SYSTEMIQ (2017), scaling the circular economy in Europe could offer investment opportunities totaling €320 billion.13 In a
circular economy, resources are used in a more sustainable way, the demand for primary raw materials is cut, resulting in increased material efficiency. Increased usage of secondary raw
materials can substitute the industry’s demand for primary raw materials. While raw materials demand could be lowered by an estimated 20%, the EU GDP would
increase by 3%. The promotion of circularity in the manufacturing sector of complex consumer durables could bring material savings worth some
€553 billion in the EU alone.14 According to a report by European Parliament (2017), the transition to a circular economy could create between 1.2 million and 3

million new jobs by 2030 and cut greenhouse gas emissions by more than 500 million tonnes between 2015 and 2035. A study by Material Economics (2018)
estimates that a circular economy could halve EU industrial emissions by 2050.15
Accordingly, circular economy efforts have become integral parts of recent environmental talks in Europe and beyond. In December 2015, the European Commission adopted its first Circular Economy Package aimed at supporting
businesses and consumers to engage a transition to a more circular economy.16 The package was followed by the Circular Economy Action Plan and proposals that cover the full lifecycle of products, from design, production, and
consumption to waste management and the development of a stronger secondary raw materials market. The initiatives resulted in a series of legislative and non-legislative actions, such as for waste management, plastics, fertilizers,
marine litter, critical raw materials and indicators measuring circularity. The EU also set out targets for recycling (65% of municipal and 75% of packaging waste by 2030) and implemented a ban on landfilling of separately collected
waste by 2030. According to the EU plans, the competitiveness of key industry sectors including manufacturing, waste management, and recycling will be strengthened by the Circular Economy Package.
The EU Green Deal
In December 2019, the new European Commission proposed its Green Deal with the aim to achieve carbon-neutrality by 2050. 17 However, achieving the net-zero GHG emissions objective by 2050 requires a profound
transformation of value chains across the EU economy based on innovative production processes and new business models. To achieve this ambitious goal, industries in all member countries have to make significant contributions.
The new Commission President von der Leyen claimed the European Green Deal to be "Europe's man on the moon moment"18, describing climate policy as Europe’s new growth strategy. According to von der Leyen, the former
growth model based on fossil fuels and pollution is out of date. The new Green Deal which is based on ten major pillars not only includes climate-neutrality by 2050 but also the establishment of a circular economy (see Figure 1). As
laid out by several studies, the establishment of a circular economy is a feasible option to reduce emissions.19 A large-scale switch from fossil-based technologies to low-carbon technology, the expansion of renewable energies, and
the promotion of energy-efficiency measures are envisioned. Since efforts will result in increased material demands further triggering greenhouse gas emissions, a net-zero emission reduction objective is only achievable with a
drastic system change in material and resource management based on circularity.
The New Green Deal for Europe comprises three distinct institutions: The Green Public Works (GPW) (an investment program), the Environmental Union (package of legislation to align policy with scientific consensus), and the
Environmental Justice Commission (EJC) an independent body to research, monitor, and advise EU policymakers. The latter is designed as an international body to ensure that the green transition is also a just one. By its broad
mandate, the EJC has the power to set new international standards for research and reporting on environmental injustices. However, the EJC takes only an advisory role, providing data on environmental changes for other EU
institutions or developing new indicators to evaluate policies. The GPW aims at delivering Europe’s economic transformation through a large investment program by including aspects of environmental justice and focusing on
decarbonizing Europe’s economy, reversing biodiversity loss and guaranteeing decent jobs across the continent. The program is financed entirely through green bonds issued by the European Investment Bank. The Environmental
Union aims at a broader target – a systemic change by offering a comprehensive regulatory package to realign policies based on scientific consensus. It is aimed to foster Europe´s position as a global green leader.
In the Green Deal, the EU commits itself to lead by example, setting standards for sustainable growth across global value chains, and using diplomacy, trade and development cooperation to advance climate action. The promotion
of a Circular Economy aims to further reduce GHG emissions but also to create economic growth and to spur global competitiveness. The new EU circular economy approach based on new waste and recycling laws will not only
represent half of the EU´s efforts to achieve net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 but also include measures to ensure access to raw material supplies. Access to critical raw materials (CRMs) used in digital and clean technologies was
named as a strategic security question in the European Green Deal. Certain CRMs are irreplaceable in solar panels, wind turbines, electric vehicles, and energy-efficient lighting, essential technologies for the establishment of a
circular economy and the promotion of energy transition. With the mining sector on the decrease in the EU and global demand on the rise, urban mining practices and the circular economy approach gain spotlight in the EU. In
times of limited access to material resources, closing industrial loops represents a promising reply to ensure the development of sustainable economy.

Consequences of Brexit for EU Circular Economy Efforts
Although a circular economy is perceived as a necessity to ensure the prosperity and wellbeing of human mankind in the future, its establishment is not self-evolving. Change requires strong political commitment and is often based
on economic rationale. With the departure of the UK from the Union, we will see changes in the direction of how EU circular policies evolve.20 The section below elaborates in detail the expected consequences of Britain´s
departure for establishing the EU Circular Economy by focusing on two major dimensions: the economic and the political realm.
The Economic Realm
The 2018 Global Environmental Performance Index lists solely European countries under the top 15.21 Switzerland ranks first, followed by France, Denmark, Malta, Sweden. The UK is put on the sixth position while Germany is
ranked 13th. However, green and clean does not necessarily correlate with a high level of circularity. Burning waste for energy, as widely practiced in some countries, minimizes landfilling but is not improving recycling rates or
establishing circular loops within the industry. In a circular economy, products remain as long as possible in the system, they are shared, reused, repaired and/or refurbished. But when they finally reach their end of life, they are
dismantled, recycled and the materials are injected back in the economic cycle. However, CE not only focuses on waste management and recycling but taking a look at every stage of a product's life, starting from the mining of raw
materials, design, production, consumption to waste management. By closing loops, a CE not only reduces the output of waste and emissions but also lower the overall demand for materials. To stay within planetary boundaries, by
2050 an average European will have to reduce by 80% the amount of natural resources they currently use for nutrition, housing, mobility and lifestyles (IEEP, 2019).
Based on the low self-sufficiency for raw materials in the EU, the physical trade balance is obviously asymmetric. By weight, the Union imports about three times more goods from non-EU countries than it exports. Hence, the Union
is very dependent and vulnerable to the import of certain raw materials. According to Eurostat, import dependency climbed from 18.6% (2000) to 23.1% in 2018. To address the issue, in 2008 the Commission launched the Raw
Material Initiative, a strategy to secure access to raw materials in the EU. In 2018, the Commission published a report highlighting the potential for a more circular usage of critical raw materials in the economy.22
A circular economy aims to maintain the value of products, materials and resources for as long as possible by returning them into the product cycle at the end of life while minimizing the production of waste. However, modern
economies are heavily dependent on a steady and secure supply of raw materials and the demand for materials is increasing with economic development. A recent OECD (2019) report projects the demand for raw materials to more
than double by 2060.23 The concept of CE stresses environmental and economic benefits gained by a more circular economic system, mainly by lowering the overall material demand and raw material consumption and increasing
resource efficiency as well as developing a secondary raw material market.

financial crisis in 2008, the EU-28 climbed to some 6,900 million tonnes in 2017. Across the EU, material consumption patterns vary largely depending on each Member States´ extraction of domestic resources, population density,
living standards, economic development, living standards, the size of agriculture, manufacturing and construction industries, but also the size of its imports and exports market. The amount of consumed material per capita in the
EU-28 varies largely. While Finland consumed the most material per (34.5), followed by Estonia (30.9) and Ireland (26.2), the UK only consumes 8.9 tonnes. Spain has the lowest consumption rate across the EU-28 with 8.4 tonnes. In
2018, the total DMC of the EU economy stood at about 13.8 tonnes per capita.25 And much of it still ends up as waste. In the EU, the amount of municipal waste generated per person in 2018 amounted to 492 kg
(2008: 518 kg), and about 490 kg in 2017.26 Municipal waste production of the UK was with 463 kg below average. In 2004, the UK´s waste production peaked at 602 kg. The top three waste
producers are Denmark (766 kg), Malta (640 kg) and Cyprus (637). At the bottom of the list, we find Poland (329 kg) and Romania (272 kg). The recycling rate of municipal waste of the EU-28
(2018) was at 47%. The top recycling countries are Germany (67.3%), Slovenia (58.9%), Austria (57.7%), while the UK shows a rate of 44.1%. Although not the core idea of a circular economy,
recycling of materials helps to improve circularity as it can be re-introduced into the economy as new substances or products.

Digitalization provides new space for platform-based enterprises focusing on circular efforts, engaging in new business models based on renting or leasing.36 At the end of life, manufacturers

require a business environment based on sharing, mainly provided by the platform economy. The UK is leading efforts in developing platform-based business
models. With the departure of the UK, experiences and insights are lost for EU policy-making. The EU lost a major innovative power,
providing difficulties to be compensated.
The Political Realm
During its membership, the UK realized its potentials by actively shaping policy-making through leading the discussions on EU policy instruments. From the 1990s onwards,
London turned into a driver for setting the EU’s environmental agenda, in some areas pushing for more action, in some areas blocking it - depending on its own national interests. As part of the
Green Growth Group, London was supportive to actions combating climate change, and it became a leader within on climate change mitigation efforts. With Brexit the group weakens
significantly and this may result in a decline of influence on the matter.
The UK was a key driver for installing the EU emissions trading scheme and pushed for more ambitious GHG reduction targets emissions by 2020 and 2030.38
Furthermore, the UK has taken a prominent role in shaping EU external environmental and climate policy and
diplomacy at the international level. As the departure of Britain may lead to a readjustment of the coalitions within the Council, it could lead to a situation
where EU environmental policy itself becomes less ambitious.

Britain´s EU membership provided a framework promoting transboundary cooperation, with keeping some room for local policy innovation. The UK
actively directed the discussions towards more market-based approaches (e.g., environmental auditing and eco-labels).39 Britain directly benefited from the membership through cleaner air, water, and oceans and the development of green market systems and turned from an
environmental laggard into a progressive environmental power pro-actively steering the discussions. However, with about 80% of the environment-related legislation coming from Brussels, London never forgot to point on the regulatory burden of environmental action during its
membership. Now, the UK needs to completely rebuild its institutional capacities regarding environmental protection (e.g., DEPA) and Brexit has also intensified internal political turmoil, such as constitutional disputes between the Scottish, Wallisian and UK governments. Given the large
disagreements among people about Brexit (urban vs. rural, pro-EU vs. anti-EU), there is an environmental divide emerging between (largely anti-EU) rural areas in favor of lowering environmental standards to attract business and pro-European urban areas in favor of upholding/tightening
high environmental standards. In the mid- and long-run, there are concerns that the break-up may weaken (or slow down) environmental protection efforts on the island. Since the UK will be occupied mainly with internal issues, the EU will not be able to get much support from the UK on
certain global environmental issues (e.g., circular economy, climate change). Consequently, future coalition-building becomes more difficult, as the UK government has new immediate priorities. This is underlined by London´s lack of interest in participation in the European Environment
Agency. Given the importance of the build-up a pan-European monitoring system for circular economy, the UK´s long-term strategy is questioned.
Implications of Brexit for Political Dynamics in the Union
During the enlargement process, the influence of each individual state on decision-making has declined gradually. Thus, for gaining leverage over EU decision-making, building winning coalitions is essential. In the EU, groups of member states with more or less close ties form coalitions to
influence the decision-making process. Basically, such coalitions emerge at an early stage, often during the informal approach to formal talks in the European Council and the Council of the European Union. And since proposals can only be rejected by a group, Britain’s departure will have a
major impact on relations in the Council of the European as new coalitions need to be formed to advance with CE. According to the Lisbon Treaty, approval of decisions in the Council requires at least 55% of votes from Member States and 65% from share-of-population votes.
Britain clearly utilized its EU membership to steer European policy-making in its favor.40 While the country supported more than 97% of the legislative measures adopted by the Council between 2004 and 2016, London voted against proposals more often and showed relatively higher
abstention scores (see Figure 6) than others. Germany is the second-largest veto player but on different issues than the UK. Closest with the UK on passing proposals come Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands.
In regards to environment-related decisions between 2010 and 2020, the UK never voted against a Council proposal, with only one abstention.41 Most votes against environment-related proposals come from Poland (6), Bulgaria, Cypress, Denmark, Hungary (4 each), followed by Malta,
Lithuania and Slovakia (3 each).42

A Franco-German Coalition for Circular Economy?
As a result of Brexit, the importance of large member states (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and Poland) increases, as their voting power gains are greater. A blocking minority can be achieved with 13 member states voting against, or at least four member states representing more than 35%
of the EU’s population. France and Germany together represent 33.2% of the EU’s population. In a different constellation, France, Italy, and Spain together represent 39.24% of the European population and can therefore easily form a blocking minority (Kleinowski, 2019).
In the past, the Union's political integration was mainly driven by a Franco-German coalition. Paris and Berlin were the primary architects for closer integration in monetary, foreign, security, as well as justice affairs. 43 London often voiced skeptical and critical voices, attempting to steer
discussions in its favor. Most of the British proposals have insisted that EU legislation should be efficient and market-oriented with a focus to reduce bureaucratic burden. London has relentlessly criticized the burden of the tight regulatory framework of the EU. Furthermore, British
environmental NGOs have played a crucial role in the creation of EU-level ENGOs and influencing European environmental policy-making.44 London was an advocate for a reduction of regulations with the aim to reduce the bureaucratic costs for small and medium-sized enterprises in the
internal market. Whereas London often called for more market-based instruments, Germany prefers approaches based on binding regulatory instruments. Brexit silences these critical voices, while others have more space. Poland is the largest Central and Eastern European member, and its
solid economic performance provides Warsaw with legitimacy to join discussions. However, in the past Poland showed no strong engagement in promoting strict environmental policies (e.g., renewable energies). In the EU-27, the voice of climate laggards such as Poland, Hungary or the
Czech Republic could turn louder. These countries blocked a new EU climate change target and refused to support writing the target into the EU’s strategic program for 2019-24. Sharing a communist past and poor environmental records, the Visegrád group could - led by Warsaw - further
slowdown EU efforts in stepping up circular economy efforts.
In another option, we could see a coalition between Germany and the eight northern European member states. Finance ministers of these countries already met in 2018 to start talks about alternative proposals for reforming the Eurozone. On various matters, such as the environment,
monetary, fiscal, EU budget, their viewpoints are close to Germany's. However, despite this close relationship with its Northern neighbors, Berlin will not risk its uniquely close bilateral relationship with Paris. Thus, the political realm about CE is still in limbo.
In times where the EU seeks to leave behind its linear production system, the departure of Britain triggers massive challenges. Modernization and promoting sustainability, such as decarbonizing production processes gain relevance but there are discussions on how to promote such a
major systematic transition. Are large industrial actors the main drivers or is change driven by the integration of a regional ecosystem of resilient actors, enabling diversity of approaches (open source)? While it is not clear yet which approach prevails over the other, some countries prefer
certain approaches. Larger investments in a future-oriented European industry are supported by several Member States (i.e., the Netherlands, Benelux Germany, France, Poland, Spain). However, the four large member states Germany, France, Italy and Spain show little interest in
promoting the digitalization of their economies, mainly out of fear of a decline in their traditional manufacturing industries. On the other hand, the Netherlands, the Nordic countries, Ireland and Czechia, see digitalization as an opportunity, also promote a sustainable economy. Also, the
UK´s strategy stresses the idea that a circular economy can be promoted by open platforms. While Britain's departure may lower pressure from competitors in the field of CE in the short term it could slow down necessary transition processes in the long run.
The promotion of a circular economy is mainly based on research and innovation, and the British research sector is known to be one of the best in the world in terms of quality and productivity. In the past, the UK has been extremely successful in European research programs, and its
universities are acknowledged for their scientific expertise and have close ties with prominent research institutes outside Europe. 45 Britain’s departure not only weakens EU Research Programs and policies but also silences voices to employ a more business-like approach to European
research programs. A necessity for increasing capacities in the field of circularity.
Covid-19 and the EU Budget
Among the policy areas where London took great interest during its membership were economic policies, global free trade, foreign affairs, security, defense, internal market consolidation and budgetary matters (e.g., the Multi-annual Financial Framework). The Union’s annual budget lays
down all expenditures and revenues and ensures the funding of EU policies and programs. The largest source of EU revenues (67%) stems from member-states transfers based on gross national income (GNI). After Germany and France, the UK was the third largest net contributor to the EU
budget. London contributed roughly €13bn (2018) to the total EU budget (about 9%), and Brexit rips a large hole in the EU budget. With difficulty, the EU managed to set up its 2020 budget of about €168.7bn (2019: €165.8bn) by rising the contributions from member states.46 The budget
covers seven large areas, with the biggest part allocated to Sustainable Growth (€59.9bn), followed by Economic, social and territorial cohesion (€58.6bn), Competitiveness for growth and jobs (€25.3bn), Global Europe, and Administration (each €10.3bn). Large parts (ca. 40%) are spent on
agricultural policy and farming. In the past, London heavily criticized the Union´s agricultural policy for its high costs. There is concern that agriculture continues to take up most part of the budget while other areas encounter cuts in their finances. After the massive stimulus programs due
to Covid-19 outbreak, the Union needed to readjust its budget. Given that the EU is left with lower financial contributions and due to the massive economic distortions in 2020, environment-related programs could receive lower funding, including circular economy efforts. As of now, the
concrete consequences of Covid-19 are not comprehensible and cannot be discussed in detail here. However, the pandemic clearly showed how vulnerable global supply chains are and how important the availability of locally produced goods is. In this respect, the virus outbreak is likely to
bring back some production lines to Europe over the mid-term. While it is unrealistic to think that globalization stops completely, there are strong calls for strengthening the local or regional production. From a historical point of view, such external shocks proved to have the power to
change the economy drastically. Future shortages of certain goods and raw materials due to the closures of borders in Europe and worldwide can only be avoided by promoting a strong local industry based on closed loops. However, the crisis takes its toll on companies´ investments in
innovation and digitalization. As a result, European governments and the EU wrapped up previously unseen financial stimulus programs. However, calls from some EU Member States were loud to abandon the Green Deal in order to have more financial resources to restore the
“conventional” economy. 47 Properly designed, such large financial supports could be used as instruments to stimulate green and sustainable circular efforts in the EU. As of now, it is too early to say how the programs will impact EU circular efforts.
EU Global Outreach for Circular Efforts
The EU seeks to form partnerships with other countries to cooperate on CE. The UK is still an influential nation with a larger global reach
than any other EU member state. Arguably, France has a global outreach too, however, not so extensive. Through the Commonwealth, London
possesses a large diplomatic network that could be used for environmental diplomacy in the past. This diplomatic network
is non-accessible now for the EU, limiting the Union's global CE efforts. In the past, the UK and France took a major role in convincing
countries to agree to the Paris Climate Change Agreement. For circular economy efforts of the EU, Brexit translates into a limitation of EU abilities to act
internationally - at least in the short term.
Conclusions
From an environmental perspective, the membership of Britain in the EU benefited both sides. Great Britain turned from an environmental
laggard into a major economic powerhouse and discussion leader in environmental policy-making. In recent years,
Britain actively contributed to policy discussions favoring approaches based on market approaches and reducing bureaucracy. With the departure of Britain, the future direction of
EU environmental policies is uncertain. Four decades ago, the focus of EU environmental protection efforts was mainly local or regional, and environmental challenges seemed to be
more simple to address. However, today we face cross-regional and complex global challenges, such as climate change, resource depletion, growing material demand, or the mounting waste
streams. With the rise in scope and complexity of ecological challenges, international cooperation among countries and regions increased relevance.
In regard to the establishment of a pan-European circular economy, the departure of the UK represents a blow to EU efforts. The Union has not
only lost a wealthy, powerful and high advanced economy but also a progressive force in addressing environmental issues. And
consequences will be felt in the economic but also in the political realm. In the past, the UK government has made progress in promoting CE by applying flexible and market-based approaches.
Major consequences of Brexit will likely be observed in the mid- or long-run after the UK government overcomes immediate priorities after quitting the Union. There is concern that Britain may
drift away from EU environmental legislation over the mid or long run and actively trying to undermine the EU environmental standards to improve its

economic competitiveness. And the Covid-19 outbreak speeds up such developments. In 2019, the government announced its 25 Year Environmental Plan aimed at to delivering
a green Brexit.48 While the plan includes ten target areas, it only peripherally points to the concept of circular economy and thus fails to include concrete measures promoting circularity of
materials.

Cascading and Systemic The evidence from tipping points alone suggests that we are in a state of planetary emergency: both the risk and urgency of the situation are acute […] If damaging
tipping cascades can occur and a global tipping point cannot be ruled out, then this is an existential threat to civilization. Prof. Tim Lenton and colleagues, “Climate tipping points — too risky to
bet against”116 The physical risks are non-linear, cascading, systemic and largely irreversible on human time frames. Climate models do not adequately represent all processes and are likely to
underestimate the risks. Many elements of the climate system exhibit tipping points or thresholds at which a small change causes a larger, more critical change to be initiated, taking that
system from one state to a discretely different state far less conducive to human survival and prosperity (see section 3 above). For example, a polar ice sheet may reach a temperature threshold
beyond which continuing and accelerated ice mass loss occurs, even without any additional rise in temperature, and such a change may take decades to centuries to be fully realized before a
new (ice-free) system stability occurs. Such threshold changes may be abrupt and irreversible on relevant time frames. And once the threshold is passed, returning conditions to pre-threshold
conditions may not restore the system. This is known as hysteresis, or bifurcation of a system, where it may be more difficult, or impossible, for a system to return to its previous state. Put more
simply: the path from A to B is not the same as the path from from B to A. Ice sheets are a good example. A chilling 2015 report on Thresholds and closing windows: Risks of irreversible
cryosphere climate change warned that the Paris commitments will not prevent the Earth “crossing into the zone of irreversible thresholds” in polar and mountain glacier regions, and that
crossing these boundaries may “result in processes that cannot be halted unless temperatures return to levels below pre-industrial” (emphasis added).117 For example, the tipping point for
the most vulnerable West Antarctic glaciers is probably between 0.5°C and 1°C, but cooling the planet back to that range would not create the conditions for their re-establishment. And in

2024, researchers again warned of “overconfidence in climate overshoot”, that is, exceeding a temperature target for decades, on the assumption that negative emissions technology will be
able to later reduce the heating and restore conditions as if there had been no overshoot. Extinctions caused by overshoot are one example. They show that “global and regional climate change
and associated risks after an overshoot are different from a world that avoids it… the possibility that global warming could be reversed many decades into the future might be of limited
relevance for adaptation planning today [because] temperature reversal could be undercut by strong Earth-system feedbacks resulting in high near-term and continuous long-term warming…
Only rapid near-term emission reductions are effective in reducing climate risks.”118 Changes in one element of the climate system may also trigger an unforeseen chain or cascade of events in
which one event in a system has a negative effect on other related components. For example, the mutual interaction of individual climate tipping points and/or abrupt, non-linear changes, may
lead to more profound changes to the system as a whole, and interactions between these climate systems could lower the critical temperature thresholds at which each
tipping point is passed.119 Together, tipping point thresholds, non-linear change and cascading events represent systemic risks, that is, the risk of a breakdown of an entire system
rather than simply the failure of individual parts: More frequent and intense extreme weather and climate-related events, as well as changes in average climate conditions, are expected to
continue to damage infrastructure, ecosystems, and social systems that provide essential benefits to communities… Extreme weather and climate-related impacts on one system can
result in increased risks or failures in other critical systems, including water resources, food production and distribution, energy and
transportation, public health, international trade, and national security. The full extent of climate change risks to interconnected systems, many of which span regional and
national boundaries, is often greater than the sum of risks to individual sectors.120 Systemic change means climate elements can tip from one state to an entirely different one with a sudden
shock that may permanently alter the way the planet works.121 In the physical interactions among the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation
and the Amazon rainforest, the polar sheets are often the initiators of cascade events,122 with Greenland and West Antarctica at risk of passing their tipping points within the 1.5°C–2°C Paris
range (and there is evidence they have already done so). Such changes are not adequately incorporated into climate models: “Change can come about abruptly and even catastrophically…
Predictive models are the lifeblood of climate science, and the foundation upon which political responses to the climate and ecological crisis are often based. But their ability to predict such
large-scale disruptive events is severely limited… The IPCC’s estimates of how much CO2 we can still emit to be on the safe side explicitly leave out many known
large-scale disruptions or tipping points because of insufficient understanding or because models cannot capture them.”123 Researchers have also investigated how
changes in forest degradation and monsoon circulation are interlinked: “It turns out that forest loss caused by direct deforestation, droughts, and fires might vastly contribute to a
changing climate in South America and could drive the coupled Amazon rainforest/ South American monsoon circulation system past a tipping point [and] suggest an

Raising resource efficiency would be the fastest way to de-risk the European economy. It would reduce dependence
on authoritarian regimes and fragile supply chains, and make the EU less vulnerable to volatile commodity prices (Letta, 2024; WEF, 2024). Circular solutions will
become more important as energy infrastructure dependencies shift from imports of fossil fuels (commodities) to the import of critical raw materials (CRMs; to create capital assets in
renewable energy). This does not mean that the EU should aim to move to autarky and become a self-sufficient economy or introduce protectionist policies. Rather, the elimination of wasteful
use of materials and energy has the potential to support the economic-security objectives of reducing import dependencies for critical products and deepening the single market and making it
more flexible (Pisani-Ferry et al, 2024). A well-functioning circular single market would increase the EU’s resilience against import shocks. Critical dependencies can be reduced by bringing
down demand for imported virgin materials, by substituting them with local, re-used and recycled materials and by using fewer materials thanks to efficiency-by-design or repair opportunities.
In energy, the recovery of biodegradable waste could contribute significantly to supply diversification. Biogas and biomethane could
meet 10 percent of natural gas demand by 2030, to which better food-waste collection and processing could contribute. Biogas production emits little carbon and prevents organic material
from going to landfill. Given that organic waste accounts for up to 50 percent of municipal waste (ECN, 2022) and 10 percent of all greenhouse gas emissions (WWF, 2021), that would reduce
environmental damage while contributing to energy security.
The next decades are likely to be characterised by supply bottlenecks as climate shocks proliferate, economies race to decarbonise and trade rivalries
disrupt flows. But even in the very short term, resource efficiency can offer a buffer to protect the EU’s economic security. For CRMs, supply deficits are already
expected for 2024 (Stewart et al, 2023). In the medium term, there are forecasts of potential supply gaps for copper and lithium of up to 10 percent and 49 percent
respectively in 2030 (ETC, 2023).
Commodity price volatility is another threat to economic security. Since 2020, the prices of critical minerals used in batteries underwent both dramatic increases and large declines
(IEA, 2024). Certain battery-grade lithium compounds increased in price by 800 percent to 1000 percent in just over one year (Riofrancos et al, 2023). This volatility is likely to
increase because of climate impacts, with 25 percent of mining executives expecting climate risks to be very significant in their operations in the next five years (KPMG, 2023).
To ensure that decarbonisation is not disrupted, the circular market for CRMs needs to expand faster. Through end-of-life recovery, design for materials efficiency and extended product
lifetimes, more reliable markets for critical minerals can be developed that would improve economic security. According to one estimate, secondary materials could meet up to 91 percent of US
demand for CRMs in low-carbon technologies by 2050 (Karali and Shah, 2022). Another example is Apple’s calculation that from one metric ton of iPhone components, recyclers can recover the
amount of gold and copper typically extracted from 2,000 metric tons of mined rock (Apple, 2024) – but users need incentives to return old devices and those devices need to be designed for
easy materials recovery. The EU’s Critical Raw Materials Act (CRMA, Regulation (EU) 2024/1252) sets targets for recycling to meet 25 percent of EU annual consumption of strategic raw
materials by 2030.
The value embedded in disused electronic devices globally is around $57 billion. The EU has room to increase from its current 39 percent recycling rate for waste electrical and electronic
equipment (WEEE) (EEA, 2024). The contribution of recycled minerals to fresh inputs that are crucial for electronics is also very low: cobalt (22 percent), nickel (16 percent), neodymium (1
percent) and lithium (0 percent), even though significant supply gaps are forecast for these transition materials (ETC, 2023). This is partly driven by low rates of recovery due to poor product
design for end of life and the lack of well-established infrastructure for recovery of such minerals (Charles et al, 2020).
However, although end-of-life treatment is important, the high projected demand for CRMs suggests that recycling alone cannot meet all the EU’s needs. One estimate suggests that globally,
recycling may only start to play a substantial role in reducing virgin material extraction after 2040, once enough recyclable materials are in use (Peplow, 2023). To achieve economic security,
end-of-life treatment must be pursued in conjunction with other measures to reduce the volume of CRMs needed further up the production chain.

crisis; space debris are at risk of becoming the equivalent of a "drifting island of plastic" [1]. Large constellation (LC) systems plan to operate tens of thousands, or even hundreds of
thousands, of satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO) [2], posing the threat of an inglorious end to the Space Age. A "take risks and fail often" approach to new technology is being
extended to space without considering that mistakes in space cannot be cleaned up as easily as they can on Earth. Satellites that cannot maneuver, cannot
avoid collisions. Loss of maneuverability can result from failures of satellite sub-systems in the maneuver chain or from collisions with small (untracked) objects that disable these subsystems.
Passive deorbit times can be minimized with lower LEO altitudes and larger area-to-mass ratios but can still require years depending on the solar cycle and larger
area-to-mass ratios realized by increasing area can lead to increased collision risk. Even satellites that can maneuver, can be involved in collisions. Conjunction warnings may
not be generated, not all lethal debris are trackable. Every time a satellite is not maneuvered in response to a low probability conjunction warning, there is a non-zero
collision risk that depends on the space situational awareness (SSA) accuracy. Additionally, every time a satellite is maneuvered, there is another non-zero probability that the
maneuver will result in a collision. In both cases, with a sufficient number of conjunctions occurring, even six sigma events can become likely. Collisions between LEO satellites are typically

catastrophic resulting in large numbers of new debris objects spread across LEO altitudes. For example, there are currently 1439 tracked debris objects in orbit from the 2009 collision between
Iridium-33 and COSMOS-2251 [3]. Even though the collision occurred at 800-km, the debris' apogees now range from 400 km to over 1,600 km. Constellations are appropriately analyzed over
their orbital lifecycles. LCs are incrementally deployed, and satellites are replenished as they fail, reach end-of-life, or are replaced with more capable models. This replenishment can be
reasonably modeled to continue until the constellation is no longer economically viable. The result is a continuing process of orbit raising and phasing, and a combination of active and passive
deorbiting. Sub-system failures and small object collisions can be mitigated with sub-system redundancy, and small objects collision can be further mitigated with shielding. Operational
techniques, such as initiating deorbit immediately after the (N - 1)-th failure with N-th redundancy, can be used to improve the effective satellite reliability. The debris environment
naturally evolves over time as objects decay and new objects are created by collisions between existing debris objects. Satellite collisions
can cause step increases in the debris population. In addition, there are over 900 derelict rocket bodies remaining in orbit which pose further debris generating
risk. Previous work [4] using a simplistic model showed that reducing the number of non-maneuverable satellites significantly increases the time to Kessler Syndrome, a self-sustaining collision cascade [5]. Another approach [2] used simplified rate equations to model population evolution. One of the simplifications was ignoring collisions of debris with other debris, precluding the possibility of a Kessler Syndrome.
