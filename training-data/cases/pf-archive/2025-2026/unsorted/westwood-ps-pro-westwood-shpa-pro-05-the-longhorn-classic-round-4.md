Digital Platforms and Platform Economics
A digital platform is an online entity that facilitates interaction between two or more distinct economic entity², allowing them to create, communicate, exchange value, or collaborate. A platform economy is a business model where digital platforms enable multiple economic entities to come together and interact in a way that “benefits” all the entities involved. Examples include Uber, Swiggy, Amazon, Instagram, and WhatsApp. Unlike traditional markets, platform economies are shaped by:
Network effects
Positive feedback loops
Switching costs
Lock-in
Together, these forces can propel one platform to dominance and erect high barriers to entry for challengers.
Network Effects
Network effects are force multipliers that allow a product/platform to become stronger when more people join or use it. For example, if most of your friends are on Instagram, it makes sense to join the same network. The larger the groups of people on a network, the stronger the network becomes. There are different types of network effects — Direct Network Effects, Indirect Network Effects, and Data Network Effects.
Direct Network Effects occur when more users join a one-sided platform (like a messaging app). As the user base grows, so do the opportunities to communicate and connect, driving both higher adoption rates and an ever-larger network.
Indirect Network Effects occur on two-sided platforms that connect distinct user groups — typically buyers and sellers. An increase in users on one side of the platform enhances trade and business opportunities for users on the other side. For example, on ride-hailing platforms like Uber, which connect drivers and riders, more drivers on the road improve the experience for riders, and vice versa.
Network Effects as the Engine for WhatsApp’s Growth
Launched by Jan Koum in 2009, WhatsApp 1.0 began as a service for updating address-book statuses, but quickly evolved into a way for people to message friends via their status updates. Koum — known for his ethos of “No Ads! No Games! No Gimmicks!” — then transformed WhatsApp 2.0 into a clean, ad-free messaging platform at a time when most phones were internet-connected³ and the only alternatives were SMS, MMS, or BlackBerry’s BBM. This initial critical mass of users created the perfect conditions for WhatsApp to corner the market: with most of a person’s contacts already on WhatsApp, new users automatically adopted it as their default messaging app, driving direct network effects.
WhatsApp’s integration with businesses further reinforced indirect network effects: a larger user base attracted more companies, which in turn reached more users and expanded the platform’s ecosystem.
Positive Feedback Loops
In systems thinking, complex networks usually have feedback loops, which can either be positive or negative. Positive feedback loops reinforce a particular feature or behaviour of the network, thereby leading to exponential changes in the system. Negative feedback loops, on the other hand, produce effects that run counter to the changes observed in the system. Both these loops act so that the system eventually achieves its version of homeostasis.
Positive Feedback and Tipping: Achieving Market Dominance
WhatsApp’s growing user base, combined with its intuitive interface and strong UX features, created a positive feedback loop that drove further adoption. Rapid expansion in key markets such as India and Brazil accelerated this viral growth. Together, network effects and the feedback loop tipped the market in WhatsApp’s favor, building a wide moat around its user base and producing a winner-take-all dynamic — evident in monthly active users rising from 10 million in October 2010 to 100 million in October 2012.⁴
Switching Costs
<mark>Switching costs</mark> expenses a user incurs when moving from one platform to another. They are common in the information-technology space because data is stored on servers, and transferring it- or adopting new hardware or software — adds expense. Switching costs may be social (losing established connections) or pecuniary/transactional. When these costs are high, they <mark>effectively lock users into a platform, even if</mark> <mark>its features</mark> or pricing <mark>become</mark> economically <mark>unattractive</mark>⁵.

We begin by defining the concepts that are key to this research. First, we adopt the definition of competition proposed by Michael Mazarr and his co-authors in a 2018 RAND study:
