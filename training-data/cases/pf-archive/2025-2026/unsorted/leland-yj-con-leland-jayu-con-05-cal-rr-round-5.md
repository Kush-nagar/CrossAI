At the most basic level, a resolution is a sentence that declares something. Another word for this is a “constative.” A sentence is determined to be a constative when it can be clearly demonstrated to be true or false. Understanding the resolution as a constative is applicable to Public Forum because <mark>every act in</mark> the <mark>round</mark>, every word and gesture, ought to prompt the judge <mark>to determine</mark> the <mark>truth of the res</mark>olution. Thus, the resolution is <mark>P</mark>ublic <mark>F</mark>orum’s connection to the overall “truth-seeking” goal of formal debate. Resolutions tend to follow two general models of truth: positive and normative.
A positive model of truth examines empirically verifiable and value-free descriptions of reality. A normative model of truth <mark>tests</mark> the <mark>ideal reality given</mark> <mark>a set of values</mark>. The language of the resolution determines if it is normative or positive. Normative and positive statements often address similar content but engage that content in distinctly different ways. The resolution, “Government tax cuts have increased employment,” is a positive statement: the value of tax cuts need not be assessed to prove this statement true. The resolution merely questions the causal relationship between two events. The resolution, “The government should cut taxes,” is a normative statement: it <mark>requires debaters to assess</mark> the value of tax cuts and determine whether cutting taxes would be the <mark>ideal action</mark> for the government to take. Both resolutions prompt an exploration of tax cuts but, ultimately, have very different focuses.
The verb of the sentence usually changes the model of truth of a statement. Verbs like “<mark>should</mark>” or “ought” are <mark>associated with normative statements</mark> because they ask about possible courses of action or <mark>obligations for agents</mark>. Verbs like “is,” “will,” or “has” are associated with positive statements because they often form resolutions that exclusively describe reality, rather than advocating for a particular course of action or set of values.

1] Emotivism---There are no objective truths independent of our evaluative attitudes as ethical judgements are not statements of facts.
Oya ’19 [Alberto; November 26th; Research Fellow at the Instituto de Filosofia da Nova, PhD in Philosophy at Universitat de Girona. “Classical Emotivism: Charles L. Stevenson.” Filosofía moral, política y del derecho. No. 22, p. 311-2. https://doi.org/10.15366/bp2019.22.016; LUCAO]
Emotivism states that <mark>moral judgments</mark> do <mark>not</mark> concern matters of <mark>fact</mark>s, insofar as they do not <mark>describe</mark> or represent the world in any way, but are <mark>simply emotional responses</mark> to it – which is why defenders of emotivism usually claim that <mark>moral judgments cannot be true or false</mark>. Thus, for example, if I judge that you acted (morally) wrong in <mark>stealing</mark> a book from the library, I am <mark>only considering</mark> the <mark>empirical proposition</mark> that you stole that book from the library <mark>and</mark> at the same time expressing an attitude of <mark>disapproval</mark> towards it. And if I judge that stealing books from libraries is (morally) wrong, I am not thinking about any proposition at all, but I am merely displaying a certain attitude toward the action of stealing books from libraries. Similarly, according to emotivism, <mark>moral language</mark> has <mark>no factual meaning</mark> at all, since it <mark>only</mark> serves to <mark>express non-cognitive states</mark> such as attitudes or feelings and, therefore, its statements <mark>cannot be</mark> <mark>analysed</mark> in terms of truth and falsehood. Thus, according to emotivism, when I say ‘Telling lies is (morally) wrong’, I am not asserting any proposition, I am only expressing my feeling or attitude of disapproval towards the action of telling lies. These ideas are well summarized by Ayer in his Language, Truth and Logic:
The presence of an ethical symbol adds nothing to its factual content. Thus if I say to someone, ‘You acted wrongly in stealing that money’, I am not stating more than if I had simply said, ‘You stole that money’. In adding that this action is wrong I am not making any further statement about it. I am simply evincing my moral disapproval of it. It is as if I had said, ‘You stole that money’, in a peculiar tone of horror, or written it with the addition of some special exclamation marks. The tone, or the exclamation marks, adds nothing to the literal meaning of the sentence. It merely serves to show that the expression of it is attended by certain feelings in the speaker.
The exception to this is Stevenson, who in his Facts and Values: Studies in Ethical Analysis (1963) argues that ethical judgments are truth-apt. His motivation for this is to preserve our normal habits of speech, which presuppose that ethical judgments have truth-values. In brief, Stevenson’s claim is that when one says that an ethical judgment is true, what he is really doing is reaffirming that ethical judgment. His example is as follows:
When Mr. A says “Jones ought not to have done it”, and Mr. B replies, “that is true”, what is the force of B’s reply? Rather obviously he too has said, in abbreviated form, the equivalent of “Jones ought not to have done it”. His “that is true” permits him as it were to repeat A’s remark, thus expressing an attitude (apart from hypocrisy) that is in agreement with A’s.
Stevenson argues that this sense of truth as reaffirming what others have said is not an unusual sense of truth, insofar as it is also typical for factual contexts. However, the important point here is that even if Stevenson’s considerations are correct and ethical judgments are truth-apt in this sense of truth, it does not make ethical judgments truth-apt in the ordinary (and interesting) sense of truth.
The claim that moral language only expresses attitudes seems to imply that <mark>there is no contradiction</mark> involved <mark>in moral disagreement</mark>. That is, if I say ‘Telling lies is (morally) wrong’ and you say, ‘Telling lies is not (morally) wrong’, we are not contradicting each other in any way; we are <mark>only expressing</mark> our <mark>different</mark> <mark>personal preferences</mark>. So, if the emotivist wishes to deny that we have moral disputes, he needs to explain why it looks like we do; since we are clearly trying to argue for something, not just expressing our personal preferences.

In this sense, the capacity for <mark>aspiration</mark> of the peoples who have been suffering the ongoing consequences of coloniality is <mark>condemned to fatalistic despair, because</mark> these <mark>peoples</mark> are conceived as <mark>unable to</mark> have the tools to formulate <mark>hopes for themselves</mark> (Kleist & Jansen, 2016). This point, suggest Kleist and Jansen, draws attention to the unequal social processes and structures that produce and distribute dispositions of hope within particular spatiotemporal conditions
Hage’s (2003) analysis of governance through hope has also made an important contribution to recognizing the biopolitics of hope and how it works in neoliberal (and colonial) settings. In particular, Hage theorizes how hope as a social category is produced and distributed by the neoliberal state; this is why, he argues, hope should always be viewed as situated within specific socio-historical conditions, rather than as a generic (ontological) phenomenon. In the neoliberal context of the US economy, for example, hope works through sustaining the aspiration of upward social mobility, regardless of whether this is likely to occur or not; a similar claim could be made for the way hope works for communities that are still suffering from settler colonialism (e.g. Indigenous communities) through sustaining the belief that the US government will honor its peace, land, and other agreements, when in fact the opposite is taking place. This sort of ‘<mark>societal hope</mark>’ (Hage, 2003), however, may <mark>entail</mark> what Berlant (2011) has called ‘<mark>cruel optimism</mark>’, namely, “a relation or <mark>attachment to compromised conditions</mark> of possibility” (p. 2) whose realization may be impossible to attain. Although the neoliberal state continues to distribute hope unequally according to Hage (2003)—e.g. some groups may be recipients of little or no societal hope—people continue to be attached to the view that upward social mobility is for everyone, only if they try hard enough. Hence, “the production, distribution and differentiation of societal hope is a fundamentally political phenomenon, constituting a way of government” (Kleist & Jansen, 2016, p. 382) that <mark>shapes</mark> the <mark>desires of subjects</mark> through hope, thus <mark>reproducing</mark> habituated or normalized life (Coleman, 2017)
Hope, therefore, comes in different forms, just as there are different ways of governing through these hopes (Kleist & Jansen, 2016), thus it is crucial to pay attention to hope’s uneven effects, that is, the exclusions that might be produced or the experiences of <mark>entrapment and</mark> “<mark>stuckedness</mark>”, to use Hage’s (2009, p. 97) term. Ongoing forms of coloniality in many Western societies (e.g. US and Canada) produce such experiences of stuckedness, when attempts to move toward a different (decolonized) future are met with <mark>failure</mark>. Such failures may <mark>communicate</mark> the message that what is actually needed as a <mark>response is to embrace</mark> and endure situations of <mark>uncertainty and crisis</mark> by developing resilience (Kleist & Jansen, 2016). This tendency fits well with neoliberal and neocolonial modes of governance, because it emphasizes individual responsibility as a means of realizing the good life, while <mark>ignoring</mark> the role of <mark>social structures</mark> (Zembylas, 2020). As Kleist and Jansen (2016) point out, this emphasis on resilience could be interpreted as a form of biopolitical governance that distributes hope according to neoliberal or neocolonial modes of governmentality.
Moving away, then, from visions of possible futures that operate to suspend alternative hopes and reproduce normalized or habituated hopes requires that hope, as we know it, needs to become explicitly anti-colonial. Yet anti-colonial hope is not necessarily found in subverting normalized hopes or interrogating the hope of change, as is often advocated for in critical theory; this is why critical hope may not be enough. Seeing hope as a mode of affect and practice that appears to be part of a biopolitical apparatus suggests that anti-colonial hope is not merely a question of thinking a better or more authentic or more autonomous form of life, but rather of “decreating” (Wrangel, 2017, p. 886) the future and rendering it ‘inoperative’ under conditions of coloniality. Most importantly, it means grounding hope in the dreams and aspirations of people actively organizing and resisting the material relations and structures of coloniality. Indeed, the idea of anti-colonial hope involves a complex and ongoing set of practices and processes of confrontation, conflict, negotiation and attunement (cf. Pedwell, 2016) of hope with de/anti-colonial projects. Therefore, moving beyond critical theory, <mark>anti-colonial hope</mark> is an affirmative <mark>practice of alternative hopes of those</mark> peoples <mark>whose future cannot be</mark> adequately <mark>captured in</mark> the <mark>language of</mark> the present or the language of <mark>coloniality</mark>, because it would be too complicit with the status quo (Eagleton, 2015). As Wrangel (2017, p. 886) writes, “it is ultimately the capacity to not hope” along the lines of neoliberal and neocolonial visions of the future that is affirmed, namely, the affirmation of the hopes of those who have been marginalized and their voice has been rendered unimportant by colonizers
Drawing on this argument, then, I am suggesting that anti-colonial hope requires an affirmative understanding of hope, namely, one that recognizes the visions of those resisting coloniality. This affirmative understanding of hope raises new questions about what anti-colonial hope does such as: Who decides which hopes are legitimate, and what does it take to break the attachment or tendency toward practices of hope that reproduce habituated or normalized life and vision—such as those exalting individuals’ resilience, while ignoring the material structures of coloniality? What is the cost of developing the capacity to not hope in neoliberal and neocolonial terms? How do alternative hopes develop over time? Anti-colonial hope thus means giving up the idea that the object of hope is monolithic or predetermined, and its fulfillment is inevitable, regardless of the socio-political conditions, only if one takes enough responsibility for realizing the good life; rather, anti-colonial hope means reframing negative critique of coloniality as affirmative practice of hope that is more centered on the search for an alternative which entails a capacity to imagine and project the new (Rebughini, 2018)—e.g. actions and practices in the present that not only acknowledge the negative consequences of racial divisions and white supremacy in the US, but also move forward to challenge those divisions and inequalities and make room for realities that are less and less tolerant of coloniality. So, based on the above ideas, what does it mean to cultivate anti-colonial hope in pedagogy? Who is going to do that? For whom? And, why?
Cultivating anti-colonial hope in pedagogy
Bloch (1998), one of the eminent theorists of hope, has suggested that hope requires education, namely, we need to learn hope. If hope is teachable, then the question is: how does one teach anti-colonial hope, and what sort of pedagogy would do that? My analysis so far has emphasized that hope needs to be understood as both an affective and a biopolitical act, and that hopes are produced, distributed and sustained differently within specific socio-political configurations. In other words, hope is not an ‘innocent’, free-floating, and (merely) existential feeling, but rather a deeply political practice that is embedded in particular affective structures. If hope is situated in neoliberal or neocolonial structures, then it will have to become anti-colonial, which does not mean that it will become ‘pure’; it merely means that we, scholars and educators, need to pay attention to the affective and biopolitical dimensions of hope and their implications, including the pedagogical ones.
Pedagogically speaking, then, critical pedagogies are not enough to do the job of cultivating anti-colonial hope, as mentioned earlier in the paper; critical pedagogies need to become de/anti-colonial pedagogies (Zembylas, 2018b), namely, pedagogical practices that are reframed so that they explicitly confront and resist coloniality with the aim of disrupting colonial structures and practices—in this case, the <mark>epistemic</mark> and affective <mark>practices</mark> that <mark>reproduce</mark> structures of <mark>hope embedded in coloniality</mark>. Sefa Dei and Kempf (2006) suggest that an anti-colonial <mark>pedagogy requires</mark> a great deal from teachers such as: imagining <mark>new classroom relations</mark> that subvert convention power dynamics; looking beyond dominant curricula for ideas and resources <mark>that acknowledge</mark> the <mark>embodied knowledges</mark>, histories <mark>and needs of different learners</mark> in the classroom; challenging the organizing structure of schools in which they work; acknowledging their self-location, responsibility and responsibility within coloniality (or settler colonialism); <mark>and</mark>, finally, <mark>teaching and rewarding student resistance</mark>, providing students with the tools to locate and value resistance to coloniality in their everyday lives and their communities’ past.
In the remainder of this paper, I offer some thoughts on the contributions that anti-colonial pedagogies can make to cultivate anti-colonial hope. Needless to say, one may argue that focusing on pedagogical practices (i.e. micro-level) is not enough, because this effort does not necessarily address systemic and structural inequalities (i.e. macro-level) inherent in education systems that reproduce coloniality (Grosfoguel, 2011). However, work at the micro-level should not be underestimated, as educators need to confront the “epistemic blindness” (Andreotti, 2016, p. 104)—I would also add the ‘affective myopia’—that is reproduced by Eurocentric pedagogical and knowledge practices more generally, thus creating new hopeful knowledges, not simply reproducing existing knowledges and practices.
In suggesting the cultivation of anti-colonial hope in pedagogy, then, I am cognizant of the dangers involved—including the risk of turning de/ anti-colonial projects into a metaphor (Tuck & Yang, 2012)—yet this is precisely why my earlier analysis has emphasized the spatiotemporal conditions of the production and distribution of hope; unless classroom or public pedagogies pay attention to the affective and biopolitical dimensions of hope and affirm alternative hopes, it will be difficult to refrain from practices that reproduce neoliberal and neocolonial visions and repositories of hope tout court. In this effort, I do not negate critical hope and the pedagogies associated with it; I argue, though, that given how pedagogical interventions need to be situated into specific sociopolitical and historical structures, then it is crucial to pay attention to the mechanisms of governing hope through which visions of coloniality are reproduced in visible, less visible and invisible ways.
Cultivating anti-colonial hope in pedagogy, for example, includes working with teachers, students, administrators, teacher educators, government bodies, and so on, to recognize how peoples’ hopes within a specific community may disrupt or reproduce the status quo, enabling or preventing the prospects of a future that breaks with coloniality and imagines the world differently (Andreotti, 2016). The first step, then, is for students and teachers to study closely their own aspirations, identifying and challenging their personal and collective affective investments and ideologies of hope. This step has to go hand in hand with explorations that make visible the hopes of peoples who have been suffering the consequences of coloniality over the centuries (e.g. Indigenous community living in Canada or the Amazon), highlighting the repositories of hope of all those whose visions, mournings, memories, voices, and spaces have been silenced. Art, literature and film can be particularly helpful in pedagogical efforts to engage with the hopes of peoples suffering from the consequences of coloniality, just as action-oriented projects that make these hopes alive, allowing collective transformative action against coloniality (Zembylas, 2018b). For example, the pedagogical interventions designed by Vanessa Andreotti, Sharon Stein and their colleagues in Canada and Brazil—called “Gesturing Toward Decolonial Futures” see https://decolonialfutures.net/ —is a paradigmatic example of using “artistic, pedagogical, cartographic, and relational experiments that aim to identify and de-activate colonial habits of being, and to gesture toward the possibility of decolonial futures” (n.p.). I would argue that these pedagogical experiments cultivate anti-colonial habits of hope, because they create affective spaces for a present and a future that affirms a more inclusive, response-able and intimate world.
Studying and especially educating for anti-colonial hope within educational systems that are already embedded in colonial systems is an inherently fragile and risky endeavor (la paperson, 2017), as it is involved in a double bind, where it is at once containing and releasing hope (see Wrangel, 2017) for de/anti-colonial futures. For example, considering that well over 80% of teachers in the US are white, it means that undertaking the task of teaching anti-colonial hope (e.g. to white students and students of color alike) has to acknowledge first the work and ideas of Black and Indigenous intellectuals and teachers. This task, therefore, is not merely a pedagogical one, but a deeply existential and political one. It certainly goes beyond the potential savior stance of an outsider teaching hope to marginalized communities, but rather asserting the classroom space as an anticolonial space in which teachers and students of different communities are committed to invent and learn from (re)sources of hope in their own as well as other communities that enact anti-colonial practices. In fact, a pedagogy of anti-colonial hope is a way of bringing together all those who benefit from coloniality into the anti-colonial (and decolonial) movement—a project that is not easy at all, yet a necessary project, if there will be any progress in truly resisting coloniality.
Teaching anti-colonial hope, then, takes different forms in different communities and requires continuous reactivation and reinvigoration—in essence, it is an unfinished and incessant project, because it is inextricably linked to larger battles for epistemic, social and political justice within systems that continue to do epistemic and actual violence (Odysseos, 2017). What are the most productive strategies through which pedagogies can affirm the hopes and aspirations of peoples from communities that have suffered the material, affective and political consequences of coloniality? If anti-colonial hopes are identified and recognized, then how subversive can they be? These questions anticipate the complexities and often paradoxes emerging from pedagogical efforts to cultivate anti-colonial hope in education systems whose organizing structures are steeped in coloniality.
Importantly, then, the task of educating for anti-colonial hope will require the creation of a new language of hope <mark>that moves beyond Eurocentric</mark> pedagogical <mark>theories and</mark> practices—a language that <mark>emerges from</mark> within the experience of the ‘colonial wound’ (Mignolo, 2005) such as the experiences and <mark>knowledges of Indigenous communities</mark>. This language of hope will enable teachers and <mark>students</mark> alike to <mark>envision new</mark> social and <mark>political imaginaries that challenge</mark> the everyday praxis of how to live together in hopeful ways, while <mark>coloniality</mark> still persists. Cultivating anticolonial hope in pedagogy, also, would seek to develop “forms of radical and transgressive praxis” (Odysseos, 2017, p. 468) that create new affective relations with those who are suffering from the consequences of coloniality in different settings. <mark>Students</mark> of color and white students alike, for example, <mark>should be offered</mark> practical <mark>opportunities</mark> (e.g. community projects) <mark>to enact</mark> manifestations of <mark>hope</mark> that include specific actions which alleviate the suffering of peoples who experience the consequences of various forms of oppression and colonization (cf. Coordination and shared activity appear to be at the center of human sociality. De Jaegher and Di Paolo (2007) characterize “coordination” as the non-accidental correlation between two or more coupled systems, so that their behavior matches to a degree far beyond what is expected given what those systems are capable of doing. How to unpack this dense hyperbole? To begin with, there is an opposition between (a) scepticism's effect upon rationality and (b) rationality's effect upon scepticism. Both rationality and scepticism are aspects of philosophy that strive for the truth they love. Both are married to philosophy and divorce is forbidden.6 One might conceive, then, of a struggle within philosophy between a scepticism that doubts in order to preserve truth by establishing what it cannot be, and a rationality that strives to establish themes by which truth is intelligible. Scepticism is always trying to unravel the fabric of reason, challenging its thematic claims through doubt and the unveiling of the condition of dubitability. It utilizes a certain expressive language that endeavours to reduce the promotion of themes as much as possible.
