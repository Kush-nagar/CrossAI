Migrant Hyporeality K---1AC
For every Heaven we enjoy, there’s a migrant living hell for it. What was once a border has been transformed into shock therapy that takes migrants into a ‘carnival of suffering’ in which they are rendered socially dead. Debate’s incessant demand to suspend disbelief for simulation is a privilege not afforded to those in the hyporeal, the world of relentless, brutal reality.
Lucht 24 [Hans Lucht; Senior Researcher @ DIIS; 8-1-2024; Anthropological Theory; Volume 25, Issue 3; “Distressed: Migrant underworlds and travels in hyporeality”; doi.org/10.1177/14634996241264670; DOA:11-7-2025] ;;
Based on fieldwork among Ghanaian migrants in Italy, this article explores how migrant workers reproduce the near-fatal distress endured on the high-risk journeys in the design and production of artificially aged high-end denim jeans. These workers must respond to a seemingly never-ending obsession of global middle-class consumers with obtaining garments that display the strain of physical labor, from which they are largely alienated. The article explores this cruel irony by taking its analytical point of departure in Susan Sontag's (2003) critique of Jean Baudrillard's notion of hyperreality and proposes the contrasting concept of hyporeality.1 To Sontag, Baudrillard's notion of an evaporating reality substituted with a simulated reality or hyperreality overlooks how the people of most of the world live—far from immune to the many shocks of actual, material reality (see also Mbembe, 2019a).
On a more general level, the article thus argues that denim production is one example of the intimate global links between two co-existing and overlapping experiential realities, where migrant underworlds and travels in hyporeality increasingly sustain privileged worlds of consumption and play. Furthermore, it explores how these contrasting realities entail different senses of time, space, and agency for impoverished migrants and privileged consumers, connected through the global capitalist production apparatus.
Why Baudrillard? Though the article parts decisively with Baudrillard's work it recognizes the importance of not only referencing but of actually integrating media and consumption into the analysis of the globalized contemporary world. What is lacking in his approach, however, are the links to production by an invisible, migrant labor force. In contrast to Baudrillard's hyperreality, <mark>life on the dark side of</mark> global capitalist <mark>production is</mark> not <mark>characterized by</mark> the evaporation of materiality into thin air but by <mark>proximity to an unsparing reality from which there is no</mark> ready <mark>escape</mark>.
West Africa is not a world without simulation and simulacra, of course. The <mark>markets</mark> in Ghana, for instance, are <mark>full of fake</mark> products, for instance crudely made “<mark>Nike</mark>” or “Gucci” clothes, but they are fake fakes (see Graw, 2012: 32). <mark>Rather than imploding reality</mark> and representation, these <mark>fakes reveal</mark> an underlying reality, accentuate the <mark>futility of escape</mark> from this reality <mark>and mock dreams</mark> and fantasies of flight, and <mark>underscore</mark> <mark>subordination</mark> to faceless powers beyond one's control.
Bearing in mind that life always overflows our categories and analytical concepts, and that nothing in migrant lifeworlds is necessarily stagnant or permanent (Lucht, 2016), the article is not arguing for a rigid dichotomy between two separate and given modes of reality. However, it suggests a new analytical vocabulary for illuminating <mark>emerging migrant underworlds</mark> in which <mark>structural and</mark> actual, <mark>physical</mark>, <mark>violence</mark> have <mark>become</mark> the <mark>norm</mark> both in the workplace and on the high-risk migration routes (see also Besteman, 2020). Acknowledging Glick Schiller's call for a study of “dispossession and displacement” (2018: 201), the article hopes to address anthropological concerns about inequality, exploitation, and violent restrictions on human mobility.
Focusing on Ghanaian migrant experience in jeans production in Italy, the article begins with a brief ethnographic background to open a discussion of inequality and access to work and mobility in Italy. Then, it presents a critical reading and critique of Baudrillard's concept of hyperreality, followed by an exploration of the changing nature of reality in late-modern consumer society in contrast to the hyporeal worlds labor migrants inhabit. Next, three subsections highlight how the experience of space, time, and agency seem to move on a continuum between two opposing yet intimately connected extremes. Finally, the article discusses human practice as a form of counter-action, even under exceptional duress.
<mark>Hyporeal worlds</mark>, the article argues, are far from <mark>characterized by</mark> simulation and the evaporation of materiality, but rather by <mark>proximity to a merciless and absorbing reality</mark> that <mark>people</mark> on the fringes <mark>struggle</mark> to navigate and find ways of contesting.
“You work like an animal”
Denim was originally a utilitarian material, responding to harsh work environments. Pictures from the late 1800s of American railroad workers, cowboys, or miners portray subjects all wearing denim—durable, sturdy, blue cloth that has been aged by toil. But denim jeans are, unsurprisingly, new-looking when they are first put together. However, they rarely appear like that on shop shelves today. Consequently, making the textile look battered is a major industry, dominated by production facilities in Italy, which serve some of the biggest global fashion brands.
This worldwide demand for distressed garments has created a highly specialized labor market for migrants in Veneto, Italy. Jeans produced by Armani, Diesel, Dolce & Gabbana, Gap, Levi's, Zara, and many others are treated so as to appear work-worn and ripped by migrant workers from Africa and Asia. They find work on the production lines—some after having torn and shredded their own clothes in often-harrowing journeys across the Sahara Desert and the Mediterranean Sea.
This was the trajectory that Benjamin from Ghana's Central Region took when arriving from Libya in an inflatable boat in 2005, having almost perished in the Sahara Desert, and then in crossing the Mediterranean. He found employment with Martelli, one of the global leaders in the business of artificially aging garments, via his network of fellow West African laborers who worked on the production lines of northern Italy (Lucht, 2012, 2019). Benjamin's initial months were defined by his employer as a trial phase because he was hired without legal documents. “They want to see if we can do the work, so they give you six months without a contract.”
In the jeans factory the migrants, standing shoulder to shoulder, paint the jeans with chlorine, first on one side, then they turn the pants over and paint the other side too, sometimes using a steel wire brush to rip the denim and make it look torn. As Benjamin explained,
You put the pants on a kind of robot, pump it up, and make the leg stiff, then paint it with chlorine. We mix the chlorine with water to limit the strength. It's a kind of acid. You dip your brush in the chlorine and then you begin to roll it up and down the leg, from join to join. That's the finishing we do. This is our work.
Benjamin and his fellow co-workers found the working conditions difficult because of the fumes of the chemicals, which irritated their lungs and made their skin itch:2
When you finish work by five o’clock, your throat, it’ll be all dried up. The chlorine smells around you, all over you, and you feel so tired. You work like an animal. We wore a mask but there was little protection, the air we breathe … And the machines were so many. You’re here, somebody's there, like about 200 people working like that in the factory.
On top of that, the Ghanaian workers were not appreciated in the workplace, Benjamin explained, but instead abused, shouted at, and insulted. Ironically, <mark>migrant workers are no strangers to toil</mark>; they have <mark>suffered</mark> on the road, <mark>in the desert</mark> and on the sea, ripping and ruining their own clothes in the process. <mark>If they survive</mark> the ordeal, they are <mark>employed as</mark> experts in dirt and distress. Poor and downtrodden, they appear to be <mark>emissaries of filth</mark> and friction, tasked with making clothes look artificially damaged because the global middle class doesn’t have the time, the kinds of jobs, nor the limited clothing budgets that produce clothes that become worn through use, labor, hardship, and age.
Still, esthetically, global consumers seem to mourn this lost dimension of physical labor and the tangible reality of poverty to the extent of paying high prices for the fiction of manual labor and instantly “old” clothing. “Dressing poor” is not a new feature of the late-modern consumer society but its popularity has accelerated in recent years with designer clothes shot, bitten, burned, or even covered in fake mud. Not long ago a pair of Maison Margiela shoes with heavy rips and tears sold for US$1425 (Bramley, 2017).
Benjamin worked in this garment factory for 1 year and 3 months after receiving his permesso di sojourno in Italy, then suddenly this employment “collapsed.” He moved on and found his current job on another factory line, doing industrial painting. His work experience is characteristic of hyporeal migrant labor: precarious, exploited, and then suddenly terminated when the employer finds ways of extracting a higher rate of profit by other means or in other locations. As the chief executive of Martelli coolly explained to The New York Times around the time Benjamin was sacked, the company had gone through various groups of migrant laborers, eventually dismissing Africans in favor of Chinese who were chosen for their “patience” and “dexterity.” “We tried Romanians, and we tried Africans. None were as good as the Chinese” (Tagliabue, 2006).
While this article focuses on jeans production in Italy, it is just one empirical example of how fashions and <mark>products that</mark> seemingly playfully augment to or <mark>suspend reality for the privileged</mark> are <mark>closely connected to hyporeal</mark> migrant underworlds. Worlds where brute, overwhelming reality, and hard and dangerous labor conditions deprive migrants of their lives and health. As Seth Holmes (2013) has shown, for instance, the availability of fruit and vegetables throughout winter in the Global North is another example of migrant underworlds underpinning a world of privilege and play. <mark>Global middle-class</mark> consumers <mark>enjoy</mark> the <mark>quasi-magical</mark> <mark>abolition of the seasons</mark>, where strawberries are always available at low cost, but where the benefits of consuming fruits and vegetables are intimately <mark>linked to the systematic destruction in the United States</mark> (and Europe too) <mark>of migrant workers’</mark> health through chemical exposure, lack of clean drinking water or adequate sanitation, intense heat, and long hours of work (Holmes, 2013: 88–110).
The perseverance of the real
The torn jeans produced by Benjamin and his colleagues and consumed at a ravenous pace by a global middle class caught up in the play of images and simulacra, indicates that the real is under pressure from simulation. At the privileged end of the scale, we seem to increasingly live in the hyperreality that postmodernist thinkers like Umberto Eco and Jean Baudrillard predicted almost 40 years ago (Baudrillard, 1994; Eco, 1986). In Baudrillard's hyperreality there's a mix or implosion of representation and reality, or of signifier and signified, so that it's no longer possible to distinguish the two. As such, our distressed jeans are real fakes, mass produced consumer goods that have lost not only their link to the history of production but to the very reality they have substituted; a copy without origin and originality. It is a world in which reality and the representations of reality have been replaced by simulation and simulacra, and where people flee from the “desert of the real” into hyperreality and the endless ecstasy of simulation, engendered by the technology of “models” (Baudrillard, 1994: 1). As Baudrillard argues, it's no longer the question of making the map similar to the territory, or even to manipulate representations of the territory, but rather that there is no truer state of being or appearance than the map. The simulacra are all there is, and the traces of the real are rotting away. The real, on the other hand, has become a utopia, something we can only dream about, like a lost object. And when the real no longer is what it used to be, “nostalgia assumes its full meaning” (Baudrillard, 1994: 6).
In current politics, nostalgic sentiments of a lost golden age when things were simpler and more authentic are increasingly taking center stage, in both left- and right-wing politics, even if nostalgia in the service of exclusionary nationalist agendas and promotion of authoritarian attitudes appear to be limited to the far right (Elgenius and Rydgren, 2022). Yet an understanding of the appeal of nostalgia in current politics should not be reduced to the effect of simulation alone. As Elgenius and Rydgren (2022) show, economic deprivation and political upheaval must also be considered, as well as how the ensuing fear and resentment increase the longing and demand for a return to times perceived as safer, more harmonious, settled, and in ethno-nationalist versions, less multicultural (see also Angé and Berliner, 2015 for an overview of anthropological works on nostalgia).
Baudrillard's assertion that we are experiencing a paradigmatic break with modernity and a substitution of the real by the hyperreal—written well before the advent of the internet and the smartphone—has been heavily criticized for, among other things, being science fiction, nihilism, hyperbole, reckless, selective sampling, and technological determinism (see, e.g., Kellner, 2020; Poster, 1988; Rojek and Turner, 1993) and he is probably guilty on most counts.
But in the present, media-saturated world, Baudrillard has drawn renewed interest and a menacing relevance. The fact that a former reality television star, until recently, was the 45th president of the United States, and may be again, has been described as Baudrillard's hyperreality come home to roost by several observers (see Morris, 2021 for a recent overview) in the sense that reality television or simulation no longer resembles or represents reality but has become the very hyperreality we inhabit. The explicit assault on “reality” and the mockery of fact-based governance as performed currently by elected representatives and officials in various regimes around the world does seem to echo and validate Baudrillard's central observation that representation and reality have imploded to the extent that it is difficult to tell the difference, and many don’t even care anymore (Morris, 2021: 2).
Yet, what jeans production in Italy also shows us about our current moment is that even the most ephemeral products have a material history. There are still people laboring intensively, even if production has been increasingly distanced from the consumer, pushed out of sight and mind. <mark>The</mark> point is that the dreamlike and sometimes <mark>infantile world of</mark> play, <mark>simulation</mark>, and consumption <mark>is supported by</mark> <mark>a</mark> cheap, silenced, <mark>disposable</mark>, and low-wage <mark>migrant</mark> workforce essential to the production apparatus of the worldwide market economy. There is nothing new in this division, as Robin Cohen has shown. Modern capitalism has always consisted of different labor regimes, as “quasi-free workers from the countryside or peripheral zones are driven or sucked into the vortex of capitalist production” alongside skilled and “free” indigenous labor (Cohen, 1987: 2). Often drifting or <mark>floating from one job to another</mark>—they are a reserve of precarious, faceless, interchangeable workers who constitute what Guy Standing has termed “the light infantry of global capitalism” (2011: 113; see also De Genova, 2002).
In fact, it's only a logical progression for late-modern capitalism to substitute “real” products and production with simulacra and simulation (think of events industries or social media conglomerates for example), since ephemeral products have shorter lifespans that raise consumption to a higher pace. Or as David Harvey argues, rather than hyperreality disrupting accumulation, “The commodification of images of the most ephemeral sort would seem to be a godsend from the standpoint of capital accumulation” (1992: 288). Think again of the ripped jeans produced by the migrants above. An untreated pair of jeans would not only fail to dazzle consumers but would also have a longer lifecycle that would decelerate consumption.
But there's another, perhaps more anthropological angle from which to question Baudrillard's notion of hyperreality. The concept is obviously Eurocentric and doesn’t consider the fact that the majority of the world's population live in poverty and expectancy of things to come while coping or not with the shocks of reality, an excess of reality really, hampering and curtailing any possible flight into fantasy. “We are no longer part of the drama of alienation; we live in the ecstasy of communication,” argues Baudrillard (1983: 130), seemingly forgetting about most of the people of the world. This is also Susan Sontag's critique of Baudrillard; that the notion of hyperreality is a form of “breathtaking provincialism” because, “it universalizes the viewing habits of a small, educated population living in the rich part of the world, where news has been converted into entertainment” (2003: 85) when the vast majority of people on earth are living lives that are in no way liberated from “the reality principle,” but rather are living arrested lives where hopes and dreams are constantly put on hold, or indefinitely postponed because of unsurmountable obstacles. As Sontag (2003: 85) argues, “There are hundreds of millions of television watchers, who are far from inured to what they see on television. They do not have the luxury of patronizing reality”.
Young West African <mark>migrants</mark> like Benjamin, for instance, may <mark>aspire to become part of the bright world of hyperreality</mark>, and many, even in poor villages, have <mark>Twitter</mark> and Facebook <mark>accounts</mark> where they <mark>live out</mark> various <mark>personas</mark> and follow the global dispersion of images. Yet <mark>the means to fulfill</mark> dreams are <mark>slipping out of their hands.</mark> They too belong to the late-modern global community of spectators, but they are not lost in simulation, nor immune to the shocks of reality, but more like playthings of circumstances, weighed down by an excessive, encroaching reality. In this world, the migrant must make a choice between abandonment to his or her fate, or outright exploitation. As Appadurai (2006: 35) argues, “There's a double anxiety: fear of inclusion, on draconian terms, and fear of exclusion, for this seems like exclusion from history itself”.
So, rather than reality vanishing and giving rise to a simulated hyperreality, it appears that reality manifests itself with brute and unshielded force in migrant underworlds. Labor <mark>migrants reproduce</mark> the <mark>pain</mark> and suffering <mark>inflicted upon them in the borderlands</mark> and the workplace <mark>in the form of high-end simulacra for</mark> the <mark>global consumers</mark> thus seems to represent an entangled, globalized world intimately connected through the clothes we wear, the gadgets we use, and the food we eat (see also Cross, 2013). Or an example of hyperreality and hyporeality existing side by side, with the latter, heavy with ominous reality, underpinning the former.
In the following three sections, to further illuminate emerging migrant underworlds, I will explore how life in hyporeality is given form and content by certain changes in space, time, and agency.
Hyporeality and space
While consumers may be enticed into cyberspace worlds of fantasy, the hyporeal territory remains an actual, physical territory, moreover an increasingly determined and dangerous one. Irregular labor migration to Europe and North America almost always entails exposure to this territory's dark version of geopolitics, which offers growing resistance to human mobility and manifests itself physically and violently on migrant bodies. In fact, running the gauntlet of these dangerous borderlands—whether through <mark>Libya</mark>, the Sahara, <mark>Greece</mark>, the Balkans, the Darién Gap, <mark>the Sonora Desert</mark>, or across the English Channel—<mark>appear</mark>s <mark>to be</mark> a commonplace <mark>shock treatment that leaves</mark> labor <mark>migrants in</mark> ever-<mark>weaker positions even before being drafted into</mark> abusive <mark>underground</mark> economies.
Benjamin and his companions suffered on the road before finding work of distressing jeans. He almost lost his life when their car broke down in Niger and they had to walk in the desert, an incident Benjamin still feels too traumatized to revisit. His experience is not uncommon. Labor migrants traveling irregularly to Italy via Libya are routinely exposed to extreme distress before entering the underground job market. This route is the most dangerous migration route in the world, and annually claims thousands of lives. In February 2023, 72 migrants and refugees drowned off Calabria, including 28 children and teens (ANSA, 2023). What was unusual about that particular tragedy, however, was not the massive death and suffering, but that the bodies washed up on Europe's shores—migrant bodies are usually carried away by the sea or wash up in North Africa.
Those who make it across carry <mark>with</mark> them both mental and physical <mark>scars</mark>. They endure thirst and hunger, the heat of the desert, the chill of the sea, and sheer wear and tear—scars, broken bones, cigarette burns, bullet holes, slave labor, imprisonment, scabies infestations, beatings, electrocution, or rape. Meanwhile, some may not make it at all, as recent disasters have reminded us. In short, the labor migrants face an avalanche of distress even before coming to Europe.
<mark>In these hyporeal borderlands</mark>, the <mark>shackles of humanitarian pretense are off</mark> and <mark>state power appears</mark> almost <mark>turbo-charged by cruelty</mark> and physical manifestations. “The gloomy festival of punishment” (Foucault, 1991: 8) on the Mediterranean and in Libya's detention centers seems to constitute a return to government-by-atrocity that <mark>echoes the violence of the colonies</mark>, if not medieval torture spectacles. This borderland appears to exist surprisingly well on the edges of the modern humanitarian and egalitarian European state with its institutions and biopower. Here, we are perhaps seeing the paradox Foucault describes when he notes that the transformation of power from the sovereign to the nation-state has only made wars even more bloody because now, “They are waged on behalf of the existence of everyone; entire populations are mobilized for the purpose of wholesale slaughter in the name of life necessity; massacres have become vital” (1990: 137). In the intensified struggle to curb migration in the name of necessity, the brutality of the borderlands reveals what is at stake: the imagined survival of Europe, its freedom, and prosperity (see Albahari, 2006).
While these worlds may seem strictly separated by the prohibitions against violence and causing death in one and their normalization in the other, they are at the same time entangled and mutually constituted. This is the fundamental and paradoxical contradiction of the current moment, Jean-François Bayart argues, “The liberalization of the international markets of capital and goods on the one hand [depends on] … the coercive division of labor on the other” (2007: 268). In other words, first people are discarded from the global economy, made into a silent reserve, and then they are arbitrarily incorporated “…into an institutionally racist and exploitative labor market” (Cross, 2013: 215).
Consequently, if our current hyperreal moment is characterized by a heightened phase of mobility in the more privileged parts of the world and a reorganization of space and time, or a regular space–time compression, that has fundamentally disoriented and disrupted economic and political practices as well socio-cultural life (Harvey, 1992: 284), then it appears to create the contrary conditions for the dispossessed. While distances may disappear, and delays are eradicated to facilitate the easy flow of people and capital between the usual power centers of the world, in other parts of the world people, rather, experience a space–time expansion as distances grow, obstacles become insurmountable, and the waiting becomes endless. Necessary journeys become longer, harder, more risky, and more expensive, if it's possible to move anywhere at all. This is a globalized world that appears to grow and shrink at the same time as pervasive global linkages create many possibilities for the few and take away the few possibilities remaining for the many (Castles, 1998: 117).
To the majority who live in “the desert of the real” the goalposts are constantly moving, and lines being redrawn in their disfavor—the rescue boats literally grounded—while the desert expands and lays waste to hope and opportunity. <mark>Hyporeality is a world of</mark> unrestrained limitations, consisting of actual and emerging physical <mark>boundaries</mark>, <mark>as well as</mark> the fine-grained and minuscule <mark>panoptic networks of surveillance</mark> and separation that permeate every aspect of modern life. In this sense, <mark>the border is no longer a fixed structure but</mark> atomized, and a reality the migrant runs up against constantly (Balibar, 2002: 82). Or, as Mbembe argues, “Borders are increasingly turned into mobile, portable, <mark>omnipresent</mark> and ubiquitous realities,” representing not only spatial demarcation lines, “…they are the name we should use to describe the organized violence that underpins both contemporary capitalism and our world order in general” (2019b: 9).
<mark>This carnival of suffering is played out in</mark> a <mark>loop</mark>. Not a week passes without footage of deaths and drownings and new forms of abuse directed at migrants and refugees in the EU African borderlands. <mark>It's not happening in secret anymore</mark>, on the contrary. In hyperreality, Baudrillard argues, there's an evasive transparency of all things, an oversaturation and schizophrenic proximity to information that, in turn, lies at the heart of the paralysis and loss of meaning (1983: 132–134). Incidentally, if there's an over-proximity to anything in hyporeality, it is to the hard facts of reality. It goes beyond the scope of this article to explore the crisis of the mass media and the confusion they increasingly create but, again, Baudrillard's dystopian take appears to ask necessary questions of the shallow idea that if only human suffering is brought to the fore, if only more information is reported, then somehow change will follow; an ideal that has long ago been belied by political reality. Journalists, anthropologists, writers, and filmmakers, all report, sometimes in real time, on the war on migration and its many casualties, often from aboard the few remaining NGO rescue vessels. But the more information is circulated about human suffering, the less meaning and interest it seems to generate (see Baudrillard, 1994: 79). Suffering takes place in plain sight and is accepted, sometimes with regret and apathy, sometimes without; as a “vital massacre” in the maintenance of European peace and prosperity.
Policy debates about encryption are complicit in the ‘semiotic motor of the necropolitical’---discussions fail to create an accessible space on the receiving end of violence AND train students to be part of the sacrificial economy.

Second, <mark>encryption advocacy</mark> frequently <mark>ignores</mark> the <mark>ways</mark> in which <mark>surveillance is</mark> fundamentally <mark>structured through intersecting lines of</mark> race, class, gender, ability, and <mark>nationality</mark>. For example, advocates often frame the problem of government surveillance as having to do with the scope of data collection; the problem, they argue, is that all individuals are under suspicion, rather than the few who should be investigated (Gürses et al., 2016). This framing establishes a problematic demarcation between mass and targeted surveillance, with the latter being implicitly (or sometimes explicitly) held up as the desirable state of affairs. Therefore:
these <mark>discussions fail to give space to</mark> those who are on the receiving end of global <mark>surveillance</mark> programs <mark>and linked</mark> forms of <mark>violence</mark>, such as extrajudicial <mark>killing, torture, and impoverishment</mark> <mark>including populations</mark> in the United States and Europe <mark>whose racialization has been integral to</mark> histories of <mark>colonialism</mark>, plantation <mark>slavery, and empire</mark>. (Gürses et al., 2016, p. 577).
In general, technolegal framings of interventions into surveillance tend to be depoliticizing. Although activists often face pressure to frame their arguments in terms their targets will find acceptable—for instance, by arguing to cash-strapped local governments that mass surveillance is expensive—to achieve their goals, doing so can also “[limit] our understanding of the implications of these data-driven practices that underpin contemporary surveillance and [dilute] their politicized nature” (Dencik et al., 2016, p. 8).
<mark>To remedy</mark> these <mark>shortcomings</mark>, scholars have proposed new <mark>frameworks</mark> which <mark>center analyses of racism and white supremacy</mark>. Rexhepi (2016, p. 2) argues that “<mark>to unsettle</mark>, decenter and rethink <mark>veillance theories</mark> beyond the taxonomies of surveillance inside liberal democracies, <mark>a critical intervention needs to destabilize and disrupt</mark> sur/<mark>violence by shifting its focus from the center to the periphery</mark>.” Simone Browne’s work is a crucial step in this direction. Browne (2015) centers theorizing race and racism in thinking through the dynamics and consequences of both historical and contemporary surveillance. In doing so, Browne (2015, p. 8) develops the concept of racializing surveillance, in which “<mark>enactments of surveillance reify boundaries along racial lines</mark>, thereby reifying race, and where <mark>the outcome</mark> of this <mark>is</mark> often <mark>discriminatory and violent treatment</mark>.” <mark>Racializing surveillance</mark> does not solely distribute harms along racial lines, but simultaneously <mark>shapes collective understandings of race</mark>.
Countersurveillance advocates also reproduce and reify understandings of race as they attempt to counteract state surveillance schemes. For instance, advocates frequently refer to George Orwell’s 1984 as a quintessential example of the dystopian future we will inevitably realize if we allow the government to engage in mass surveillance (Gürses et al., 2016). The problem is that Orwell tacitly depicts mass surveillance as normalized where non-Western countries are concerned due to his use of totalizing mass surveillance as the novum of his dystopian United Kingdom; relying on this novel to understand the dynamics of mass surveillance “undermine[s] a political reading that would attend to the racial, gendered, classed, and colonial aspects of the surveillance programs” being critiqued (Gürses et al., 2016, p. 577). The use of such touchstones, centered as they are on the threat of figures of the Other and their oppressive, immoral practices as counter to the intrinsic values of Western states, should attune us to the broader use of such mythological tropes when considering the real policy debates surrounding surveillance, such as the regulation and use of encryption technologies; this is quite evident in our discourse analysis. In the following section we therefore turn to a semiological treatment of the rhetoric of the Crypto Wars to help unveil the mythic representations of racialized populations which prove foundational for understanding the rhetorical tactics of both state and private sector actors as they negotiate the political reality of regulating encryption technology.
A Semiology of the Crypto Wars
<mark>Policy debates over encryption are</mark> often <mark>leavened with mythological language</mark> by all concerned to more provocatively ground arguments made by state actors for the intrinsic security risks of unfettered cryptographic technology and, conversely, those made by private sector actors for less restrictive regulations in order to maintain competitive advantage in the global market. The mythological language used is entirely <mark>premised on representing long-standing</mark>, totemic figures which encapsulate deviant <mark>stereotypes</mark> common <mark>of racialized populations</mark>, rearticulated within the context of encryption, digital communication, and the security threats they represent. Here, we use mythology as articulated by Roland Barthes (1972, p. 114-5), for whom:
[myth] is constructed from a semiological chain which existed before it: it is a second-order semiological system. That which is a sign (namely the associative total of a concept and an image) in the first system, becomes a mere signifier in the second. We must here recall that the materials of mythical speech […] are reduced to a pure signifying function as soon as they are caught by myth. Hyporeality and agency
As suggested above, lack of rights and protection, be they social, legal, or political, appears to be a pervasive feature of hyporeality, combined with an excess of reality. <mark>Lifeworlds are</mark> at once <mark>sucked hollow</mark> so that people are socially and existentially, if not physically, choking, causing hibernation and passivity and sometimes death, while at the same time infused with the all-consuming urgency of the here and now. Yet, as mentioned earlier, nothing in migrant lifeworlds is necessarily stagnant or permanent and they are susceptible to human practice. “Human beings not meant to move do move, and those not meant to stay do stay,” Maurice Stierl argues (2019: 1, italics in the original). Even in the most difficult and dangerous situations, Michael Jackson writes, people always seek to be “complicit in their fate” (1998: 30).
Actions and modes of opposition, however, rarely take the form of direct resistance to political and structural oppression, as Mbembe argues about the post-colony. Rather we should look out for, “the myriad ways ordinary people guide, deceive, and toy with power instead of confronting it directly” (Mbembe 2001: 128; see also Cohen, 1987: 181). In short, what hypopraxes are possible in the maelstrom of space-time expansion?
Incidentally, Baudrillard is not occupied with subjective agency, nor is Virilio. They identify no point from which to revolt against the current predicament. In hyperreality we’re all extras in a reality show, or “pure screens.” There are no intersubjective relations, but only “relational décor,” as the human subject floats weightless around hyperreality like an astronaut, “Isolated in a position of perfect and remote sovereignty, at an infinite distance from his universe of origin” (Baudrillard, 1983: 128).
In hyporeality, on the other hand, the <mark>dispossessed cannot afford to ignore reality</mark>. As Sontag reminds us, they need to be constantly alert to its many vicissitudes and real-life complications. At the same time, many are not content with living a life of poverty and degradation; they aspire to something more, more than what they have been thrown into. Perhaps they aspire to little else than being part of our globalized, hyperreal world but that's still an action that requires imagination and agency, and which clashes violently with the current geopolitical order, or perhaps disorder, creating a mass grave on the doorstep of Europe.
The journey to Europe, then, could be seen as representing one such practice of challenging hyporeality, a form of indirect resistance to a life of poverty and stasis. Though not political acts per se, migrant journeys seem to represent a form of “migrant iconoclasm” (Lucht, 2016: 158) that engenders certain political transformations (Stierl, 2019: 2). They are symbolic but also physical attacks on a perceived sacred geopolitical order that is met with necropolitical deterrence. That is, in seeking a life deemed worth living, the labor migrants I encountered challenge and circumvent hardening European spatial imaginaries and are met with a savagery that reveals what's at stake: the imagined survival of Europe.
Yet <mark>migrants</mark> somehow <mark>still manage to cheat death of its pound of flesh in the borderlands</mark>, <mark>demonstrating</mark> room for solidarity and <mark>resistance in hyporeality</mark>, albeit <mark>discreetly and indirectly</mark>. CK's story illuminates this ongoing struggle between migrants’ dreams and desires for life and the “vital massacres” through which they must plot a course.
CK, one of Benjamin's closest friends from Senya Beraku, had worked on and off in the jeans factory too, but was now stacking fruit in a warehouse during the harvest season (Lucht, 2019). I had heard stories about CK from other Ghanaian collaborators for some time, because of his courage on the road, but there was nothing extraordinary about his demeanor. Unlike Benjamin, he was soft-spoken and not very talkative, visibly distressed by the hardships in Italy: the chronic lack of work and the impossibility of making plans when there was no telling what tomorrow would bring. But when their boat capsized off the coast of Tripoli in 2005, CK swam out and saved the life of a mutual friend, Yakubu, and many others. Embarrassingly for a young man from a fishing village, Yakubu couldn’t swim. He was drowning and cried out for CK, who had managed to save himself from the wreck that had happened in the surf close to the shore. “He was going down, seriously. He was never good at swimming,” CK smiled, recalling how he and the other fishermen from Senya Beraku went back into the sea to rescue Yakubu, and then their fellow African migrants until they were all safe, except for two people who did not respond to first aid, and who lost their lives that morning. To keep warm, they built a fire on the beach and dried their clothes. Some weeks later they tried the crossing again, and this time they made it.
Benjamin noticed my silence following CK's humble recollection of these heroic events and said: “Now, you’re seeing the real CK.” Unfortunately, CK's bravery in the hostile borderlands had not been matched by material success. He had stayed in Italy for 10 years without regular work (see Lucht, 2019). But the rescue story remained, now sometimes recounted as a farce, and the way Yakubu had cried out with a trembling and gasping voice had become a joke with which everybody greeted CK.
There are many things to learn from this anecdote. First, migrants are not necessarily powerless in the face of the overwhelming distress in the borderlands. <mark>Violence</mark>, in its most deadly manifestations, may be a <mark>central organizing power</mark> in hyporeality, but migrants find ways to navigate the hostile terrain. The <mark>swimmers from</mark> <mark>Ghana</mark>'s coastal communities literally <mark>dragged themselves and</mark> their compatriots <mark>out of</mark> the claws of the borderland's <mark>deadly embrace</mark>. Though unreported, the <mark>events</mark> of that morning, the everyday solidarity and courage displayed, <mark>live on</mark> in the memory of the Ghanaian migrants <mark>as</mark> one of those little big <mark>instances when power was deceived</mark> of its prey, when the tables were turned, when <mark>resistance</mark>, however, small and insignificant, to the current geopolitical order, <mark>succeeded</mark>. When we got to see the “real CK.”
Moreover, human time persists. Human time is the time of myth and ritual, of nature and the gods, that allows us to momentarily reclaim a lost or broken human rhythm, albeit in constant tension with the external time regimes of hyporeality. CK's story, for instance, with its tempo and reorganization of reality, takes us into migrant lore to remind us of human resilience in hyporeality but also to reset the clock that has run down on his own project in the present. He refused to be swept away by the waves off Libya, to let his colleagues die, and emerges from the story as a guardian of migrant lives and time, even if his own trajectory has fallen into great uncertainty.
Thirdly, <mark>migrants always advance through transgression</mark> (see also Stierl, 2019). <mark>To advance they must breach borders</mark>—<mark>physical</mark>, <mark>social</mark>, and <mark>political</mark> borders—<mark>and risk their lives</mark> in the process. This recurring migrant brinkmanship often characterizes practices in hyporeality. It's always <mark>all or nothing</mark>, <mark>stay home</mark> and <mark>you’re</mark> (<mark>socially</mark>, symbolically) <mark>dead</mark> or <mark>go</mark> to Europe and <mark>you’re dead too</mark>. <mark>When a migrant dreams</mark> of something different, <mark>a better life</mark> economically, socially, and existentially, he or she [they] is not just up <mark>against the limits of individual power</mark> but also against the dark geopolitics of hyporeality that dictates that poor people stay in the places they come from or suffer the consequences. As Zygmunt Bauman argues, the most unfortunate segments of the world's <mark>population</mark> are “annexed” to the land even if it <mark>no longer offers a life worth living</mark>, and wherever they turn up and seek shelter they are <mark>seen as vagabonds</mark> and trespassers (1998: 18).
Nevertheless, desire for life is difficult to snuff out, even if a struggle to govern subjective aspirations is emerging as a kind of final frontier in Europe's dark geopolitics. In an echo of colonial educational cinema, EU-financed information campaigns tour the West African countryside and hinterland villages with mobile cinemas, showing films of suffering and depravation on the road, and enlisting “failed migrants” to bear testimony to the dangers and futility of seeking change (Vammen, 2021). The hope is to preempt migration by crushing would-be migrant trajectories before they take form, by getting migrants to internalize borders and the related violence in what observers have called “aspiration management” (Carling and Collins, 2018).
Finally, concurrent with everyday forms of resistance and bravery, migrants are storytellers, as we saw in CK's narrative, and storytelling as an intersubjective practice remains one of the most powerful avenues for re-enchanting the otherwise frozen and hostile terrain of hyporeality. Storytelling makes the world inhabitable even in the most difficult and dangerous times, as Michael Jackson has powerfully shown (2002). CK's story of rescuing his fellow migrant travelers from certain death in the surf off Libya—and his fellow migrants’ narration and commemoration of the story—celebrates how, even in hyporeality, human agency makes a difference; how everyday bravery and sacrifice are recognized; how solidarity with one's fellow human beings, even at the risk of one's own life, reaffirms a stubborn horizon of morality that refuses to yield to the necropolitics of the borderlands. Storytelling “…aid[s] and abet[s] our need to believe that we may discern and determine the meaning of our journey through life: where we came from and where we are going” (Jackson, 2002: 16).
Making the world one we have a hand in creating through storytelling, as well as other, everyday forms of resistance and re-enchantment, has real experiential implications for a sense of agency in hyporeality and is perhaps the existential oxygen supply that stands between perseverance and breakdown. It gives hope that human actions matter, and that change is possible in the way it turns the tables on power, “Thus allowing us to feel that we actively participate in a world that for a moment seemed to discount, demean, and disempower us” (Jackson, 2002: 16). In recounting CK's story, the Ghanaian migrants also seemed to resist the hyporeal categorization of CK—a subhuman, working the dark machinery of an increasingly childish hyperreal world lost in play and consumption—and “the real CK” was allowed to flicker for a moment in Benjamin's small kitchen in Veneto, far from home and family.
There's another form of storytelling among migrants in hyporeality, too. It serves the same pragmatic purpose, to be able to cope in a hostile environment, but it takes a great toll: the <mark>prediction of inevitable loss</mark> and defeat in the face of overwhelming obstacles or what I have elsewhere <mark>called defeatist agency</mark> (Lucht, 2012: 82). Some <mark>migrants</mark>, having faced years of harsh opposition and failure, <mark>become experts in predicting how things will</mark> eventually <mark>go wrong</mark>—<mark>the underside of resistance</mark> and perseverance—yet this too is a <mark>way of making</mark> the <mark>world less complex and</mark> more predictable and <mark>manageable</mark>, however, psychologically hard such a mental state of emergency remains (see Luhmann, 1999: 125).
How the migrants will be received in Europe—as heroes of anti-globalization, as calculating economic opportunists (Cohen, 1987) or perhaps as not even as human at all—remains a contested question. But having made it to Europe and found work in the underground is no guarantee that things will go smoothly from there on, as we have seen. The world is never there for the taking, it's always beyond another border. Once in Italy, African migrants often end up in endo-colonial type work situations, on factory lines or in farm fields, or waiting at roundabouts and junctions in Naples hoping to pick up day labor (Lucht, 2012).
Yet, the tenacity of human practice, even under extreme duress, makes a qualitative difference to subjective experience of space and time. <mark>In fact</mark>, <mark>human practice can be a place of resistance</mark> in itself, <mark>a</mark> counter site, or <mark>heterotopia</mark> (Foucault, 1984: 3), <mark>to</mark> the <mark>dominant</mark> spatial and temporal <mark>properties of hyporeality</mark>. <mark>Migrants walk</mark> the streets of often undesirable or abandoned neighborhoods, for instance, <mark>and in that walking make these places their own</mark>, or appropriate them (de Certeau, 1988: xix, 97). Migrants put up and <mark>establish rogue social</mark> and physical <mark>infrastructure in</mark> these <mark>places that</mark> otherwise <mark>lack</mark> legal, social, and political <mark>protection</mark>. They construct, for instance, shanty towns in the long shadow of the border, spontaneous refugee camps in inner cities, or they <mark>squat derelict buildings</mark>. They <mark>establish underground communication</mark> and transportation too, sell SIM cards, make money transfers, <mark>organize fake paperwork</mark>, and somehow <mark>find ways into the draconian job markets</mark>.
Migrant infrastructure, therefore, is an ongoing fixation of the nation-state, since it challenges spatial domination and is thus the target of an ongoing “infrastructural warfare,” as Mbembe has framed it (2019c: 82). This is one of the characteristics of necropower, or the subjugation of life to the power of death, that informs hyporeality. It looks not only to render people actual or living dead, but also to sabotage any unwanted social infrastructure by demolishing ghettoes, tearing down empty buildings, and bulldozing tented camps (Mbembe, 2019c). As seen, for instance, in the “Jungle” of Calais where French authorities force migrants and refugees from their makeshift shelters in the borderlands between France and the United Kingdom.
Yet <mark>migrants persevere</mark> and through everyday practices turn hostile places into temporary “homes” (Lucht, 2012: 82), <mark>though</mark> these <mark>advances are rarely theirs to keep.</mark> In a process that begins with urban decline, regeneration and then gentrification, migrants create value that is then likely to be reappropriated by capital interest and real estate developers as unwanted areas come to be seen as exciting and bohemian, and the original inhabitants are pushed out (Morell, 2015; see also Çağlar and Glick Schiller, 2018 for an overview of the literature on gentrification vis-à-vis migrant and minority inhabitants).
