A. The War Powers Resolution: From Reassertion to Ritual
Congress enacted the War Powers Resolution in 1973 in response to executive excesses during the Vietnam War.151 Its purpose was explicit: “to insure that the collective judgment of both the Congress and the President will apply” to the introduction of U.S. forces into hostilities.152 The Resolution imposed reporting requirements, time limits, and a mechanism for congressional termination of unauthorized engagements. At enactment, many scholars viewed the Resolution as a meaningful reassertion of legislative authority.153 Its structure reflected confidence that procedural constraints could discipline executive behavior even absent judicial enforcement.
B. Executive Resistance and Doctrinal Evasion
Presidents of both parties have resisted the Resolution from its inception, often questioning its constitutionality while selectively complying with its reporting provisions.154 More damagingly, administrations have adopted <mark>narrow interpretations</mark> of “hostilities” to <mark>avoid</mark> triggering the Resolution’s time <mark>limits</mark>.155
The Supreme Court’s decision in INS v. Chadha, invalidating the legislative veto, further weakened the Resolution by eliminating Congress’s most direct enforcement mechanism.156 Although <mark>Congress</mark> retains the theoretical power of the purse, it has <mark>rarely exercise</mark>d it.157 It is much easier to exercise the <mark>power</mark> of the purse <mark>before hostilities</mark> are initiated. Doing so <mark>after</mark> the fact <mark>would</mark> almost certainly <mark>be politically detrimental</mark> to any sitting Congress member. <mark>It</mark> i<mark>s</mark> almost <mark>impossible</mark> to be <mark>re-elect</mark>ed <mark>as the</mark> Congress <mark>member that voted not to protect</mark> our <mark>troops</mark> overseas.
As such, the check that was put into place to reign in executive use of force is no longer viable.158 As such, today the <mark>War Powers</mark> Resolution functions less as a constraint than as a ritual. Reports are filed; <mark>debates are</mark> held; resolutions are introduced—and then <mark>abandoned</mark>.159 The Maduro seizure passed through this ritual <mark>without consequence</mark>, confirming long-standing scholarly warnings that the Resolution has become functionally obsolete.160

XII. Foreign Force and Domestic Democratic Erosion
The seizure of President Nicolás Maduro cannot be fully understood through the lens of international war powers alone. It must also be situated within a broader pattern of democratic erosion in which foreign uses of force operate as accelerants of domestic authoritarianism. Comparative constitutional scholarship has increasingly demonstrated that democratic <mark>backsliding</mark> rarely proceeds through sudden rupture. Instead, it <mark>advances through</mark> the <mark>normalization of extraordinary power</mark>s <mark>exercised in legally ambiguous spaces</mark>.169 The <mark>use of</mark> military <mark>force abroad</mark>—especially when framed as necessary, limited, or exceptional—has proven to be one of the most effective such spaces to slowly erode democratic principles on the path toward authoritarianism.
Foreign force is uniquely potent in this regard because it <mark>lies at the intersection of secrecy</mark>, <mark>nationalism</mark>, <mark>and emergency</mark>. It allows executives to claim necessity while resisting oversight, to invoke security while suppressing dissent, <mark>and</mark> to redefine legality through practice rather than law. Over time, these dynamics reshape domestic constitutional culture, rendering once exceptional powers routine.170
The Maduro seizure exemplifies this process. By presenting a hostile military operation as neither war nor aggression but as executive law enforcement, the Administration simultaneously expanded its coercive capacity abroad and weakened institutional resistance at home.
A. Hungary and the Externalization of Emergency
Hungary <mark>provides</mark> a paradigmatic example of how external threats are used to consolidate internal power. Under Prime Minister Viktor Orbán, the Hungarian government has repeatedly invoked migration, terrorism, and foreign instability to justify emergency measures that erode judicial independence, constrain civil society, and entrench executive dominance.171 Although Hungary’s uses of force abroad have been limited, the <mark>logic</mark> of externalization—treating foreign threats as justification <mark>for</mark> domestic restructuring—has been central to its <mark>constitutional transformation</mark>.
What matters for comparative purposes is not scale but function. Orbán’s government <mark>leveraged</mark> foreign crises to normalize emergency governance, often without formal declarations or clear endpoints. Over time, these measures ceased to be temporary responses and became permanent features of Hungarian constitutional law.172 The danger here is obvious. Historically the United States has historically resisted this pattern through stronger separation-of-powers norms. But as the Maduro seizure demonstrates, those norms are weakening. When foreign force is deployed without congressional authorization and without judicial review, the <mark>in</mark>stitutional pathways that once prevented <mark>Hungari</mark>an-style consolidation begin to erode.
B. Turkey: Militarization and Executive Personalization
The Hungarian slide into normalizing emergency governance is not the only cautionary tale. Turkey’s constitutional trajectory offers an even more direct warning. Over the past two decades, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan has systematically fused foreign military action with domestic political consolidation. Beginning with operations against Kurdish forces in northern Iraq and Syria, Erdoğan has deployed military interventions not merely to address external security threats, but to strengthen his domestic political position and justify authoritarian measures at home.173 Following the failed 2016 coup attempt, Erdoğan's government declared a state of emergency that lasted two years, during which tens of thousands were detained and Turkey's parliamentary system was transformed into an executive presidency with sweeping powers.174 Turkish operations in Syria, Iraq, and against Kurdish forces have been framed as existential security measures, enabling Erdoğan to marginalize opposition parties, weaken parliamentary oversight, and expand executive decree authority.175
Crucially, Turkey’s foreign operations were not merely external. They were performative—used to construct a narrative of indispensability around the executive. Military action abroad reinforced the claim that centralized authority was necessary at home. Over time, constitutional amendments codified this concentration of power. 176
The lesson for the United States is not that it will exactly replicate Turkey’s trajectory. That is not the fear. The fear is that executive control over foreign force creates unchecked feedback loops. The more unilateral the force, the weaker the oversight; the weaker the oversight, the easier it becomes to justify further unilateralism. The Maduro seizure exemplifies this dangerous pattern.
C. Russia <mark>Before Ukraine</mark>: Normalization Before Rupture
Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022 is often treated as a sharp break of acceptable use of force. But comparative scholars have emphasized that the invasion was preceded by years of incremental normalization of executive military power. Following Russia's 2008 invasion of Georgia, domestic institutions demonstrated no appetite for checking executive military decisions.177 The Duma rubber-stamped military operations; courts dismissed challenges to deployments as non-justiciable political questions; and state-controlled media amplified official narratives without critical scrutiny.178 Russia's 2014 annexation of Crimea and intervention in eastern Ukraine further entrenched this pattern.179 Each operation expanded presidential authority while dulling domestic resistance.180
Thus, what made Ukraine possible domestically was not merely geopolitical ambition, but institutional habituation. Russian courts, <mark>legislature</mark>, and media had already <mark>internalized</mark> the idea that <mark>foreign force lay beyond</mark> meaningful <mark>constraint.</mark>181 By the time full-scale invasion occurred, the legal and political infrastructure for resistance had long since collapsed.182
The Maduro seizure places the <mark>U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates on a dangerously similar path as Russia. It demonstrates how precedent operates asymmetrically, and how powerful states shape norms through action. When they erode constraints incrementally, they weaken not only their own institutions but the global expectation that force will be checked by law. Thus, the Maduro seizure has destabilized the entire global order and has the potential for worse, as it extends what is acceptable in international use of force law.

The Maduro seizure illustrates how these dynamics converge even in a mature constitutional democracy. 185 Democracies, like the United States, historically rely on institutional authorization and public debate to check the country’s use of force. A signal of democratic erosion is the bypassing of such checks.186 As those mechanisms weaken, foreign force becomes an increasingly attractive site for <mark>executive experimentation</mark>. The Maduro seizure was not an aberration; it was a stress test the system failed.187
XIV. Norm Cascades and Authoritarian Mimicry
One of the most troubling aspects of unilateral force by powerful democracies is its demonstration effect. Norms do not erode symmetrically or in a vacuum. When influential states defect, others follow. And not always by imitating the exact factual scenarios, but by adopting the same rationales.188
The claim that force may be used to seize foreign leaders under the guise of law enforcement is easily portable. It requires no declaration of war, no Security Council vote, and no domestic authorization. It supplies precisely the kind of legal ambiguity authoritarian leaders exploit.189 <mark>How can the U.S.</mark> effectively <mark>check</mark> other nation’s <mark>future power grabs</mark> – <mark>perhaps</mark> most destabilizing a potential Chinese takeover of <mark>Taiwan</mark> – <mark>if America</mark> has <mark>set</mark> the <mark>precedent</mark> such unilateral and unjustified use <mark>of</mark> force is <mark>acceptable</mark> as a police action? In this sense, the Maduro seizure does not merely weaken U.S. law. It reshapes the global normative environment in which future uses of force will be judged. Because Executives who can act without authorization and without consequence will eventually do so for less compelling reasons.190
The Maduro seizure was justified as necessary and exceptional. But it was also unreviewed and unpunished. That combination—not motive—is the hallmark of democratic erosion. If the United States is to avoid the trajectories observed in Hungary, Turkey, and Russia, it must treat the Maduro seizure not as a discrete controversy, but as a warning. Foreign force without constraint is not merely a violation of law. It is a mechanism of constitutional decay.191
XV. Structural Constraints & Core Bargain
International law has long recognized head-of-state immunity as a structural feature of sovereign equality rather than a personal privilege.192 Immunity protects international stability by preventing unilateral coercion through domestic courts and executive action.193 Although modern international criminal law has narrowed immunity in multilateral forums, it has never authorized unilateral military seizure by individual states.194 The distinction between prosecution and apprehension is critical: jurisdiction is not equal to a license to use <mark>unilateral force</mark>.195
A. Nuremberg, the ICC, and Multilateral Accountability
Nuremberg is often invoked to justify aggressive accountability measures, but it proves the opposite of what unilateralists suggest. Following World War II, the Allied powers (United States, United Kingdom, France, and Soviet Union) established the International Military Tribunal (IMT) at Nuremberg, Germany, to prosecute major Nazi war criminals.196 The tribunal established crucial precedents for individual criminal responsibility under international law, rejected superior orders as an absolute defense, and affirmed that heads of state could be held accountable for international crimes.197 As such, the Nuremberg prosecutions followed total war and collective military defeat, not peacetime abduction.198 The International Criminal Court similarly operates through consent and multilateral cooperation, not unilateral enforcement.199 In sum, no international tribunal authorizes a state to deploy its military to seize a foreign leader absent host-state consent or Security Council authorization.
The same is true for U.S. domestic courts. More specifically, cases such as United States v. Alvarez-Machain establish only that domestic courts may exercise jurisdiction over abducted defendants.200 They do not authorize the use of military force abroad.201 Scholars have repeatedly warned against conflating criminal jurisdiction with international legality.202 Treating criminal culpability as a justification for force collapses the distinction between law and violence.203 The Maduro seizure represents precisely this conflation. It inexplicably converts the existence of domestic criminal charges into a warrant for unilateral military action.
It is precisely this unilateral action, the seizure of a sitting head of state, that is categorically different from extradition, indictment, or covert abduction. It is an assertion of sovereign power through force. That assertion cannot be reconciled with the post-1945 legal order, which channels accountability through collective mechanisms precisely to prevent such acts.204
If criminal accusation alone suffices to justify military seizure, the prohibition on the use of force is effectively nullified. The Maduro seizure therefore threatens not only Venezuelan sovereignty, but the viability of international legal constraint itself.205
B. The Post-1945 Settlement and Its Core Bargain
The modern <mark>international</mark> legal <mark>order rests on</mark> a simple but ambitious bargain: states renounce unilateral force in exchange for collective security. <mark>The U</mark>nited <mark>N</mark>ations <mark>Charter</mark> embodies this commitment through Article 2(4)’s prohibition on the use of force and Article 51’s narrow selfdefense exception, supplemented by Security Council authorization for collective action.206 This framework was not designed to eliminate violence altogether. It was designed to channel violence through law, constraining when and how states may resort to force. <mark>The</mark> prohibition on unilateral force was deliberately categorical. <mark>Postwar</mark> architects understood that allowing states to invoke necessity, justice, or enforcement unilaterally would recreate the very instability the Charter sought to prevent. The <mark>lesson</mark> of the interwar period <mark>was n</mark>o<mark>t that force had been used for bad reasons</mark>, <mark>but</mark> that it had been used <mark>without</mark> institutional <mark>constraint</mark>.207
The January 2026 seizure of President Nicolás <mark>Maduro</mark> represents a direct challenge to this settlement. By using military force to incapacitate a foreign government while denying that war had occurred, the United States effectively asserted a new category of lawful unilateral force— one neither contemplated nor permitted by the Charter system.
XVI. The Prohibition on Force and the Temptation of Recharacterization
A. Article 2(4) and Its Deliberate Breadth
Article 2(4) prohibits not only war, but “the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state.”208 This language was intentionally expansive. It reflects an effort to foreclose semantic evasion—to ensure that states could not escape constraint by recharacterizing force as something other than war.209
The Maduro seizure falls squarely within this prohibition. The operation involved kinetic military force deployed within Venezuelan territory to remove its head of state. That the United States denied engaging in war does not alter the legal character of the act.
As such, the Maduro seizure is an example of law enforcement as a normative trojan horse. By framing the operation as law enforcement—grounded in criminal culpability rather than armed conflict—the Executive attempted to introduce a new justificatory pathway into jus ad bellum.
This move <mark>is normatively dangerous</mark>. Law enforcement logic is individualized, discretionary, and backward-looking. Jus ad bellum is collective, categorical, and forward-looking. Conflating the two allows states to bypass collective security mechanisms whenever they allege criminal wrongdoing.210 <mark>If accepted</mark>, this logic would permit any state to seize foreign leaders unilaterally, so long as domestic law supplied an indictment. <mark>The</mark> <mark>Charter</mark>’s prohibition on force <mark>would remain</mark> formally <mark>intact but functionally hollow</mark>.
B. Emergency Use of Force
“In 1837, US Secretary of State Daniel Webster articulated a definition of self-defence, which evolved into customary international law.”211 This definition arose from the Caroline case, in which British forces destroyed a U.S. vessel, the Caroline, while it attempted to deliver goods to Canadian insurgents.212 The British shot at the Caroline and set it on fire. Webster declared that Britain's actions did not constitute legal self-defense, which was only justified if the “necessity of [that] self-defense [is] instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation.”213
This was certainly not the situation with the Maduro seizure. The threat was not so instant and overwhelming the there was no time to deliberate nor take any other action. The U.S. had been monitoring Maduro’s every move for months before deciding one day to act.214 The executive undertook this police action because it wanted to, not because it needed to.215
The Maduro seizure therefore reveals a deeper transformation: the migration of executive violence from exceptional emergency into the architecture of ordinary governance.216 This shift mirrors patterns identified in comparative constitutional law, where democratic erosion occurs through legal continuity rather than rupture. Modern power expands not through coups, but through reinterpretation and normalization.217 If this executive claims the institutional capacity to act unilaterally, and this Maduro seizure is exercised without consequence, that capacity becomes embedded.218
XVII. Security Council Paralysis and Executive Opportunism
A. The Reality of Council Dysfunction
Defenders of unilateral force often point to Security Council paralysis as justification for bypassing collective mechanisms. It is no secret that geopolitical division—particularly among the five permanent members—has limited the Council’s effectiveness.219 But the Charter’s design anticipated this possibility. The prohibition on unilateral force was not contingent on Council functionality. It was a baseline rule, not a default to be abandoned when inconvenient. Allowing states to substitute unilateral action for collective authorization converts paralysis into permission. That move transforms a structural limitation into a license for executive opportunism.220
The true danger lies not in one-off violations, but in pattern formation. When powerful states repeatedly act unilaterally while <mark>invoking Council dysfunction</mark>, they <mark>normalize</mark> the idea that collective security is optional. Over time, the exception becomes the rule. The Maduro seizure exemplifies this transition. The United States did not claim that Security Council authorization was impossible; it treated authorization as unnecessary. That shift—from regretful <mark>bypass</mark> to confident irrelevance—signals a deeper erosion <mark>of</mark> the <mark>entire international legal system</mark> of checks and balances. 221
This is particularly troublesome because international law rarely collapses through open repudiation. It erodes through practice that is tolerated, justified, or imitated. By their very nature international laws and norms rely on the collective will of the people – the people of the nations, not one nation. The most damaging violations are those accompanied by legal rhetoric, because they reshape expectations for others rather than simply break the rules of their own nation. 222
The Maduro seizure was not defended as a raw exercise of power. It was defended as lawful— indeed, as restrained.223 That defense matters. When powerful states violate norms while denying that they have even done so, they invite others to do the same.224 This dynamic helps explain why norm erosion often accelerates after initial defection. Once legality becomes contestable rather than categorical, restraint becomes discretionary. Or even unnecessary.
As noted, one of the central insights of international relations theory is that justifications travel more easily than facts. States do not need identical circumstances to imitate behavior; they need usable rationales.225 And the <mark>U.S</mark>. justification, this law-enforcement <mark>rationale</mark> advanced in the Maduro seizure <mark>is exceptionally portable</mark>. It requires no declaration of war, no collective authorization, and no acknowledgment of armed conflict. It <mark>allows</mark> executives to claim legality while <mark>avoiding</mark> the <mark>political cost</mark>s traditionally associated with aggression.
<mark>For authoritarian</mark> or hybrid regime<mark>s</mark>, <mark>this</mark> rationale <mark>is invaluable</mark>. It supplies a vocabulary through which coercive intervention can be reframed as accountability. The result is not merely more violations, but a redefinition of what counts as violation.226
B. Authoritarian Mimicry and the Portability of Justifications 2
The cumulative effect of these dynamics is a shift from collective security to discretionary policing. Under a policing model, powerful states assume responsibility for enforcing norms directly, without institutional mediation. This model may appear attractive in cases involving abusive regimes or alleged criminality. <mark>But</mark> it is fundamentally incompatible with sovereign equality. Policing presupposes hierarchy, whereas collective security presupposes equality.227 The Charter chose the latter precisely because the former reproduces imperial dynamics under the guise of order.228 The Maduro seizure is a seismic shift, <mark>where the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates is not a participant in a legal order, but rather is its enforcer. And whatever the U.S. claims it’s intensions are, does not really matter. As with domestic democratic erosion, international legal decay cannot be arrested by appeals to benign intent. The Charter does not prohibit force because states are always malign and out for destruction. It prohibits force because even well-intentioned unilateralism produces instability.229
The United States occupies a unique position in the international system. Its actions disproportionately shape legal expectations. When <mark>it adheres to constraint, it reinforces norms</mark>; when it defects, it accelerates erosion.230 This asymmetry imposes responsibility, not privilege. The Maduro seizure therefore carries global significance beyond Venezuela or even Latin America. It signals whether the United States continues to view itself as bound by the legal order it helped create—or as entitled to revise that order unilaterally.
Norm erosion is not irreversible, but repair is costly. Once unilateralism becomes normalized, restoring collective constraint requires affirmative repudiation, not mere abstention. States must clarify that violations were unlawful, recommit to authorization mechanisms, and accept limits even when inconvenient. Absent such steps, the logic of discretionary force will harden. The fear is that the Maduro seizure will become precedent not because it was right, but because it was uncontested.
The post-1945 international legal order was designed to eliminate unilateral force not by moral appeal, but by institutional design. The January 2026 seizure of Nicolás Maduro represents a decisive breach of that design—not only as a violation, but as a redefinition of legality itself. By substituting law-enforcement rationales for collective authorization, the United States has weakened the prohibition on force at its most vulnerable point.
If this move is accepted, the Charter system will persist in form while collapsing in function. Collective security will yield to discretionary policing, and law will retreat before power dressed in legal language. Preventing that outcome requires recognizing the Maduro seizure for what it is: not an anomaly, but a stress test the international legal order may not survive without repair.231
XVIII. From Diagnosis to Repair
The January 2026 seizure of President Nicolás Maduro crystallizes a constitutional failure decades in the making. The episode did not occur because the law was unclear; it occurred because legal constraints were rendered optional through institutional abdication. Congress declined to exercise its war powers, courts declined to adjudicate their boundaries, and the Executive exploited the resulting vacuum. The authoritarian ratchet turned not through a single usurpation, but through accumulated nondecisions.232
Repair therefore cannot be rhetorical. Condemnation without constraint can actually accelerate normalization. What is required is structural recalibration—mechanisms that reintroduce friction where executive power has learned to operate without resistance.233
First, <mark>Congress must reassert control</mark> over the initiation of force <mark>through</mark> bright-line <mark>authorization</mark> requirements. The persistent executive practice of recharacterizing hostile military action as “law enforcement” or “non-hostilities” has hollowed the war power of its core.234 Statutory reform <mark>should</mark> therefore <mark>define “use of force”</mark> to include any kinetic military operation within another state’s territory absent consent.235
Second, Congress must impose real consequences for unauthorized force. Automatic funding cutoffs, mandatory withdrawal provisions, and expedited authorization votes would realign incentives without dictating strategy. These mechanisms are not novel. They operationalize the Constitution’s original allocation of authority.236
Judicial <mark>engagement need not be sweeping</mark> to be effective. <mark>Courts</mark> can <mark>arrest normalization</mark> by policing threshold <mark>questions</mark>—particularly whether a given action constitutes a “use <mark>of</mark> force” triggering constitutional and statutory <mark>constraint</mark>s. Narrow holdings <mark>reject</mark>ing <mark>semantic evasion</mark> would restore separation-of-powers discipline without entangling courts in operational judgments.237

A. Revised Test
This Article <mark>propose</mark>s that <mark>the President</mark> may <mark>use force</mark> without direct congressional approval if she seeks <mark>to protect a</mark>n important <mark>national interest recognized</mark> by Congress that will not rise to the nature, scope, or duration of the war. A President determines what constitutes a war based on the circumstances <mark>and</mark> any parameters established by Congress. Although the revised test retains the two prongs of the OLC’s test, it includes additional limiting principles.
First, “important national interests” must be <mark>defined by Congress</mark>, not the President. Instead of national interests deriving from those reasonably recognized by the President, there must be a statutory authority authorizing force to defend the interests. This prong is highly formalistic, wherein the President must find a statute passed <mark>through</mark> bicameralism and presentment that identifies the national interest, justifying their decision to use military force.
Congress can recognize an important national interest either <mark>explicit</mark>ly <mark>or implicit</mark>ly. Explicit recognition would include a clear statement of the national interest and approval for using military force to protect it.339 An excellent example of explicit recognition is the Taiwan Relations Act.340 This Act recognizes Taiwan’s strategic value and provides an ambiguous security guarantee, wherein intervention in Taiwan is left to the President’s discretion.341 Explicit recognition thus requires Congress to state that the subject is an important national interest and that defending this interest justifies military intervention.
Implicit recognition occurs when Congress passes a law to achieve a specific foreign policy goal.342 By passing the law, Congress recognized that the goal was an important national interest. Still, implicit <mark>authorization</mark> must come from a bill in the context of foreign policy or the military. It cannot be from a domestic law irrelevant to national security or foreign policy. Additionally, military intervention must be necessary to achieve that goal recognized by the law.
The American intervention in Somalia illustrates an implied recognition of a national interest. The OLC justified American military intervention in Somalia as implicitly authorized by the Horn of Africa Recovery and Food Security Act passed by Congress.343 This act authorized emergency aid.344 Still, military intervention was the only way to distribute this aid.345 Congress’s law implicitly recognized getting humanitarian aid to Somalia as an important national interest. <mark>Thus</mark>, <mark>Congress must pass</mark> a <mark>law</mark> <mark>recognizing</mark> an important national <mark>interest and</mark> either <mark>explicitly approve</mark> military <mark>force or consider it necessary to protect</mark> that recognized <mark>interest</mark>.
Second, the President can use military force without explicit congressional approval only when the intervention constitutes a war under Congress’s “declare war” power. This test accepts that the original conception of war encompassing all conflict initiation is untenable and accepts the delineation between limited conflicts and expansive wars. The OLC’s proposed “nature, scope, and duration” parameters can guide the President and their advisers to analyze what constitutes a war. Still, Congress retains the power to override the President’s standard for what constitutes a war.
Therefore, defining what constitutes a war will be treated as a concurrent power between the Executive and the Legislature. Although concurrent powers leave deference to the President, it does not foreclose Congress from defining what constitutes a war. Congress is incentivized to assert its authority to interpret the meaning of war, which supersedes presidential discretion. Still, congressional silence will not prevent the President from intervening. The President’s discretion in interpreting what constitutes a war is proportional to the amount of clarification Congress gives.
To illustrate how this test would operate, consider President Biden’s recent air campaign against the Houthi militant group in Yemen. The Houthis, a Yemeni militant group, continue to attack commercial shipping in the Gulf of Aden.346 These attacks threatened American ships and global trade passing through the Suez Canal.347 In response, President Biden ordered strikes against Houthi positions.348 In a letter to Congress, Biden claimed that the strikes were “consistent with my responsibility to protect United States citizens both at home and abroad and in furtherance of United States national security and foreign policy interests, pursuant to my constitutional authority as Commander in Chief and Chief Executive and to conduct United States foreign relations.”349
Both prongs must be met for the President to be justified in using military force. If there is a failure to justify intervention through a national interest recognized by Congress or if the conflict is assessed to reach the level of war, then the President cannot use force without congressional approval.
Under this test, President Biden’s claim to inherent powers would be insufficient. Instead, Congress would need to preemptively pass legislation that identifies global shipping routes as a vital interest that the President may use appropriate force to defend. Recognizing an important national interest does not need to be highly specific to shipping in the Gulf of Aden. Congress can provide a broader authorization. The key is that Congress must recognize global shipping as an important national interest. Alternatively, the President could look through legislation that identifies global shipping as a vital interest and can only be enforced by using military force against Houthi positions.
Then, President Biden would need to determine whether the strikes would likely reach a constitutional war’s nature, scope, and duration. This would be based on President Biden’s determination unless Congress passed legislation establishing guidelines for when a conflict reaches the scope of war. Once both prongs are met, then the strikes would be permissible.
Thus, the test shifts the determination of an important national interest away from the President’s discretion. Still, by allowing for implicit authorization, the President retains the flexibility to make the strikes. The test adds at least one further step that guides the executives’ decisions about what is in the national interest.
B. Why the Revised Test
The revised test maintains the OLC’s general proposal but includes limiting principles for each prong. Still, it is imperative to recognize why these alterations effectively balance the President’s pragmatic needs and the Constitution’s prophylactic protections. Ultimately, the revised test achieves this balance because it creates a constitutionally permissible framework, preserves Congress’s essential functions of determining long-term policy goals, grants the President flexibility to respond to threats to important national interests, and creates a judicially enforceable standard.
1. Constitutionally Permissible
First, the proposed OLC test revisions avoid reliance on problematic constitutional arguments. The OLC relied on inherent presidential powers and historical gloss to justify the President’s power to intervene without congressional approval.350 Both these justifications are contrary to the Constitution’s spirit. Inherent presidential powers are incompatible with the principle of limited and enumerated powers that undergird the Constitution. Historical gloss undermines the concept of a written Constitution. Just because the branches have acted unconstitutionally does not allow them to assume power through adverse possession. The danger of accepting these arguments is that they justify the President’s further encroachment on the legislature since they can act without statutory authority in those cases. If the President can justify acting on their own in war, what is to stop this extension into other domestic affairs, such as legislating without congressional approval? The proposed test forgoes the need to rely on unconstitutional arguments and thus avoids establishing dangerous precedents that could further expand the imperial presidency.
If Congress must define national interests, then <mark>military intervention</mark> by the President <mark>becomes an act of executing</mark> the <mark>law rather than</mark> acting on inherent <mark>power</mark>s. Requiring the President to only act on national interests recognized by Congress ensures that the President only executes a conflict <mark>via the Commander in Chief</mark>, Take Care, and Executive Power <mark>clauses</mark>. The President does not need to rely on questionable arguments of residual powers in foreign affairs, but is granted that discretion by Congress.351 Thus, the OLC only needs to rely on the textually defined role of the President and not stretch the understanding of presidential powers to include inherent powers that would expand the authority of the President and undermine the essential checks and balances of our federal government.
The Court’s holding in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. et al. v. Sawyer352supports the revised test. In this case, from the Korean War, the Court rejected Truman’s assertion of inherent powers to break a steel mill strike.353 The Court held that the President lacked the power from Congress or inherent powers to act domestically.354 Justice Jackson’s concurrence in establishing three zones of presidential power became the framework that the Court utilized when analyzing actions by the President.355 Zone 1 is when the President acts on Congress’s express or implied authorization.356 Zone 2 is when the President acts on independent powers from the Constitution, where concurrent authority exists with Congress, and the President acts independently.357 Zone 3 is when the President is at odds with Congress and can only act when given explicit power in the Constitution.358
Applying the Jacksonian framework, the revised test moves the debate of presidential war powers from a Jacksonian zone two or three to a zone one, where the President and Congress agree. Zone one powers do not need inherent powers; thus, this test does not need to rely on them. Therefore, the revised test brings presidential war powers into line with the Jacksonian framework and ensures that they are within the bounds of the powers granted to the President.
Congressionalist scholars could criticize this revision as impermissibly delegating legislative authority to the Executive, as Congress does to administrative agencies.359 In doing so, this test would greatly expand presidential power. Although delegation is not ideal, the current war powers structure grants the President broader discretion than if Congress proactively defined it. By requiring that the statute be explicit or implicit with context and necessity limitations, it ensures that there is always an intelligible principle. Moreover, the revision ensures that Congress at least considered the potential for conflict and could deliberate on the importance of the interests. The revised test is not the ideal construction of war powers, but it better comports with presidential power than the current model.
Furthermore, the first prong requiring Congress to define a national interest properly is compatible with the legislature’s role in the Constitution’s War Powers Scheme. The text of the Constitution gives Congress the power to “declare war.”360 Importantly, the Constitution does not define how Congress “declare[s] war.” Nothing prevents Congress from implicitly or preemptively authorizing the President to engage in future conflicts. The early Supreme Court agreed with this conclusion in Bas v. Tingy, where the Court stated that Congress could pass legislation that implicitly authorizes “war.”361 Thus, it is within Congress’s power to preemptively authorize through explicit or implicit statutes what national interests permit the President to use force to defend.
Next, the second prong avoids using historical gloss to justify a narrow interpretation of the meaning of war. Since the statutory authorization grants the President the ability to utilize force, there is no need to seek a narrow definition of war justified by historical practice. Instead, the second prong is more a recognition that Congress cannot delegate in advance authorization for a large-scale conflict. This is because the “declare war” power needs to be retained to some extent, and the more significant the conflict, the more likely Congress needs to give direct and close-in-time authorization.362 Defining what constitutes war is thus a Jacksonian Zone 2 concurrent power, wherein the President can determine what constitutes a war unless Congress passes a statute on the issue.
Additionally, the second prong limits the extent to which Congress can delegate military affairs. A fair concern with these revisions is that Congress is withdrawing from the declarations of large-scale wars through delegation. However, this is where the second prong creates a limitation. Congress cannot cede from its explicitly granted powers since they are not privileges of the legislature but part of a broader scheme to defend against tyranny. Thus, by including the nature, scope, and duration test, Congress cannot delegate large-scale wars but can define the Constitution’s war powers through the state. Absent this definition by Congress, the President can be guided by their interpretation, which the OLC provides. Effectively, it prevents Congress from delegating largescale conflicts without explicit authorization.
Treating the definition of war as a concurrent power is compatible with the Supreme Court’s understanding of presidential and congressional war powers. In the Quasi-War case, Little v. Barreme, the Court found that the specificity of Congress’s statutes limited the President’s discretion in enforcing an authorization to seize vessels.363 Congress’s explicit statutory direction on what ships the Navy could seize superseded the President’s interpretation.364 Still, the Court recognized that the less direction Congress granted, the greater discretion the President had.365
In this case, evaluating what constitutes a war through a “nature, scope, and duration” analysis can be considered reasonable. It accounts for the fact that the more intensive the fighting is, the larger the conflict covers, the longer the conflict lasts, and the greater the likelihood it is a war that requires congressional authorization. Still, <mark>if Congress disagrees with</mark> the <mark>President’s parameters</mark>, <mark>it can provide</mark> greater <mark>specificity by</mark> passing <mark>new</mark> laws and <mark>regulation</mark>s.
These arguments illustrate that although the revised test takes a pragmatic and living constitutionalist approach, it remains bound by the Constitution’s text. In doing so, it avoids assertions of inherent presidential powers or historical gloss. Thus, the proposed revision to the OLC’s test solves the challenges to its suspect constitutionality.
2. Preserves the Role of Congress
Second, the proposed revisions to the OLC’s test preserve Congress’s role in guiding long-term strategic decisions. While many scholars agree that Congress should have some role in initiating conflict, the proposals revolve around how Congress writes and passes military authorizations or increases Congress’s supervisory powers.366 The first fails because it is impractical, as illustrated by the previous sections.367 The second would be similar to the War Powers Resolution, which does not preserve Congress’s role as the first mover in a conflict. The proposed test provides a more practical approach by giving Congress the exclusive power to define national interests. In taking a formalist approach to defining national interests, Congress retains a role in defining the longterm goals of American foreign policy and military intervention.
Likewise, the revised test guides the President on what constitutes recognition of a national interest by Congress. One option is for the statute to clearly state the national interest and explicitly permit military force to defend it. Alternatively, Congress can give implicit authorization if the law defines a goal or policy that requires military intervention. Notably, because presidents rely on authority in statutes rooted in national security or foreign policy, the test avoids concerns that presidents will use irrelevant statutes to justify their military actions. Additionally, domestic laws unrelated to foreign policy cannot meet the requirement for defining a national interest.
For an example of the national interest model, consider the case underlying the Curtiss-Wright decision. Although the Court’s dicta argues for inherent presidential powers in foreign affairs, the facts of the case illustrate Congress defining a national interest that the President can achieve.368 The case arose from a law allowing the President to control the flow of arms into the Chaco region to achieve peace.369 Congress gave the President statutory authority and direction to make decisions to achieve the stated goal of peace in the region.370 The law did not mandate specific sanctions or military intervention, but defined the policy goal and granted discretion to the President in achieving this goal.371
Critics of the revised test could argue that permitting implicit authorization allows the President to use statutes that do not intend to grant the President initiatory war powers to justify their actions.372 This concern about presidential manipulation is valid, as many statutes likely provide some language that could be interpreted as implicit recognition of a national interest. Moreover, the AUMF following 9/11 has been repeatedly manipulated by the President to permit interventions in conflicts increasingly tangential to the War on Terror.373
These criticisms are valid. While the test does not provide a silver bullet to prevent a President from acting in bad faith, it still provides more legal definition to identify when that President is exceeding his authority. The law <mark>can constrain</mark> the <mark>President by creating norms</mark> internalized in the executive branch and assist Congress and the populace in <mark>identify</mark>ing <mark>when they exceed</mark> their <mark>authority</mark>.374
Still, the additional context <mark>and</mark> necessity requirements <mark>alleviate</mark> some of these <mark>circumvention</mark> concerns. To avoid misuse, presidents could not use domestic laws or irrelevant statutes to justify military intervention because the test requires the law to be in the context of national security or foreign policy. Moreover, the requirement that military intervention be essential to achieve the law’s goals prevents Congress from considering, or should have considered, that passing the law might necessitate military force. Additionally, <mark>it can permit</mark> the President to establish <mark>doctrines of deterrence regarding nucl</mark>ear weapons and autonomous systems to avoid later challenges to their <mark>deployment</mark> by the public. Although not infallible, these <mark>limit</mark>ing principle<mark>s prevent blatant</mark> statutory <mark>manipulation</mark> by the President.
By retaining Congress’s role as the initiator of conflict, the public can debate the interests that justify using force abroad.375 Congress’s <mark>openness</mark> in its decision-making <mark>ensures</mark> that the <mark>public has</mark> a greater opportunity to <mark>weigh in</mark> on the conflict. Even if the issue does not maintain significant political salience, the ability for public discourse as to what constitutes important national interests warrants the force to defend.
As for the second prong, treating it as a concurrent power offers Congress the option but not the obligation to define what constitutes a war. Doing so incentivizes Congress to assert its authority, if it wishes, to limit the President’s power. Similarly, Justice Jackson’s concurrence utilized this institutionalist approach in its Category Two powers.376 Where Congress has not spoken, the President has greater discretion. Still, if <mark>Congress</mark> decides to define a Constitutional war, they can. Thus, the nature, scope, and duration can be legitimate guiding principles for the President without congressional action.
3. Pragmatic <mark>Respect</mark> for the <mark>Need for</mark> Presidential <mark>Flexibility</mark>
Third, the proposed test provides the President with flexibility in using force. Although the President cannot define a national interest unilaterally, she can still pursue limited military incursions. <mark>The President can</mark> still <mark>make rapid decisions to defend</mark> vital <mark>interests</mark>, only losing their overreach into long-term strategic markers.377 Moreover, if the President recognizes risks <mark>or</mark> needs, they can <mark>persuade Congress to</mark> pass new legislation that <mark>preemptively recognize</mark>s a national interest that might require using military force.
Additionally, the second prong defers to presidential expertise in determining what interventions will rise to the level of war. Even if Congress were to assert its power and set forth a standard defining what constitutes a war, the President would still have latitude in determining which military interventions would meet that standard. Thus, the President retains significant power to assess situations and determine the most appropriate response within the confines of the statutes passed by Congress.
Moreover, setting <mark>clear standards</mark> to guide the President’s decisionmaking can <mark>ensure</mark> greater <mark>legitimacy and support</mark> when conflict is pursued. Presidents can avoid arguments that their intervention is not constitutional and ensure that Congress does not oppose the conflict for lack of voice or authorization.378 The specific parameters for explicit or implicit recognition further reduce ambiguity. In doing so, the President can focus more on implementing foreign policy and less on the legitimacy of that power.379
4. Judicially Enforceable
Lastly, the revised test creates a judicially enforceable standard for determining if an action is within the national interest. The Court defers to the President when national security and foreign affairs issues arise, as it concerns their lack of expertise.380 On the one hand, this instinct is admirable. Courts do not wish to determine what interests and issues warrant military force to defend when they lack the expertise and advisors possessed by the President. Conversely, it provides the President far too much discretion and leaves Congress with less legal and political support in a dispute.
The proposed test ensures that the Court can resolve disputes between the Executive and Legislative branches while not pontificating on the substantive foreign policy. The formalistic rule that Congress must define a national interest does not require the court to assess the wisdom of the choice.381 Congress either explicitly recognizes the national interest or implicitly recognizes the national interest by passing a law establishing a foreign policy goal. Moreover, the <mark>specific parameters</mark> surrounding explicit or implicit recognition by Congress <mark>reduce </mark>concerns of <mark>ambiguity that</mark> might concern a court. Instead, the rule <mark>creates</mark> a standard that the Court can use to ensure the proper branch decides while treating the substance as plenary.382
Critics will argue that courts will not be empowered because suits regarding war powers are not justiciable.383 Although the justiciability question is too large to discuss thoroughly in this Article, it is essential to provide some discussion. War powers are typically not justiciable because the Court holds them as political questions, and the most likely plaintiffs, members of Congress, do not have standing on an individual basis.384 The political question doctrine arises because there are concerns about establishing a judicially manageable standard that does not interfere with textually committed powers granted to the political branches.385 Establishing a clear standard for when the President can initiate an intervention can address the court’s justifications for using the political question doctrine.386 Since the test provides a <mark>judicial</mark>ly manageable standard, the court might be more willing to address these <mark>issues</mark>.
The more difficult issue is the standing of congresspersons. The current standing doctrine prevents members of Congress from suing to block presidential use of force.387 The Court rejects standing primarily over logical concerns about individual members representing the will of Congress and the concern of interfering with substantive foreign policy decisions.388 Ideally, the court would be less concerned about substantive interference because of the formalistic standard created by the revised test. Still, courts will have to alter the standing doctrine to permit members of Congress to sue if they are to play a role in enforcement.

C. Appropriations Clause Suits and the Separation of Powers
As we have now seen, congressional <mark>Appropriations</mark> Clause <mark>suits have a good chance of making it past</mark> the procedural <mark>hurdles that</mark> have <mark>stymied</mark> prior lawsuits <mark>attempt</mark>ing <mark>to correct</mark> presidential <mark>overreach in the national security sphere</mark>. And, if preceded by strategic legislating, such suits have an even better chance of succeeding on the merits. <mark>This</mark> outcome would be entirely consistent with—and, indeed, could help <mark>streamline</mark>—<mark>the</mark> Supreme Court’s <mark>framework for assessing separation-of-powers challenges</mark>. As discussed earlier, if Congress were to clearly and narrowly appropriate funds for national security purposes, or to expressly prohibit an expenditure, then a presidential action in violation of those restrictions would fall into Youngstown’s category three, where executive power is at its “lowest ebb.”309 As Justice Jackson recognized in Youngstown, appropriation of funds—even regarding national security—is a power the Constitution commits wholly to Congress: “Congress alone controls the raising of revenues and their appropriation and may determine in what manner and by what means they shall be spent for military and naval procurement.”310 A congressional <mark>appropriations restriction on</mark> specific <mark>national security spending is</mark> the <mark>paradigmatic exercise of</mark> congressional <mark>authority</mark> that Justice Jackson recognized in Youngstown. If Congress could establish that it exercised this power, presidential action to the contrary would violate the separation of powers, as squarely dictated by Justice Jackson’s canonical Youngstown concurrence.
It is true that Appropriations Clause lawsuits combine separation of powers and national security, two areas of traditional judicial abdication. But, ironically, <mark>Appropriations</mark> Clause lawsuits <mark>are likelier to succeed than</mark> many <mark>other separation-of-powers or national security cases</mark>. Unlike many other provisions of the Constitution, <mark>the Court</mark> has <mark>recognized that the Clause involves a “straightforward and explicit command.”</mark>311 This gives the judiciary an easily administrable test for the familiar judicial exercise of constitutional interpretation.312
Just as importantly, the Court has noted that the Appropriations Clause was designed “as a restriction upon the disbursing authority of the Executive department”;313 its very purpose is “to assure that public funds will be spent according to the letter of the difficult judgments reached by Congress as to the common good.”314 In so doing, the Clause prevents the Executive from replacing Congress’s judgment with its own. The D.C. Circuit has also recognized that the Clause is “particularly important as a restraint on Executive Branch officers,” and has called it “a bulwark of the Constitution’s separation of powers among the three branches.”315 It would be ironic for the courts to invoke the separation of powers as a reason to avoid adjudicating straightforward disputes under a constitutional provision so precisely designed to empower one branch and rein in another.
This last point <mark>hints at</mark> the <mark>broader</mark> theoretical <mark>issues</mark> that Appropriations Clause litigation implicates. The courts have developed each of the procedural roadblocks discussed above in the national security context because they held a particular view of the separation of powers and of the judiciary’s role. The view the courts developed was an understandable one. As seen throughout this Part, many of the cases that triggered restrictive procedural rules involved individual draftees or members of Congress trying to get courts to declare the existence or the conduct of a military action unconstitutional—requests almost uniquely designed to provoke judicial recoil. To prevent abuse of the judicial forum, the courts adopted a more restrictive attitude toward their own role vis-à-vis the other branches. This attitude was unusual as a historical matter316 and reached beyond what was necessary to rein in frivolous cases.
More recently, however, the courts have returned to a more robust vision of the judicial role in both separation-of-powers317 and national security318 disputes. <mark>Bringing suits</mark> under the Appropriations Clause <mark>could</mark> both <mark>reinforce and shape</mark> this <mark>trend</mark>. In discussing the Executive’s decision to decline to defend statutes, for instance, the Court in Windsor <mark>sounded a larger theme about</mark> the importance of <mark>adjudication in interbranch conflicts</mark>. “[W]hen Congress has passed a statute and a President has signed it,” the Court said, “it poses grave challenges to the separation of powers for the Executive at a particular moment to be able to nullify Congress’[s] enactment solely on its own initiative and without any determination from the Court.”319 The President’s failure to follow congressional appropriations is exactly the sort of unilateral nullification about which the Windsor Court cautioned. Judicial engagement with Appropriations Clause lawsuits is thus a natural outgrowth of the Court’s developing view of the separation of powers. But because they involve relatively narrow disputes over whether certain expenditures were authorized, such suits can actually help courts <mark>minimize</mark> the <mark>interbranch friction that</mark> might otherwise <mark>grow without intervention</mark>.
To see why this is so, consider Justice Scalia’s dissent in Windsor. The dissent advocated for a restrictive view of congressional standing, based on the Vietnamera conception of the courts’ role. Rather than look to the courts, Justice Scalia said, Congress should confront the President politically—through “the elimination of funding,” among other methods.320 The problem with this logic, however, is that it provides no answer to the inevitable follow-up question: what happens if the President ignores Congress’s funding command? To the extent the restrictive view of judicial power provides an answer to this question, that answer is to tell Congress to take even more extreme measures: to deny all funding to the Executive, refuse to confirm presidential appointments,321 or even impeach the President.
To be fair, Justice Scalia seemed to realize the herculean nature of this task.322 But a majority on the current Court, as well as in the lower courts, appears to recognize that the judiciary need not totally abandon the field—even in national security cases.323 After all, Congress cannot use its appropriations power to confront the President, as Justice Scalia suggested, if the President thinks she can simply transfer funds to evade Congress’s prescriptions. VI. The Case Against The Clause: Responding To The Main Critiques Of This Congressional Strategy
Given the historical interest that members of Congress have shown in pursuing national security lawsuits, and the model presented in Burwell, it is possible that courts will face more Appropriations Clause national security litigation in the future. Congress’s use of this tool can be criticized on grounds of its wisdom, effectiveness, and appeal to partisanship. But ultimately these <mark>challenges fail to grasp</mark> the extent of the problem posed by the modern imbalance in the separation of powers, and <mark>the targeted nature of</mark> the solution that <mark>Appropriations</mark> Clause litigation provides. Overall, the use of such lawsuits by Congress could serve as an effective aid in recalibrating the imbalance of power and asserting its constitutional role in war making and national security.
First, the specter of the robed, faceless, unelected judge ordering the President to withdraw troops from combat evokes, for many, a deep-seated discomfort. The traditional critique of judicial involvement in the war powers context resembles the arguments for applying the political question doctrine: judges lack the competence359 to analyze the relevant facts360 and decide what are essentially policy questions,361 particularly where national security is at stake.362
These arguments against judicial involvement in foreign affairs and national security have been heavily criticized,363 and are particularly inapplicable in the context of Appropriations Clause litigation. Judges have historically been involved in questions of national security,364 the separation of powers,365 and the Appropriations Clause.366 The relevant factual questions are well within judicial competence; they require courts to answer whether Congress appropriated funds, and whether the President spent funds in violation of those restrictions. Deciding an appropriations challenge would not be tantamount to making policy: Congress made its policy determination by choosing to restrict funding, but the courts are needed to prevent unconstitutional actions in contravention of that policy.
<mark>Arguments</mark> against judicial involvement in national security appropriations disputes <mark>rely heavily on the status quo</mark>, equating judicial abstention with neutrality and judicial involvement with activism and bias. But neutrality is not necessarily neutral. Judicial abdication in these questions heavily favors the Executive.367 To insist that courts stay out of these disputes is to argue that Presidents should always have the last say, unless Congress pursues impeachment. But this position is entirely inconsistent with Congress’s constitutional authority over appropriations and war making, as envisioned by the Framers. The ability to keep leashed the dog of war was intended to be one of Congress’s most effective checks on unbridled executive war making. To decline to adjudicate these disputes would be tantamount to cutting the leash.
Second, critics of national security appropriations litigation may contend that if a single house of Congress had standing to sue the President for any alleged appropriations misstep, these suits would be too easy to institute, resulting in “congressional end-runs around the legislative process and threaten[ing] to involve the courts in virtually every political dispute.”368 Because it is easier to get a majority of one house to vote to bring a lawsuit than to get a veto-proof two-thirds majority in each house to pass or repeal a law over a presidential veto, these suits might function as a bad faith means of congressional opposition.
Ultimately, however, Appropriations Clause <mark>suits are unlikely to be</mark> a <mark>frequent</mark> recourse. First, floods of litigation have not accompanied at least some past expansions of legislative standing, despite similarly calamitous predictions.369 Second, Appropriations Clause lawsuits are not “end-runs around the legislative process” in the traditional sense because they already involve a completed legislative process—the appropriations bill at issue has been passed, and congressional plaintiffs can only seek judicial redress of its unconstitutional violation. Third, appropriations challenges are not “too easy” to bring. Individual members would not be able to seek redress of the institutional injury without authorization from at least a majority of one house of Congress. Either Congress would have to pass ex ante framework legislation authorizing individual members to bring appropriations challenges, or individual members would need to seek authorization via resolution for each lawsuit. Similarly, congressional plaintiffs would need either to point to an explicit restriction that was violated or to have previously restructured national security appropriations in order to argue in the future that spending for a certain activity did not fit within narrow appropriations categories. The legislative activity that this would require would likely weed out frivolous claims.370
But <mark>even</mark> if appropriations litigation only occurs in the most <mark>extreme circumstances</mark>—when a President engages in national security decisions so objectionable that her own party is willing to oppose it—that <mark>is enough of an application for</mark> these <mark>lawsuits to be an effective</mark> and useful <mark>tool</mark>. Even in these limited circumstances, Appropriations Clause litigation would vindicate the constitutional prerogatives of Congress as an institution. And correcting the institutional imbalance of power that has developed between the political branches, contrary to constitutional design, is precisely the goal that the Appropriations Clause can help to serve.
A final critique of Congress’s use of appropriations litigation is that it will limit presidential discretion in the conduct of national security.371 If Congress only seeks to bring appropriations litigation in response to violations of explicit restrictions, presidential flexibility in national security spending would continue unaffected. However, if Congress recognizes the usefulness of such litigation, it could potentially remove presidential spending discretion and narrowly appropriate in order to bring appropriations litigation for illegally transferring funds between the narrow appropriations categories. In this scenario, Congress would have limited presidential discretion, and—<mark>critics</mark> would <mark>argue</mark>—<mark>removed</mark> the President’s <mark>ability to respond quickly</mark> and flexibly to a national security crisis.
However, this argument <mark>ignores the history of</mark> appropriations and presidential <mark>emergency action</mark>. In the past, when the <mark>President</mark> was faced with an emergency, she was <mark>expected to convene</mark> Congress <mark>immediately to appropriate funds</mark>, <mark>or</mark> to <mark>take on the risk of spending unappropriated funds and asking</mark> Congress to sanction the act <mark>as soon as possible</mark>.372 The President would still have that option if Congress returned to a structure of narrow, specific national security appropriations. In the case of a true emergency, the President can respond; but she assumes the risk that Congress will not affirmatively sanction the expenditure after the fact.373 This system <mark>properly places the burden on the President</mark>, <mark>because the Constitution intends</mark> that the President <mark>should try to avoid</mark> those <mark>risks by</mark> seeking political <mark>approval</mark> and appropriations <mark>before acting</mark>.
To the extent some effects of appropriations litigation may be undesirable, it is simply the price we must pay “for our system of checks and balances.”374 Although the price sometimes seems “exorbitant to many,” on balance it is desirable to fortify legislative powers against executive encroachment, because though a “kindly President” may overstep the separation of powers today, there is no telling how “another President might use the same power” tomorrow.375

There is an intimate and neglected relationship between existental risk and democracy.
Democracy must be central to efforts to prevent and mitigate catastrophic risks. It is also an antidote to many of the problems manifest in the TUA. Do those who study the future of humanity have good grounds to ignore the visions, desires, and values of the very people whose future they are trying to protect? Choosing which risks to take must be a democratic endeavour.
We understand democracy here in accordance with Landemore as the rule of the cognitively diverse many who are entitled to equal decision-making power and partake in a democratic procedure that includes both a deliberative element and one of preference aggregation (such as majority voting)sis. Decision-making procedures are not either democratic or non-democratic, but instead lie on a spectrum. They can be more or less democratic, inclusive, and diverse.
The democratic constraint of extreme measures may simply be a form of collective self-interest. <mark>Voters are unlikely to tolerate</mark> global catastrophic risks (<mark>GCRs</mark>), which incur the death of a sizeable portion of the electorate, if they know they themselves could be affected. Scholars of existential risk who take an interest in the future of Homo sapiens are choosing to consider the species in its entirety. If certain views are excluded, the arguments for doing so must be compelling.
