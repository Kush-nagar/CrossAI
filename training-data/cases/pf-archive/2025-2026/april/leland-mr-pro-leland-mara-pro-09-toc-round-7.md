Inspired by the pervasive finding that procedural fairness is particularly important in contexts with low
distributive fairness (Brockner, 1996; Brockner & Wiesenfeld, 2005), this literature has focused on ethniccultural minority groups which can be considered, outcome-wise, disadvantaged groups in their
respective societies. Taken together, this body of work has yielded mounting evidence that, for
disadvantaged ethnic-cultural minority groups, procedural fairness perceptions vis-à-vis the resolution of
ethnic-cultural issues by societal actors are associated with a plethora of positive outcomes for the
involved minority group members. Moreover, as a research collective of social justice and equity scholars
(see our positionality statement in Box 1), we also consider these outcomes to be beneficial for society at
large. In the current contribution, we advance a comprehensive theoretical model that offers a deeper
understanding of the processes underlying such ethnic-cultural procedural fairness perceptions.
The current article is divided into eight sections – see Table 1 for an overview of the individual research
lines. We start by reviewing classic and <<Box 1 Omitted>> recent empirical evidence of the effects of
procedural fairness enactment with respect to multicultural issues (Section 1). To theoretically ground
these findings, we advance a collective model of procedural fairness (CPF; Valcke et al., 2020).
Integrating insights from Self-Categorisation theory (Turner et al., 1987, 1994), the relational model of
procedural fairness (Tyler & Lind, 1992), and deontic theory (Folger, 1998, 2001), this model presents a
fine-grained theoretical analysis of the psychological processes that give rise to disadvantaged minority
group members’ responses to ethnic-cultural procedural fairness (Section 2). Moreover, the CPF model
also advances our understanding of third-party minority group members’ responses to procedurally
(un)fair treatment of citizens belonging to another minority group (discussed in Section 3), and majority
group members’ reactions to procedurally (un)fair treatment of minority groups (Section 4), as it also
disentangles the psychological mechanisms that give rise to procedural fairness effects observed among
the latter two groups. The present review further highlights two noteworthy caveats of procedural
fairness enactment with respect to ethnic-cultural issues (Section 5). We conclude our article with a
discussion of the theoretical advances this new line of research has engendered, as well as its
relationships with two other prominent models of procedural fairness (Section 6). Finally, practical and
policy implications are considered (Section 7), as well as constraints on generality and directions for
future research (Section 8).
The benefits of procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making

Procedural fairness theory is one of the most prominent and extensively documented theoretical
frameworks in psychological literature (Colquitt et al., 2023; De Cremer & Tyler, 2007; De Cremer & Van
Knippenberg, 2002; Van Dijke & De Cremer, 2008). The central tenet of this influential account is that
authorities can increase their <<Table 1 Omitted>> legitimacy as a decision-maker and facilitate
acceptance of their decisions when they apply the principles of procedural fairness to their decision
processes (Tyler, 2006; Verboon & van Dijke, 2011); that is, when they refrain from showing bias towards
one of the stakeholders, grant all those involved the possibility to voice their opinions, use only accurate
information and adhere to strictly ethical guidelines to reach a conclusion, allow for evaluation, and if
necessary, modification of past decisions (Leventhal, 1980; Thibaut & Walker, 1975). Group authorities
can reap further benefits from enacting procedural fairness which go beyond sheer decisional
compliance, because fairness perceptions have also been related to, among others, group cohesion (e.g.,
Lind & Tyler, 1988), commitment to the group as an entity (De Cremer et al., 2010) and cooperation
among group members (De Cremer & Tyler, 2005).
Most relevant to the present research, it has also been shown that procedural fairness is a powerful
approach to handle disputes and disagreements (Huo & Tyler, 2000, 2001; Huo et al., 1996; Tyler et al.,
1998). The fact that procedural fairness has the capacity to defuse potentially conflictual situations
seems particularly relevant for societal decision-makers in modern-day diverse societies, which, as we
have described above, are characterised by the presence of many ethnic-cultural groups (Coleman, 2013;
Pew Research Center, 2008; U.S. Census Bureau, 2008). The sheer fact that each of these minority groups
have their own needs, demands and interests makes it particularly tedious for societal decision-makers
to deal with ethnic-cultural issues, that is, issues related in some way to ethnic, cultural and linguistic
matters and in which ethnic-cultural minority members are thus the stakeholders. Consider, for example,
a situation whereby a local political council has to decide whether or not to allow one of their
administrative staff members working at the town hall to wear a headscarf. Or think about the recent
debates in Western countries about whether or not to remove statues and other monuments
reminiscent of colonial times (Carrega & Allen, 2020). Other real-life examples include the organisation
of religious slaughter during the Eid festival in Belgium (Wax, 2019), language discrimination at schools
(Murillo & Smith, 2011) and allowing prayer during work hours (Rasking et al., 2013). In all the above
cases, the interests of entire minority groups are at stake, and authorities must carefully navigate
through the decision process surrounding these issues, in order to minimise frustration and discontent
among members of the involved minority group and hence, to avoid societal turmoil.
Particularly revealing in this regard is the recent Sanda Dia case in Belgium. Sanda was an undergraduate
student with a migration background who died as a result of physical abuse during a thirty-hour student
baptism. After this tragic incident had occurred, his fellow students who had been in control of the
baptism ceremony were taken to court on charges of unvoluntary manslaughter with suspected racist
motives. Despite the seriousness of the offence, all culprits received rather mild sentences, which
sparked serious popular anger and civil unrest – particularly among ethnic-cultural minorities (Decré,
2023). In response to these public and online displays of discontent, however, representatives of the
court published an open letter wherein they explained the reasons behind the decision and they
organised open debate sessions, allowing people to voice their opinions about the case. In the aftermath
of these initiatives, a slow but steady decrease in minority moral outrage about the trial was observed
(Bauwens, 2023). In this way, the Sanda Dia case illustrates the importance of fair and unbiased

procedures – and clear communication about them – for societal actors when they resolve (sensitive)
ethnic-cultural issues.
Procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making: proximal outcomes
Seminal work has indeed documented the usefulness of procedural fairness enactment in dealing with
such ethnic-cultural issues -see Table 1 (Section 1.1) for a brief overview. Platow et al. (2008), for
example, studied the impact of procedurally fair leadership in the context of a real-life intergroup
dispute, i.e., a series of mob riots between Anglo-Australians and Australians of Middle Eastern descent
in December 2005 in Sydney, Australia. They presented their participants with vignettes describing how
an (Anglo-Australian vs Middle Eastern Australian) authority figure had dealt with these large scale
physical intergroup confrontations. The results revealed that participants themselves were less likely to
engage in similar violent protest actions when an ingroup authority had granted (vs denied) their
ethnic-cultural group voice in the course of the decision process. In a similar vein, Peate and colleagues
(Peate et al., 2008) manipulated Australian citizens’ collective voice perceptions with respect to the
settlement of a land claim dispute between the traditional Australian community and non-Indigenous
Australians. It was shown that providing voice to Indigenous participants’ ethnic-cultural group increased
their acceptance of the outcome of the decision and curbed their willingness to protest against it. In
addition to this, further work by Platow et al. (1998, 2013) in laboratory-induced intergroup settings has
analogously revealed more positive reactions to procedurally fair versus unfair leaders (in terms of
leader trustworthiness, respect, etc.).
Procedural fairness enactment in ethnic-cultural decision-making: distal outcomes
Taken together, these seminal studies clearly illustrate the importance of procedural fairness in
(ethnic-cultural) intergroup issues and/or disputes. Yet, it should be noted that this research has focused
on proximal outcomes or reactions that are directly related to the decision procedure, e.g., evaluations
of the decision-making authority and acceptance of his/her decision. Complementing these seminal
findings, a more recent line of research – summarised in Table 1 – has extensively shown that members
of fairness recipient minority groups can also respond to the perception of ethnic- cultural procedural
fairness in ways that are beneficial to social cohesion and social capital in diverse societies (Dierckx et al.,
2020, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2023, 2023; Valcke et al., 2020, 2020). In the next subsections, we
describe three broad classes of empirical insights that follow from this new body of research findings,
and we will argue why they are relevant for decision-makers in diverse societies.
Minority member societal belongingness and identification
A first series of studies has revealed positive relationships between ethnic- cultural procedural fairness
perceptions among minority group members and their feelings of societal belongingness (i.e., feeling
accepted and included by society at large) and societal identification (i.e., feeling committed to and
satisfied with one’s society at large). For example, Valcke and colleagues (Valcke et al., 2020) showed that
perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by societal actors lead minority group members to feel that
they belong to society. In a scenario study (Study 1), conducted in a Black American sample, these
scholars manipulated the perceived fairness climate (fair versus unfair) towards minorities in a fictitious
society. Specifically, participants read an excerpt of an “interview” in which the interviewer probed a
minority member’s perceptions of the institutions installed in this imaginary nation. In the fair climate
condition, the interviewee described these institutions as fair, unbiased, and striving to implement
ethical and accurate procedures when dealing with ethnic-cultural issues. Conversely, in the unfair

climate condition, the interviewee described them as unfair, biased, and uninterested to implement
ethical and accurate procedures with respect to the resolution of ethnic-cultural issues. Subsequently,
participants were asked to imagine living in this society, and the authors probed their expected sense of
belonging. The results of this mental simulation revealed stronger expectations of societal belongingness
in the fair (compared to the unfair) climate condition. Importantly, these findings could not be explained
in terms of participants’ own procedural fairness perceptions (i.e., with respect to the treatment of
African Americans in the U.S.). Further corroboration for these results was provided by a cross-sectional
study, wherein African Americans reported on their perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by the
main institutions in the U.S. society and their personal feelings of societal belongingness. The results
indeed indicated a strong positive relationship between these target variables (i.e., r = .30).
With respect to (societal) identification, Valcke et al. (2020) reported a similar pattern of procedural
fairness effects. In a questionnaire study (Study 2), the authors sampled linguistic minorities at a
predominantly Dutch Belgian university. These non-Dutch speaking participants completed an online
survey which gauged how procedurally fairly they felt that they and their linguistic minority peers within
their research group were usually treated by their supervisors. They also completed a measure which
probed their feelings of identification with their research group. In a similar vein, Valcke and colleagues
(Valcke et al., 2020, Study 3) also sampled Black American minority group members for a correlational
study. These participants completed measures tapping into their procedural fairness climate perceptions
(with respect to societal actors) and their feelings of identification with society. Across both studies,
Valcke et al. (2020) consistently observed strong and positive relations (rs .32–.46) between participants’
self-reported procedural fairness climate perceptions and their identification with the group represented
by the relevant, study-specific authority (i.e., one’s research group in the academic linguistic minority
sample and the U.S. society in the Black US sample). Taken together, these findings thus indicate that
procedural fairness perceptions vis-à-vis one’s ethnic group can bolster feelings of societal belongingness
and foster identification with superordinate groups, such as one’s research entity or society at large.
Positive personal outcomes for minority group members
Importantly, these heightened feelings of belongingness and identification among minority group
members, in turn, have been shown to yield further downstream beneficial effects. In this section, we
delineate a second set of studies which has revealed positive consequences situated at the personal level
(i.e., for the involved minority group member). For example, Dierckx and colleagues (Dierckx et al., 2021)
reported five studies showing that perceptions of procedurally fair societal institutions can effectively
reduce perceived discrimination among minority group members. Of particular interest are the results of
their second study, which used a modified version of Crocker and Major’s (1989)
attribution-to-discrimination paradigm. In this experiment, Black Americans were invited to participate in
a “text comprehension study”. They first read a bogus news article which portrayed the US societal
institutions as increasingly procedurally fair (vs unfair). Subsequently, in an ostensibly unrelated task, the
authors gauged how this (un)fairness prime affected participants’ interpretation of negative feedback to
a fellow Black American. Specifically, they read an article describing the case of a White landlord who
had refused a young Black man as a tenant for one of his houses, and the participants were invited to
reflect on potential reasons for this rejection. Interestingly, participants in the fair condition were less
likely to attribute this negative outcome to discrimination, compared to those in the unfair condition.
Dierckx et al. (2021) referred to this remarkable result as a “trickle-down” effect, whereby the perception

of procedurally fair societal institutions (situated at the “collective” societal level) culminates into
reduced perceptions of group discrimination by majority group members (situated at the “interpersonal”
level). Of special relevance to the present literature overview, a recent study of ours,1 conducted among
Coloured South Africans, further unravelled that this negative relationship between procedural fairness
and perceived group discrimination was fully mediated by societal identification. Given that perceiving
discrimination against one’s ethnic-cultural group typically yields negative psychological (Kessler et al.,
1999; Priest et al., 2013) and even physical (Anderson, 2013; Williams et al., 2019) consequences, these
curbed perceptions of group discrimination can thus indeed be considered a desirable consequence of
fair treatment by societal actors.
In corroboration with the latter premise, Valcke et al. (2020, Study 3) further observed positive
relationships between Black American participants’ perceptions of procedurally fair treatment by
societal institutions and their subjective well-being, subjective happiness, and psychological health (as
measured by the General Health Questionnaire; Goldberg & Williams, 2000). Analogous to the findings
reported above, it was shown that societal identification mediated these associations. As such, it can be
concluded that minority group members’ procedural fairness perceptions curb their perceived group
discrimination, and they foster their subjective well-being and psychological health – findings which can
be explained in terms of enhanced societal identification. These studies clearly illustrate that
ethnic-cultural procedural fairness can have a positive personal impact on minority group members living
in diverse societies.

It may seem paradoxical to write this in an opinion piece. But it needs saying: arguments alone have no
meaningful effect on people’s beliefs. And the implicit societal acceptance that they do is getting in the
way of other, more effective forms of political thinking and doing.
I’m a researcher who studies the intersection of psychology and politics, and my work has increasingly
led me to believe that our culture’s understanding of how political persuasion works is wrong. In the age
of Donald Trump, Elon Musk and the rise of the far right, commentators have endlessly opined on the
problems of fake news, polarisation and more. But they’ve mostly been looking in the wrong places –
and have focused too much on words.
Take “debates”. They’re a central part of most election campaigns around the world, seen as so
influential that they’re often governed by strict rules around media coverage and balance. Yet evidence
suggests that watching debates has no impact on opinions whatsoever. In 2019 researchers analysed 56
TV debates in 22 elections in the US, Canada, New Zealand and Europe from 1952 to 2016. The study
tracked nearly 100,000 respondents to see whether debates helped undecided or decided voters to
make up or change their minds. They found no evidence that they did. In 2012, a reporter ran another
analysis about whether debates influenced election outcomes. As he put it: “The effects of debates on
eventual votes are likely mild, and, in most cases, effectively nil.”
There are lots of reasons why debate (and indeed, information-giving and argumentation in general)
tends to be ineffective at changing people’s political beliefs. Cognitive dissonance, a phenomenon I

studied as part of my PhD research, is one. This is the often unconscious psychological discomfort we feel
when faced with contradictions in our own beliefs or actions, and it has been well documented. We can
see cognitive dissonance and its effects at work when people rapidly “reason” in ways that are really
attempts to mitigate their discomfort with new information about strongly held beliefs. For example,
before Trump was convicted of various charges in 2024, only 17% of Republican voters believed felons
should be able to be president; directly after his conviction, that number rose to 58%. To reconcile two
contradictory beliefs (that presidents shouldn’t do x, and that Trump should be president), an enormous
number of Republican voters simply changed their mind about the former. In fact, Republican voters
shifted their views on more or less all the things Trump had been convicted of: fewer felt it was immoral
to have sex with a porn star, pay someone to stay silent about an affair, or falsify a business record. Nor is
this effect limited to Trump voters: research suggests we all rationalise in this way, in order to hold on to
the beliefs that let us keep operating as we have been. Or, ironically, to change some of our beliefs in
response to new information, but often only in order to not have to sacrifice other strongly held beliefs.
But it’s not just psychological phenomena like cognitive dissonance that make debates and arguments
relatively ineffective. As I lay out in my book, probably the most important reason words don’t change
minds is that two other factors carry far more influence: our social relationships; and our own actions
and experiences.
A sea of evidence demonstrates that our friends have the power to change our beliefs and behaviour –
not by arguing with us, but simply by being around us or showing us new ways of living. Studies on social
contact theory show that when people are set up in conditions to become friends and collaborate, they
become less prejudiced against the identity groups their new friends belong to. This phenomenon
probably explains many of the advances in gay rights over the past few decades: as people came out,
their friends changed their views on homosexuality, resulting in one of the fastest ever recorded shifts in
public opinion. Similarly, research shows people are most likely to engage in climate-friendly action (such
as installing a heat pump), if their friends do – far more likely than if they are given cash rewards or other
kinds of incentives. Our friends broaden our field of concern; they get us involved in the world, and they
build the trust that human beings appear to require to open up to new ideas. Their indirect influence
achieves more than arguments, especially from strangers, ever could. In other words: when it comes to
persuasion, it’s not the conversation, it’s the relationship.
Our actions and experiences also have a profound effect, albeit often in counterintuitive ways. For
example, research shows that in otherwise similar conditions, women turned away from having an
abortion become somewhat less in favour of abortion rights, while women granted an abortion become
somewhat more in favour. We might expect those denied would become more passionate about access,
but apparently not – probably because, in part due to cognitive dissonance, people tend to bring their
beliefs towards their actions, even if those actions are compelled. The influence of actions and
experiences on belief systems is also evident when it comes to climate crisis: those who experience
intense climate-related weather events are more likely to believe in climate change and in addressing it.
Compared with these influences, arguments alone have comparatively little power. Yet our institutions
are still shaped as though words were enough. We’re infrastructurally, collectively trapped in the liberal
belief that politics is mostly about talking, then sporadically voting. What’s needed by anyone hoping to
promote progressive ideas is not arguments, but an infrastructure that fosters new relationships and
experiences. This could look like anything from a waiting area for parents to mingle before school lets

out, to a workplace union, to a public park. These forms of infrastructure allow people to relate and act
in new ways, building friendships and trust, and encouraging the actions that ultimately allow people to
change their political views.

Simulating policymaking conditions
As intimated, CUPA prescribes two types of prefigurative play. The first is a make-believe, ‘as if’ type: we
ask researchers to act ‘as if’ something desirable, if implausible, has happened. Specifically, we ask them
to imagine that they exercise significant, if not unbounded, power to define policy and then do so; to act
‘as if’ they have just been appointed as a government minister or senior policymaker and then pursue a
utopic political agenda. This will, among other things, facilitate and encourage the embrace of a diverse
affective register. Play, as Cooper explains (2016), can be fun, pleasurable and stimulating, whilst the
utopian impulse should be grounded in radical hopefulness and desire. And of course, rage and
heartbreak do, and should, inform the way we engage with existing policy, and visions of the way
forward. That these emotions are produced through, and are productive of, dreams and their reflexive
deployment is a key dimension in CUPA.
The second form of play demands mimesis: a re-presentation of policy structure and process. As
discussed, identifying and replicating the precise parameters, variations and ‘political pressure points’
(Cooper, 2013, p.14) of a policy system is likely to be a (sometimes prohibitively) difficult task. The
complex and layered assemblage of the state is not only vast but also frequently indeterminate,
sometimes hidden, and often shifting. To speak of ‘policy’ as a defined and easily identifiable ‘text’ is
always already to turn something dynamic, fluid, contested and ongoing into a static ‘object’. The many
dimensions of a policy system may be difficult to ‘capture’ and recreate, and will vary significantly given
that different policy systems are constructed differently, formally or otherwise. This is, therefore, an
aspect of CUPA requiring contextually specific development.
The simulated conditions identified and deployed by projects using CUPA will necessarily be heuristic,
partial and interpretivist: tools with which to produce innovative and provocative forms of knowledge,
which do not and cannot straightforwardly reproduce ‘outside reality’. Put differently, CUPA will produce
policies that manifest via whichever simulated conditions have been selected, interpreted and ‘tested’. In
light of this, the practical task of researchers will be to ascertain what policymaking conditions are
identifiable, reproducible and of interest to specific research projects. We imagine that most efforts to
articulate these conditions will require engagement with specific policy literatures, institutions and
institutional literatures. For instance, one might wish to explore what resources are available to ‘real’
policy-actors working within contemporary policy systems. By way of example, the UK’s HM Treasury
produces a routinely updated ‘Green Book’, a document which provides ‘approved . . . methods to
support the provision of advice to clarify the social – or public – welfare costs, benefits and trade offs of
alternative implementation options for the delivery of policy objectives’ (2022, p.1). Moreover, we would
recommend that simulated conditions relate both to a polity broadly and to the subsystems responsible
for the particular policy or series of policies under scrutiny. The challenge of CUPA is not merely to
produce utopic policies, but to produce utopic policies in response to extant policies. Treating negative
critique as prefatory to utopic policy formulation captures this commitment in important ways, but the

brief could be extended. For instance, we might provisionally suppose that the way a policy topic area is
constructed is a reflection of e.g. contingent configurations of policy-actors and institutional structures.
To emulate and limit oneself by reference to this topic area would therefore be to simulate policy-making
conditions.
The utopian components of this practice encourage radical experimentation within the constraints of the
policymaking system. For example, a project focused on social security policy might attempt to unsettle
how constellations of actors and ‘evidence’ are, or could be, classified and included in policy-making
processes, within the established frameworks of institutional requirements. Kerr (2023) demonstrates
that actors with radically different wealth privilege (welfare-reliant mothers and the super-rich) are
awarded different levels of credibility and – subsequently – different roles in UK policymaking spaces.
Whilst the poor are treated as sources of information, the wealthy become ‘epistemic agents’ whose
knowledge is treated as equally credible to that of the state. CUPA invites theorists to not only explore
how these dynamics might be shifted but to shift them by e.g. actively interviewing welfare recipients as
‘experts’ in committee hearings rather than ‘stakeholders’ to be surveyed, selecting and citing their
accounts as ‘empirical evidence’ rather than service user opinions, and co-creating policy in
collaboration, rather than consultation with them. In a policy area that often implicates the very poorest,
and the very richest, as well as businesses, economists, behavioural scientists and so on, CUPA forces us
to question who is a subject ‘expert’ or a stakeholder with niche interest; whose knowledge should be
considered ‘objective’, and how this objectivity can be credentialised in a way that aligns with positivist
demands for ‘evidence-based’ policymaking; and what policy outcomes might result. Put differently, how
– and to what extent – can existing requirements and norms of policymaking be appropriated and
repurposed, and what vestige of state conservatism remains? Through this enactment and play,
conditions are simulated, and everyday utopic policies can – potentially – be produced.
Alternatively, a researcher interested in budgetary constraint might wish to ‘play’ with the pervasive
neoliberal commitment to reducing fiscal deficits, without leveraging taxation. They might, for instance,
confine their utopic policy re-writes to the budget allocated to an original policy. What socially
progressive, radical policies – if any – might be realisable under this profound constraint? Is radicalism
available on the cheap, and what are the existential consequences of proposing as much? This kind of
project of prefigurative policy reform would have to tackle the not insignificant challenge of expressing
social justice in regressively economic terms, and its reflexive conclusions might ruminate on whether
redistributive policies are even possible in the context of austerity.
In imagining these projects, it is easy to foresee resistance to the playful co-option of statecraft. Critical
theorists have long identified contemporary states as ‘bad objects’: patriarchal, colonialist,
white-supremacist and neoliberal (Dhawan, 2019; Newman, 2019) and cautioned against entanglement
which, intentionally or otherwise, reproduces its forms. How you approach these critiques depends on
how you understand the ontology of the state and the normativity of entanglement. Much
contemporary scholarship resists unitary ontologies of statehood (e.g. the state as monolith) in favour of
an understanding of the state as a multifaceted assemblage of domestic and global interactions,
institutions and practices that produce plural state ‘effects’ (Jessop, 2014; Newman, 2019). This
subsequently undermines readings of the state as ontologically bad, rendering contradictory readings
intelligible and opening up the conceptual space for radical engagements with different elements of
state power – in which critique can operate alongside construction.

Illustratively, Stubbs and Lendvai (2016) suggest that we require a ‘double orientation’ to policy and
power, so that we might ‘recognise hegemonic plans and projects, but . . . be attentive to their
interruptions, disjunctures and challenges’. From this perspective, engagement with state assemblages is
not only normatively ambivalent but sometimes politically necessary. If state spaces hold the potential
for transgression, then a politics of refusal might forgo too much. Building on this, and as parsed by Roy
(2022, p.167) with respect to feminism, the notion that progressive politics is mired by entanglement
with hegemonic conditions traps its proponents ‘into property logics’ and ‘defensive corrective labour’
that ultimately limit ‘world making capacities’. We are, Roy suggests, inexorably in and of the world,
proximate to and inscribed in relations of power, inhabiting a ‘messy ground that cannot be cleaned up’
(Roy, 2022, p.176). In turn, she recommends critique as care – a way to enable accountability without
the paralysis of total refusal. This, then, brings us back to the circularity of Levitas’ utopia as method, to
the epistemological centrality of provisionality, and the necessary collective and reflexive work of
deconstruction, reconstruction and so on.
Conclusion
CUPA realises the prefigurative, utopic promise latent in CPA. By elaborating on CPA’s commitment to
positive critique, it augments its potential. When applied, we anticipate that CUPA will establish a library
of hopeful, enchanting and provisional utopic policies that demonstrate the possible possibilities of
historical state assemblages. Through enactment, and productive estrangement, we anticipate that
researchers will use CUPA to establish a new critical distance from, and vantage point on, existing policy.
In turn, we hope it will aid in the articulation and clarification of utopian desires and contemporary social
movement demands. Moreover, we expect that, by identifying and testing certain kinds of political
pressure points, CUPA will illuminate, in more detail, how these points frustrate progressive projects and
how such frustrations might be addressed. CUPA encourages an avowedly emotional epistemology,
treating radical hopefulness and deep desire as integral to the generation of intellectually and politically
powerful knowledge. Not unrelatedly, it requires a commitment to the alleviation of human suffering
and the realisation of human potential, as well as an imaginative response to related challenges.
Speculation represents a routine form of knowledge production in the field of policy studies, and critical
policy studies is very often involved in normative projections. What added value, then, is provided by an
explicit turn to utopianism, as described here? What does it offer beyond a renewed commitment to
forwardlooking academic praxis and avowed, future-looking normativity? In brief, utopianism promotes
particular epistemic habits, affective experiences and genres of expression with the potential to
significantly extend and pluralise forms of policy knowledge. CUPA harnesses the power of our creative
imagination and capacity to hope and play, and applies this power in unfamiliar and generative ways. It
opens space to produce knowledge through enactment – enabling radical forms of expression and
praxis.
