In this Article, we have presented new evidence that in political question doctrine decisionmaking, <mark>courts treat foreign affairs</mark> cases <mark>different</mark>ly. Across all four of our measures of the foreign affairs character of a case, and even after <mark>controlling for</mark> <mark>other</mark> <mark>factors</mark> that may influence judges’ political question doctrine decisions, we found that the U.S. District Courts are more likely to dismiss foreign affairs cases than non-foreign affairs cases.207 We confirmed Bradley and Posner’s basic finding that the doctrine is concentrated in the area of foreign affairs.208 Beyond that, by using a <mark>large</mark>r <mark>dataset</mark> and <mark>multivariate statistical analysis</mark>, we found <mark>strong ev</mark>idence of differential treatment on the basis of the foreign affairs character of a case. 209 Consistent with the descriptive claims of foreign affairs exceptionalism, foreign affairs cases are indeed different in the political question doctrine context.
Although <mark>scholars</mark> have <mark>argued</mark> that <mark>foreign affairs</mark> have become <mark>normalized</mark> in the U.S. Supreme Court, including <mark>as a result of</mark> the Court’s 2012 decision in <mark>Ziv</mark>otofsky v. Clinton, 210 we found no <mark>evidence </mark>of normalization in the U.S. District Courts. To the contrary, our findings <mark>suggest</mark> <mark>continued</mark> foreign affairs <mark>exceptionalism</mark> in the post-Zivotofsky period, with courts persistently more likely <mark>to dismiss</mark> <mark>foreign affairs</mark> <mark>cases</mark> than other cases on political question grounds. 211 Thus, rather than normalization, our results indicate continuity in foreign affairs exceptionalism in the U.S. District Courts.
Overall, our findings suggest that, as a descriptive matter, the political question doctrine operates largely as a foreign affairs abstention doctrine. To be sure, it is, as we have shown, more than that: the doctrine remains robust in non-foreign affairs cases, too.212 But three of our findings reveal the political question doctrine’s strong foreign affairs focus: the large proportion of political question doctrine dismissals that are foreign affairs cases,213 the significantly higher dismissal rate for foreign affairs cases than for nonforeign affairs cases, 214 and the higher probability of dismissal in foreign affairs cases than in non-foreign affairs cases. 215 Foreign relations law scholars who include the political question doctrine in their catalogs of doctrines used by courts to avoid adjudicating disputes involving foreign affairs have good reason to do so.216
As a prescriptive matter, our findings may be welcome by exceptionalists who have argued against judicial involvement in foreign affairs. For them, our findings might suggest that, as a legal matter, courts are applying the political question doctrine exactly as they should in foreign affairs cases.217 Our findings might also suggest that further doctrinal innovations to limit judicial involvement in cases involving foreign affairs are unnecessary— especially in light of the multiple already-existing alternative doctrines that courts use to dismiss such cases, such as the act-of-state doctrine, the foreign sovereign immunity doctrine, and the forum non conveniens doctrine.218
But our findings are likely to be concerning to normalists who have criticized the political question doctrine’s use by courts to abdicate their duty of judicial review in foreign affairs cases.219 For them, our findings might suggest an even more urgent need for doctrinal change to limit political question doctrine dismissals and for resistance against the emergence of novel abstention doctrines, such as international comity abstention, that would give courts additional doctrinal tools to avoid adjudicating cases concerning foreign affairs.220 Insofar as the political question doctrine operates as a foreign affairs abstention doctrine, one might argue that new foreign affairs abstention doctrines would be redundant to the political question doctrine and other existing abstention doctrines.221
Beyond foreign affairs, our findings shed empirical light on other previously unanswered questions about the political question doctrine in action. 222 For example, although some commentators have argued that the U.S. Supreme Court’s decision in Zivotofsky v. Clinton has had little impact on political question doctrine decisionmaking in the lower courts, we found that the political question doctrine dismissal rate decreased after Zivotofsky. 223 This finding is consistent with our interpretation of Zivotofsky as representing a new jurisprudential regime for political question doctrine decisionmaking that emphasizes the doctrine’s exceptional nature. 224
Moreover, we found that contrary to the widespread belief that the doctrine is no longer frequently used to dismiss non-foreign affairs cases, the doctrine continues to be robustly applied in domestic contexts, especially in cases raising issues of constitutional law.225
 Although there are reasons to expect political question doctrine decisions to be especially fertile ground for ideologically motivated judicial decision making, we found no evidence of ideological effects.226 This finding suggests that in the context of political question doctrine decisions in the U.S. District Courts, there may be at least some degree of agreement across the ideological spectrum regarding choices between judicial abstention and judicial review.
In our era of increasingly expansive assertions of executive power, a sound empirical understanding of the political question doctrine is especially important, even beyond foreign affairs. Ever since Marbury v. Madison, 227 judicial review has come to be accepted as an important pillar of constitutional democracy and the rule of law, for it is largely through judicial review that the political branches of government are held accountable to constitutional constraints.228
Yet the political question doctrine is an exception to judicial review229— and a particularly potent one compared to other limits on justiciability. Whereas the barriers of lack of standing and ripeness can be overcome by finding a proper party to bring a claim and bringing the claim at the proper time, the political question doctrine is “absolute in its foreclosure of judicial scrutiny”; when it applies, “it renders the government conduct immune from judicial review.” 230
From this perspective, our findings are especially relevant. At a time <mark>when</mark> the <mark>exec</mark>utive branch has launched military <mark>strikes</mark> against foreign countries <mark>without Congress</mark>ional <mark>auth</mark>orization,231 the foreign affairs character of a case continues—even post-Zivotofsky—to make it more likely that a <mark>court</mark> will <mark>grant</mark> a <mark>motion to dismiss on political</mark> question <mark>grounds</mark>, precluding judicial review. 232 Likewise, at a time when a wide range of other executive actions are raising international law233 and constitutional law concerns,234 the presence of international law or constitutional law issues makes it more likely that a court will dismiss a case on political question grounds, again precluding judicial review. 235 Our findings thus imply that it is now as important as ever to critically consider the political question doctrine and its implications for judicial review, constitutional democracy, and the rule of law.

Price ’16 [Zachary; 2016; Associate Professor in the Hastings College of the Law at the University of California, J.D. from Harvard University, A.B. from Stanford University; Notre Dame Law Review, “Law Enforcement as Political Question,” https://scholarship.law.nd.edu/ndlr/vol91/iss4/11/, vol. 91]
This Article proposes a framework for nonenforcement’s reviewability— one rooted in considerations of “suitability” for review rather than notions of preclusive executive prerogative. In prior work, I have addressed the scope of executive nonenforcement authority in its own right and directly questioned the origins and validity of a supposed preclusive nonenforcement prerogative.10 Here, I build on this account by exploring reasons why executive enforcement obligations may nonetheless defy complete judicial elaboration. In particular, although courts have often invoked notions of Article II prerogative to justify their passivity with respect to nonenforcement,11 I argue that institutional <mark>limitat</mark>ion<mark>s on courts</mark>—limitations <mark>with </mark>a broader <mark>resonance in constitutional</mark> and administrative law <mark>doctrines—provide</mark> a cogent descriptive and normative <mark>justification for</mark> judicial <mark>deference</mark> to executive nonenforcement.
The Constitution by its terms obligates the President to “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.”12 Yet courts confront very real practical and institutional challenges in ensuring faithful execution of prohibitory statutes by enforcement officials. To begin with, directly <mark>compelling</mark> an <mark>enforcement</mark> suit <mark>in any</mark> particular <mark>case</mark> would raise acute separation-of-powers concerns, as it <mark>would collapse </mark>the constitutional <mark>separation of</mark> judicial and executive <mark>power</mark> and compromise the court’s neutrality in adjudicating the resulting lawsuit. Beyond this particular formal problem, moreover, insofar as enforcement officials must pick and choose between cases because they cannot do everything, courts will rarely have objective benchmarks for assessing whether enforcement agencies are focusing on the right priorities, or indeed whether they are genuinely doing their best at all. The upshot is that exercise of executive nonenforcement authority, like certain other core executive functions, is effectively a political question, in the peculiar sense of the “political question doctrine”—it is an area where institutional limitations on courts place a gap between what executive officials ideally should do and what courts will require from them. What is more, the twin criteria used to identify political questions more generally, “textual assignment” to a political branch and absence of “judicially manageable standards,”13 provide key guideposts for the limits on judicial power over executive enforcement. Bringing enforcement suits and prosecutions in particular cases is a textually assigned function of the executive branch, while the broader executive task of setting priorities for enforcement frequently presents a judicially unmanageable inquiry.

Where states can afford to build, deploy, and control a ballistic missile submarine force, it improves survivability and reduces their dependency on hair trigger alert or launch postures. Deployed in sufficient numbers and on diverse platforms, cruise missiles could preclude first strike vulnerability for even the smallest nuclear states. And cruise missiles are highly accurate. Third, nuclear war is unlikely to occur by means of a surprise attack “out of the blue” absent prior political confrontation. Fourth, the quality of national military command and control systems matters a great deal in maintaining nuclear stability with eight, or fewer, nuclear powers in Asia. Command and control systems must provide for negative control against accidents or usurpation of authority, and for secure positive control to guarantee at least minimum or assured retaliation. Nuclear command and control systems are based on the soft power of knowledge-intensive technologies instead of the hard power of metal and mass. Disorganized or rigid command-control systems may push decision makers into unnecessary reliance on simplified options and fast triggers. Political accountability, especially during a nuclear crisis, matters just as much. Whose fingers are on the button in, for example, North Korea or Pakistan? Who is in charge?
