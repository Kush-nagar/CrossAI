Background – The Changing Narrative Around Mag-Moss Rulemaking
For decades, the FTC has developed its consumer protection rules in one of two ways: (1) as directed by Congress in federal legislation or (2) under Section 18 of the FTC Act. While rulemakings in the first category generally follow the Administrative Procedures Act, Section 18 requires many more steps.
Most of these steps were added to the law in 1980, in response to perceived overreach by the agency during the Pertschuk era. According to Jeffrey Lubbers, a professor who has analyzed <mark>FTC rules developed</mark> under Mag-Moss (e.g., the Credit Practices and Used Car rules), the<mark> average time</mark> it <mark>took</mark> to complete them was almost <mark>six years</mark>, with some rules taking even longer. By contrast, the average time to complete rules under the APA (e.g., the COPPA and GLB rules) was less than a year. Due to this dramatic difference, Mag-Moss <mark>rulemaking</mark> ha<mark>s</mark> long been viewed to be <mark>extremely cumbersome</mark> – not a process to be launched lightly or with the expectation of rapid completion.
During the past year, however, the narrative around Mag-Moss has changed dramatically as the FTC has sought new ways to obtain monetary relief and strengthen its hand in privacy. In March 2021, the FTC formed a task force designed to identify possible candidate for Mag-Moss rulemaking, acknowledging its “bad reputation” but touting its capacity to deter harms through market-wide rules and civil penalties. Then in July, the FTC (by 3-2 vote) announced changes to its internal rules, stating that the changes would “streamline” the Mag-Moss rulemaking process, remove “decades of self-imposed red tape,” and allow the FTC to issue “timely rules” on a wide range of issues, particularly in the area of privacy.
One week later, the President’s Executive Order on Competition encouraged the FTC to issue a rule to address “unfair data collection and surveillance practices” – a call soon echoed in letters from Congress and consumer advocates. Then, on December 9, following a series of policy statements extolling the virtues of rulemaking vs. “whack-a-mole” enforcement, Chair Khan released an ambitious plan to launch multiple Mag-Moss rulemakings to “define <mark>with</mark> specificity” unfair or deceptive practices – including a range of “abuses stemming from surveillance-based business models.” (Khan also called for multiple rules to define “unfair methods of competition” – an equally bold and controversial move that implicates another part of the FTC Act.)
Presto! In just eight short months of (seemingly lockstep) messaging, the counter-narrative was complete: Mag-Moss rulemaking (lots of it) will enable the FTC to stop industry-wide harms, obtain penalties in a wide range of areas, and regain its leadership on privacy.
The Many Steps in the Mag-Moss Rulemaking Process
Now comes the hard part – actually developing these rules in the “timely” way that’s been promised. Of significance, Mag-Moss requires the FTC to prove that any practice it seeks to regulate is unfair or deceptive under the FTC Act – i.e., it doesn’t expand the FTC’s ability to reach conduct it can’t already reach in its enforcement actions. Here are the statutory <mark>requirements</mark> in greater detail:
1. FTC publishes Advanced Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (ANPR) for public comment. ANPR must detail FTC’s area of focus and objectives, as well as possible regulatory alternatives. Thirty days prior to publication, FTC must send copies to its Congressional oversight committees.
2. FTC publishes Notice of <mark>Proposed Rule</mark>making (NPR) seeking public comment. NPR must set forth the rule text, reasons supporting it, any alternatives, and a preliminary regulatory analysis assessing the costs and benefits of the proposal and alternatives. At the time of publication, FTC must have reason to believe (in the form of FTC cease and desist orders or other evidence of widespread conduct) that the practices are prevalent. FTC must make all comments public.
3. Informal <mark>hearings</mark>. Any interested person may seek a hearing and present views. If FTC determines there are disputed issues, it must allow participants to submit rebuttal information and cross-examine other persons. (FTC may impose limits on this process but such limits are a basis for court review and reversal – see below.) A presiding officer shall make recommendations as to all relevant and material evidence.
4. FTC publishes <mark>Final Rule</mark>. Rule must be accompanied by a Statement of Basis and Purpose detailing (1) the prevalence of the practices (2) how they are unfair or deceptive, <mark>and</mark> (3) the economic effect of the rule. Final regulatory analysis must assess the rule’s costs and benefits and why it was chosen over alternatives. Any person may petition FTC for exemption from the rule.
5. <mark>Judicial review</mark>. Any person may seek review (in the D.C. Court of Appeals) within 60 days of promulgation. In addition to normal grounds for review under the APA, the court may (1) direct FTC to consider additional submissions (2) set aside the rule if it’s not supported by “substantial evidence” (a markedly different standard than “arbitrary and capricious”) (3) set aside the rule if FTC’s limits on rebuttal or cross examination precluded disclosure of material facts. Court decisions are final, subject only to Supreme Court review.
6. Rule enforcement. When enforcing rules, FTC may seek penalties (now up to $46,517 per violation) and/or consumer redress in federal district court. However, to get penalties, FTC must show the defendant violated the rule with “actual knowledge or knowledge fairly implied,” and must refer the case to DOJ for review and filing.

The agency has followed the Trump administration’s lead to axe government resources, however its approach has become more labour-intensive.
The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) has fallen in line with the running trend of the second Trump administration to cut its staffing and work with fewer resources. Under Chair Andrew Ferguson, the agency has decided to streamline its focus on health care and Big Tech, priorities on which the previous leadership under Lina Khan also focused. However, the FTC has also opted for a more laborious approach to its enforcement, leading to inevitably overlooked enforcement cases for the agency.

Brain Drain in the Time of Enforcement
Trump has defined his second term as President by culling the staff numbers at key US agencies, with Ferguson adhering to the Trump party line and its unitary executive theory of government, according to Squire Patton Boggs US antitrust and competition practice head Michael Wise. He tells CDR: “The FTC really is taking direction from the president in ways that typically has not been the case in the past.”

The FTC had cut 94 of its staff by May this year, with aspirations to reduce its workforce by 10% overall. The recent government shutdown has added fuel to the fire, after the Office of Personnel Management issued instructions for agencies to “prepare decisional documents” in line with reduction in force (RIF) decision-making. Wise concludes that the administration is “trying to find ways to use the current shutdown as a way to reduce the size of government”.

However, Ferguson has opted to operate with a case-by-case enforcement approach, rather than the
sweeping rules-based approach that was utilised during Khan’s time in the role. Wise notes: “It is an interesting situation at the moment, because you have what I would think of as normally competing interests that are butting heads.”

On the one hand, he states that Ferguson is “very intentionally aligning himself with Trump on a couple of areas that are antithetical in some ways to that aggressive enforcement stance”, including with his staffing and resourcing cuts. He has also stepped into line with Trump’s agenda to heavily deregulate.

However, this case-by-case enforcement approach is far more laborious. “Each case requires an individualised assessment,” explains Wise. Additionally, he notes that it creates more uncertainty for clients, as “the approach tends to be a little bit more scattered because they do not have the resources to pursue broad policy changes, it does mean that clients have to be a little bit more on guard”.

Health care and Big Tech
<mark>With fewer weapons in its arsenal, the FTC has decided to</mark> streamline its focus on health care and life sciences, and Big Tech issues. Greenberg Traurig antitrust litigation and competition regulation cochair Bill Katz points out that health care “has always been a priority for the FTC, regardless of who is in the White House […] because the health care industry in the US affects so many people”.

As for Big Tech, the FTC has maintained course on several key Biden-era decisions to break up Big Tech strongholds, and Ferguson has also agreed with previous FTC efforts against Amazon, with the agency securing an order against the retail behemoth in September this year for its enrolment of millions of consumers in Amazon Prime subscriptions, which the company subsequently made difficult to cancel.

However, Wise remarks that while the FTC is <mark>focus</mark>ing <mark>its aggressiveness on Big Tech</mark> and the health care sector, <mark>fewer resources mean it is “at the expense of</mark> aggressiveness in <mark>other areas that they might</mark> historically <mark>be</mark> more <mark>involved in”.</mark>

He adds that this reality means that “if you are not in one of those target areas […] <mark>it is unlikely that</mark> [the <mark>FTC] even have the resources to come after other</mark> types of conduct that they might in a different environment”.

The result of the agency’s direction under Ferguson appears to have been a drop in antitrust business for US lawyers. Greenberg Traurig antitrust litigation and competition regulation co-chair Tonya Esposito says that in her practice, she has “experienced a bit of a slowdown in terms of the pace with which the FTC seems to be proceeding”.

Katz remarks that he is keeping an eye on which cases the FTC goes after, and says these instances provide guidance on which <mark>industries</mark> the FTC is deciding to go after in its enforcement actions.

“What you see is what you get. If [the FTC] is bringing enforcement actions in a particular industry related to a particular type of conduct […] then if I have got clients in that industry engaged in similar types of conduct, this is a good way to check in and ensure we are not doing anything inconsistent with the enforcement actions being brought.”

Morrison Foerster global antitrust co-chair Alexander Okuliar agrees, noting: “That is why I think the <mark>agency is going to be careful about where it directs its resources</mark> and its enforcement efforts, so that each case brought is very impactful and precedential. Because that is the way you can establish industry guidance in spaces, by showing people this is what we are going to pursue as an agency and succeeding in that pursuit.”

Of course, just because it is now legal doesn’t mean sports betting is anything new. PASPA merely pushed the practice to an underground black market, as overly restrictive government regulation does. The illicit betting market thrived for decades, with billions of dollars in economic activity going from American’s wallets to illegal offshore operations, criminal interests, and beyond.

With legal sports betting becoming more widely available, research shows bettors are taking their business to the legal, regulated market.

New research from the American Gaming Association (AGA) shows that 74% of sports bettors believe that it is important to bet with legal betting services. This corresponds with a <mark>25% decrease in spending with illegal books</mark> since the Supreme Court ruling. In other words, <mark>bets are shifting from the illegal market to the legal one.</mark>

In addition to simply wanting to comply with the law, bettors say they prefer legal markets because they know their wagers will be paid out, and that there are legal remedies they can pursue if a problem arises.

The study also found that 55% of people who placed wagers with illegal operators did so thinking they were betting legally.

Legal sports betting, with low tax rates and reasonable regulation, is a win for the economy, and taxpayers. It is a better way for government to gain new revenue than seeking to hike existing taxes.

Americans have legally wagered more than $22 billion since the 2018 ruling opening up sports betting in the states. That translates to $198 million in new tax revenue for state and local governments – and that is with less than half the states having sports betting.

Moreover, 2020 was set to be a record year for the industry. Before the pandemic hit, $3.5 billion had been wagered in January and February, smashing the previous record of $1.9 billion from a year earlier.

There is room for the sports betting market to grow, and opportunity for smart government policies. Still, overly aggressive government taxation and regulation can ruin a good thing. High tax rates, especially those above 15%, will reduce betting activity, as bettors stick with the black market.
