Another potential source of friction between China and India arises from Chinese plans (or rumors of such plans) to divert water from the upper Brahmaputra and funnel it via a series of tunnels and canals to northeastern China, where existing supplies are hugely inadequate.33 While dismissed by many Chinese experts as overly ambitious and costly, the notion of diverting water from the Brahmaputra has generated considerable anxiety in India, where experts fear that the resulting decline in water flow into the Indian section of the river would threaten agricultural productivity. Given the centrality of farming in the Indian economy and political system, any Chinese move to proceed with such a diversion project could lead to increased tension between the two countries. 34
Few analysts believe that a Sino-Indian conflict over the Brahmaputra is likely in the years immediately ahead. Both countries have strong motives for maintaining friendly–if not necessarily, warm–relations between them, and water issues have not yet dominated the bilateral agenda. This, however, is where global warming enters the picture. The Brahmaputra, like the Indus, draws much of its flow during dry seasons from the melting of Himalayan glaciers–and these, as has already been noted, are melting as a result of climate change, and could eventually disappear. For both China and India, the melting of the Himalayan glaciers will have momentous consequences. Given the Brahmaputra’s critical importance to agriculture and economic activity in both countries, <mark>any</mark> significant longterm <mark>decline in its flow would be</mark> highly <mark>disruptive, causing</mark> widespread hardship and social <mark>unrest</mark>. 35

Any conflict between China and India over the waters of the Brahmaputra, should one occur, is most likely to remain a localized affair, without provoking a full-scale mobilization of forces on both sides. During the 1962 war over Arunachal Pradesh, Chinese army troops engaged their Indian counterparts in disputed areas along the border, but neither side escalated to large-scale combat. Conclusion
As noted in this article, most analyses of water’s role in the climate-conflict nexus emphasizes the likelihood that warming-related scarcities will exacerbate pre-existing antagonisms within poor and divided states, leading to internal conflict and state collapse. Nevertheless, some analysts have considered the possibility that water shortages could also lead to conflict between states, notably if the countries involved depend to a considerable extent on a shared river system and if one of the riparian states–typically, the upstream country–diverts the river’s flow for its own benefit at the expense of downstream countries.
This risk appears particularly significant in South Asia, where several highly-populated countries rely on a number of major rivers which depend for a significant part of their flow on meltwater from the Himalayan glaciers—large frozen reservoirs of water that are contracting as a result of climate change. In the absence of greater efforts by these countries to address this peril in a collaborative, equitable manner, looming <mark>water shortages</mark> could <mark>combine with</mark> other <mark>antagonisms to trigger</mark> armed conflict, possibly entailing the use of <mark>nuclear weapons</mark>. As any such conflict–even those fought without nuclear weapons–would cause widespread chaos and economic turmoil, it behooves U.S. leaders to work with their counterparts in China, India, and Pakistan to promote greater collaboration in the management of their shared water resources.

A modern <mark>great power war</mark> could see nuclear weapons, bioweapons, autonomous weapons, and other destructive new technologies deployed on an unprecedented scale.
It would probably be the most destructive event in history, shattering our world. It could even threaten us with extinction.

We’ve come perilously close to just this kind of catastrophe before.¶ On October 27, 1962 — near the peak of the Cuban Missile Crisis — an American U-2 reconnaissance plane set out on a routine mission to the Arctic to collect data on Soviet nuclear tests. Competition among the world’s most powerful countries shapes our world today. And whether it’s through future incidents like the lost U-2, or something else entirely, it’s plausible that it could escalate and lead to a major, devastating war.
Is there anything you can do to help avoid such a terrible outcome? It is, of course, difficult to imagine how any one individual can hope to influence such world-historical events. Even the most powerful world leaders often fail to predict the global consequences of their decisions.
But I think the likelihood and severity of great power war makes this among the most pressing problems of our time — and that some solutions could be impactful enough that working on them may be one of the highest-impact things to do with your career.
By taking action, I think we can create a future where the threat of great power war is a distant memory rather than an ever-present danger.

This has heightened worries about Beijing's capacity to bolster its hold on vital minerals in weaker nations. Afghanistan's resources are viewed strategically by regional actors as well. The strategic importance of REEs cannot be overstated in today’s technology-driven world. These minerals are indispensable for manufacturing high-performance magnets, batteries, wind turbines, and military hardware, including missile guidance systems and stealth technologies. China currently dominates the global rare earth supply chain, controlling approximately 80 % of production and 90 % of processing capacity [10]. This near-monopoly has raised alarms in Western capitals, particularly as tensions between the U.S. and China escalate over trade, technology, and military supremacy. The growing demand for green energy solutions has further heightened the scramble for rare earths, as countries seek to secure supplies for their renewable energy transitions. Afghanistan’s untapped deposits, therefore represent a potential alternative source that could reduce reliance on China. However, exploiting these resources is fraught with challenges, including Afghanistan’s lack of infrastructure, ongoing insurgency, and the geopolitical maneuvering of global powers seeking to control or influence access to these critical minerals.
Afghanistan’s mineral wealth has been known for decades, but decades of conflict have prevented large-scale extraction. Soviet geologists first mapped the country’s mineral potential in the 1980s, and subsequent surveys by the U.S. Geological Survey and the Pentagon confirmed significant deposits of rare earth elements, lithium, copper, and iron ore [6]. Among the most notable sites are the Hajigak iron ore mine, which also contains rare earth elements worth an estimated $336 billion, and the Khanneshin carbonatite complex in Helmand Province, which is rich in lanthanum and cerium. Lithium deposits in Nuristan and Herat have also drawn comparisons to major reserves in Bolivia and Chile (Discovery [11]). Without using metaphorical analogies, geological surveys demonstrate Afghanistan's potential as a vital mineral supplier by confirming substantial lithium, neodymium, and rare earth deposits. Despite this potential, Afghanistan’s mining sector remains underdeveloped due to insecurity, corruption, and a lack of investment. The <mark>Taliban</mark>’s takeover in 2021 has added another layer of uncertainty, as the group <mark>seeks to monetize </mark>these <mark>resources while facing international sanctions</mark> and limited recognition. <mark>China has emerged as the most active</mark> foreign <mark>player</mark>, securing rights to the Mes Aynak copper mine and expressing interest in rare earth projects. The Mes Aynak ore body is located about 30 km southeast of Kabul and contains copper and cobalt deposits worth an estimated $102 billion [12]. China invested $630 million in non-financial direct investment in Afghanistan at the end of 2020, primarily in the area of mining (Global [13]). However, <mark>Western companies remain wary</mark> of engaging with the Taliban regime, leaving Afghanistan’s mineral sector in a state of suspended animation, caught between geopolitical rivalries and internal instability. The competition over Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals is a microcosm of the broader great-power rivalry between the United States and China. For China, securing access to these resources aligns with its Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and its long-term strategy to dominate global supply chains [15]. Chinese state-owned enterprises have already made significant investments in Afghanistan’s mining sector, viewing the country as a potential supplier of critical minerals to fuel China’s high-tech industries. However, Beijing faces significant risks, including security threats from groups like ISIS-K, which have targeted Chinese interests in the region. Additionally, international sanctions on the Taliban complicate financial transactions and deter broader investment. The United States, meanwhile, has adopted a more cautious approach, focusing on diversifying its rare earth supply chains through partnerships with allies like Australia and Canada rather than engaging directly with Afghanistan under Taliban rule. The mining sector in Afghanistan is controlled by the Taliban, and it is unclear where revenues end up [7]. Washington has also invested in research to develop alternative materials and recycling technologies to reduce its dependence on rare earths altogether. Russia, though not a major consumer of these minerals, has sought to position itself as a mediator in Afghanistan, leveraging its historical ties and security partnerships to maintain influence in the region. The recent visit to Kabul by Sergei Shoigu, secretary of Russia’s National Security Council and former defense minister, sends a clear signal to the West about which bloc has the most influence in the country [16]. This great-power competition underscores how Afghanistan’s mineral wealth has become entangled in broader geopolitical struggles, with each actor pursuing strategies that reflect their national interests and risk tolerance.
Regional dynamics further complicate the picture, as neighboring states jockey for influence over Afghanistan’s resources. <mark>Pakistan</mark>, which shares a long and porous border with Afghanistan, <mark>views the country’s mineral wealth as</mark> both <mark>an</mark> economic <mark>opportunity</mark> and a potential source <mark>of instability</mark> [9]. A stable Afghanistan is essential for the successful development of the China-Pakistan Economic Corridor [17]. The China-Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC) could serve as a conduit for transporting Afghan minerals to global markets, but security concerns and political tensions with the Taliban regime pose significant challenges. India, meanwhile, has sought to counterbalance Chinese influence in Afghanistan but faces logistical and diplomatic hurdles, particularly given its strained relations with Pakistan. Iran, which has cultural and economic ties to western Afghanistan, may also seek to leverage its position to gain access to mining projects, particularly in regions with large Shia populations. Iran also secured chromite through barter in exchange for fuel imports (Discovery [11]). Central Asian states like Uzbekistan and Tajikistan, while they could potentially serve as transit routes for Afghan minerals, remain wary of the Taliban’s ideological alignment with militant groups in their own countries. These regional rivalries and alliances create a complex web of interests that will shape how Afghanistan’s mineral resources are developed or whether they remain untapped due to persistent instability and competing agendas.
The economic potential of Afghanistan’s rare earth minerals is immense, but so are the governance challenges that could prevent the country from capitalizing on this wealth. The “resource curse” theory, which posits that countries rich in natural resources often suffer from corruption, conflict, and economic stagnation, looms large over Afghanistan. The Taliban’s lack of international recognition, coupled with their limited administrative capacity, raises serious questions about whether they can manage the mining sector transparently and equitably. Corruption has long plagued Afghanistan’s extractive industries, with reports of illegal mining, smuggling, and revenue diversion under previous governments [18]. The <mark>Taliban’s reliance on mining revenues to fund their regime</mark> could exacerbate these issues, particularly, if they prioritize short-term gains over sustainable development. Additionally, unregulated mining poses significant environmental risks, including water contamination and habitat destruction, which <mark>could</mark> provoke local opposition and further <mark>destabilize the country</mark>. Because of the possibility of acid leaching, soil erosion, and toxic tailings contaminating essential water systems, environmental risks are especially serious. Such outcomes highlight the need for sustainable extraction frameworks and are comparable to those seen in mining regions of the DRC and Myanmar with lax regulations. Without robust governance mechanisms and international oversight, Afghanistan’s mineral wealth may fail to translate into broad-based economic growth, instead <mark>fueling conflict</mark> and inequality.

3.3. Limitations and mitigations This study further acknowledges several methodological and contextual limitations inherent in researching rare earth elements extraction in Afghanistan. A critical constraint stems from the Taliban regime’s opacity, which restricts access to reliable mining production data, revenue figures, and official policies [20]. Government reports from the pre-2021 era may be outdated, while current Taliban disclosures lack independent verification. To mitigate this, the study cross-references multiple sources, including satellite imagery analysis by groups like Global Witness and confidential industry estimates from mining consultants. Security risks present another major limitation, as physical fieldwork in mining regions like Helmand remains hazardous due to insurgent activity and political sensitivities [21]. This constraint affects the direct observation of extraction practices and community impacts. As a workaround, the study relies on remote sensing data, verified local media reports, and interviews with NGO workers who maintain a limited operational presence. However, these secondary sources may miss nuanced on-ground realities. The research also faces potential selection bias in expert interviews. Many interviewees – particularly Western analysts and former Afghan officials – may harbor inherent skepticism toward Taliban governance [22]. Conversely, Taliban-affiliated sources might exaggerate the regime’s administrative capabilities. To balance these perspectives, the study includes input from neutral third-party actors like UN monitors and incorporates academic literature from Chinese and Russian scholars to diversify geopolitical viewpoints. Temporal limitations affect the study’s findings, given Afghanistan’s rapidly evolving political landscape. The Taliban’s mineral policies and international engagement strategies may shift abruptly due to economic pressures or factional disputes [23]. To address this, the research framework includes quarterly updates from monitoring groups like the Afghanistan Analysts Network, ensuring findings remain relevant despite fluid conditions. Lastly, the comparative case analysis carries inherent limitations as no perfect analogies exist for Afghanistan’s unique combination of Islamic governance, a sanctions regime, and mineral wealth. While the DRC and Myanmar provide useful parallels, differences in cultural, geographic, and historical contexts necessitate cautious interpretation [24]. The DRC illustrates how institutional weakness enabled warlord capture of mining rents, while Myanmar shows how sanctions fractured formal markets, fueling illicit extraction. By contrast, Afghanistan’s theocratic hybrid model differs by embedding religious authority in mining governance. The study explicitly acknowledges these disparities during analysis to avoid overgeneralization. Through these mitigation strategies – source triangulation, diversified sampling, real-time monitoring, and contextual awareness – the research maintains rigor despite Afghanistan’s challenging research environment. Future studies could enhance validity by incorporating advanced geospatial analysis or a partnership with Islamic scholars to interpret Taliban governance frameworks more accurately.
