Meiches ‘17 [Benjamin Meiches, 4-10-2017, ***Assistant Professor of Security Studies and Conflict Resolution at the University of Washington-Tacoma,*** “Weapons, Desire, and the Making of War” Critical Studies on Security 5, no. 1, p. 1-9,  http://dx.doi.org/10.1080/21624887.2017.1312149, ACCESS: https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Zwb53a34kH2NiPpX_lwZS-WAl6mGtj6Q/view?usp=sharing] // SPF + kd

What makes weapons fire? What causes states to gather arms? How do weapons influence individual action? Long before the popularisation of Actor–Network Theory (ANT), Bruno Latour took a shot at these questions by indicting the National Rife Association’s classic claim: ‘guns don’t kill people, people kill people’. Latour blew the argument away: ‘What does the gun add to the shooting? In the materialist account, everything: an innocent citizen becomes a criminal by virtue of the gun in her hand. The gun enables of course, but also instructs, directs, even pulls the trigger- and <mark>who, with a knife in her hand, has not wanted</mark> at some time <mark>to stab someone or something</mark>’ (1994, 31). What Latour describes is <mark>a desire to stab </mark>(or shoot) that <mark>emerges from</mark> an <mark>encounter with</mark> the <mark>weapon</mark>. This desire, Latour argues, <mark>incites</mark> a <mark>transformative reaction in humans</mark> that effectively <mark>enables weapons to produce their users</mark>.

This article expands on this observation by developing a speculative account of the powers of weapons to generate human desires. The argument originates from the suspicion that design, narrative, and violence constitute only instances of the complex imbrications of humans and weapons. Consequently, the article contends that <mark>weapons should be understood</mark> <mark>first</mark> and foremost <mark>as entities that operate on desire</mark> and <mark>only secondly as instruments of violence</mark>. To this end, the article criticises the <mark>dominant image of weapons</mark> present <mark>in</mark> international relations (<mark>IR</mark>) and security studies that <mark>subordinate the use of weapons to human intentionality</mark>. This model <mark>simultaneously overstates the role of human interest in shaping the</mark> form and <mark>function of weapons and understates the formative powers of weapons in generating human conduct</mark>. The primary contribution of this article is the development of a theoretical paradigm that offers weapons a more substantial in the production of insecurity, war, and violence. As such, the purpose of this article is to offer a broad outline of the series of affective connections that link humans and weapons and to demonstrate that weapons operate as bodies that incite, prompt, and undermine human agency in ways that are largely unaccounted for by traditional security studies literature.

Indeed, in a Spinozan sense, a weapon is a body with its own ‘conatus’ or striving to preserve its being (1992, 108). The possibility of a weapon ‘conatus’ implies that weapons engage in complex relationships with humans for their own purposes. Consider, for example, an approach to understanding weapons in terms of the biological metaphor of evolution (Fishel, forthcoming). Understood as a form of non-life, weapons fire, rust, and explode in stockpiles, battlefields, jungles, deserts, and other environments in unprecedented numbers (Nordstrom 2004). In fact, the past several millennia could plausibly be described as a catalytic process of weapon emergence and development or an adaptive success story including attendant processes of diversification, speciation, and resiliency. Weapons, understood metaphorically, thrive in an ecosystem whose basic conditions they have forever altered through a series of feedback loops (Bateson 2000). In this light, weapons benefit from the formation of a complementary relationship with their human designers and users who facilitate weapon procreation and proliferation. <mark>Discourses about human</mark> sovereignty and<mark> intentionality create</mark> a <mark>highly productive</mark> ruse or a <mark>form of camouflage,</mark> which <mark>disguise</mark>s <mark>how weapons exploit fear</mark>, conflict, and profit <mark>to recreate the conditions of their regeneration</mark>. This ruse would be an advantageous strategy since the human population reproducing the myth would also be the group primarily subjected to it. The successful implementation of this strategy would require specific affective connections between weapons and human populations, which, while diverse, integrate weapons into human fantasy, embodiment, institutional arrangements, and aesthetic proclivities. In short, it requires affects that necessitate weapon– human relations. Weapons consequently would need to develop the capacity to produce desires for these relations. While this perspective may seem absurd, leaping forward with assumptions about the distinction between life and non-life, agency and causality, anthropomorphism and sociality, it offers a valuable reinterpretation of weapons, which calls into question the dimensions of the weapon–human relationship that facilitate the explosive growth and diversification of weaponry. In doing so, it offers an alternative figuration of the agencies at work in a historical process that might best be described as ‘the weaponisation of politics’, where weapons not only coexist with humans, but are cared for, sought after, and treated as essential features of political life.

This article proceeds in four sections. The first contrasts the role of weapons in traditional IR theory with the recent turn to think non-human objects and things as political entities. It shows how dominant assumptions about state security, power, and social constructions problematically restrict the role of weapons in political life. The second section develops an account of weapons as desiring- machines drawing from the writings of JFC Fuller, the philosophers Gilles Deleuze and Felix Guattari, and contemporary theorists of violence. By demonstrating the poignant relationship between weapons, desire, and affect, this section describes multiple different models for how weapons incite human desires. The third section uses these insights to examine Carl von Clausewitz’s classical definition of war and shows how his definition omits weapon agencies. Reading weapon agency back into Clausewitz introduces an alternative explanation about how weapons precipitate warfare. The final section extends this insight Gregoire Chamayou’s critical reading of drone warfare. This section demonstrates how weapons, as nonhuman forces, have played a powerful role in the making of war long before the appearance of drones.

Section 1. Weapons in international relations

Traditional IR theory views weapons as tools, which augment or diminish state power, but do not change its essential functioning. Consequently, <mark>IR focuses on how different actors deploy weapons in</mark> balances of power, strategic windows, or <mark>institutional patterns</mark>. However, by rendering war ‘unwinnable’ the invention of nuclear power complicates this relationship (Jervis 1990) and presents an anomaly in state-centric explanations of power (Herz 1962). Contemporary theories have used the anomalies of nuclear politics as a way of rereading the political tradition (Deudney 2007). Nonetheless, the tool-based explanation of weapons remains prominent even in the context of nuclear politics where various problems related to proliferation, accidents, misuse, testing, and mining are understood as forms of mismanagement or poor policy (Perrow 1999; Sagan 1995). Even the possibility of nuclear disarmament appeals to this dominant paradigm (Burke 2009). In this way, the small space for a critical interrogation of weapons in relation to nuclear power ultimately disappears by reasserting the utility of weapons to state agencies (Biswas 2014; Mutimer 1999).

Constructivist and critical approaches to weapons, in contrast, introduce many new elements into the discussion of weaponry, such as the normative regimes, legal barriers, and historical or identity-based prohibitions regarding specific weapons systems (Cooper and Mutimer 2011; Kinsella 2011; Pretorius 2008). Constructivist and critical approaches thus challenge the naturalness of the state’s control of weaponry. Unfortunately, this upside comes at the cost of treating weapons as largely derivative of social belief. The materiality of weapons, procedures of technical design and engineering, and the affective properties of weapons are consequently treated as secondary in this approach. For example, Helen Kinsella’s The Image Before the Weapon, which offers a brilliant genealogy of the ‘principle of distinction’, shows how gender and power produce the division between combatants and non-combatants (2011, 6). However, the account privileges the articulation of this social division largely independent of the materiality of the different targeting, delivery, and mechanical systems constitutive of weapons. While the relationship the principle of distinction has clear implications for weapon design and deployment, weapon systems also make substantive contributions to the formation of the combatant non-combatant division by establishing the conditions of possibility for a social distinction to be operational. In this respect, a materialist approach deepens the resources available for critical insights into the genesis of social distinctions regarding the use of weapons. Indeed, recent work in new materialism, object-oriented ontology, and speculative realism foregrounds the agency of objects in social life. While there is considerable debate over the merits of different approaches to non-human entities, these theories offer an important rebuttal to classic realism, rational human interest, and social construction as explanations of causality and ontology (Shaviro 2014; Bogost 2012; Meillassoux 2010; Harman 2009; Barad 2007). According to these theories, objects of all kinds resist the categories, representations, and strategies applied to them while producing unintended or unanticipated changes in politics. Focusing on objects consequently shifts the register for interpreting security institutions as independent entities, which have productive effects on security and insecurity (Grove 2014). Object-centred analysis thus challenges a basic conceit of IR theory by opening up the black box of objects. Materialist analyses similarly introduce an alternative reading of the role of weapons in political life because they afford weaponry an indeterminate, yet significant potential to influence other actors. While it is beyond the scope of this article to fully outline materialist approaches, whether understood in terms of ‘actants’, ‘thing power’, or ‘aesthetic dimension’, the key value of materialist thinking is to destabilise the privilege given to human action (Latour 1994; Bennett 2010; Bryant 2011; Bogost 2012; Morton 2013). In doing so, materialist theories enter new entities and relations into security analysis and offer new explanations of the emergence of violence. The specific approach adopted by this article is to view relations between human and non-human objects as a series of affective encounters, which produce desire or motive force. In this context, desire does not refer to static or mutable human interests, but preconscious social drives and impulses, which constitute the motivational forces that give form to individual interest (Widder 2016; Smith 2007). In this sense, ‘desire’ as discussed throughout the article relies on Deleuze and Guattari’s vision of desire as a series of forces that produce a social field, which in turn frames how individuals respond to a particular set of possibilities. As the next section will detail, this approach explores points of contact between human and non-human things, which allows for formative relationships to emerge between them without treating one element of this relationship as fully dominant. In this sense, this article theorises how human–weapon encounters produce forms of desire, which slowly develop into stable relationships between weapons and humans. The benefit of this approach is that it does not predetermine the meaning of weapons by assuming they operate as instruments of violence, their human intended use, but instead examines their various ‘productive’ effects on human fantasies, bodies, and politics. At present, critical literature on weapons engages the relationship to materiality in several ways. First, a burgeoning literature examines specific weapon assemblages such as tanks, planes, chemical weapons, landmines, or small arms (Larrinaga 2016; Vucetic 2016; Shapiro 2015; Beier 2011; Stavrianakis 2011). Second, several histories of arms and warfare similarly place emphasis on the role of weapons in complex social systems (Bousquet 2008; DeLanda 1997; De Landa 1991; McNeill 1984). Third, many scholars make critical arguments deconstructing classical images and understandings of weapons in order to include new forms of weapons in the purview of security studies. These theories require addressing how weapons emerge in broader context of gender, race, coloniality, and power, but rarely theorise material dimension of weaponry (Rappert 2013; Santana 2009; Booth 2008; Verwimp 2006; Diken and Laustsen 2005). In each case, critical interpretations of weaponry tend to focus on specific histories, legacies, or weapon systems rather than analysing the potential powers of weapons as a particular class of object. In addition, an emerging body of literature focuses critical attention on the technical and social aspects of targeting as constitutive of weapons use (Dill 2015; Nordin and Öberg 2015; Holmqvist 2013; Grayson 2012; Weber 2005). This literature directly comments on the relationship between weapons and the intelligibility of violence and often shows how weapon devices produce phenomenological frames that legitimate war and trauma. However, critiques of weapon targeting often view weapons as passive structuring agents rather than active contributors to the formation of human experience. As such, this article explores a variety of features of weaponry, which implicate practices of targeting, and also complicate the relationship between humans and weapons in broader contexts. In addition, a number of important materialist analyses of weapons have emerged focusing on the agentic capacities of weapons in nuclear politics, militarisation, and warfare more generally (Bourne 2016, 2012; Cooper and Mutimer 2011). These approaches are largely consonant with the theory advanced in this article. However, unlike this piece, they do not place theoretical emphasis on the relationship between weapons and the production of desire. This emphasis on materialism and desire offers three important contributions to the field of critical security studies. First, focusing on desire adds depth to existing explanations of the genesis of attachment to weaponry at both social and somatic levels. Hence, this article provides an alternative account of how specific procedures of targeting, particular practices of warfare, or even certain classes of weapons become dominant. In contrast to a materialist approach centred exclusively on technical or social side, the focus on desire opens up both aspects as mutually constitutive of security politics. Second, the article concentrates on the affective potential of weapons. Unlike ANT and other materialist theories, this approach places less emphasis on the network or series of relations that define an object in favour of highlighting how weapons operate on the basis of affective potentials. By reading affective encounters back into classic literature on war, the article foregrounds how material agencies have been explicitly left out of security studies literature and demonstrates the impact for contemporary theories of war. Finally, the article extends materialist analysis to the example of drone warfare and shows how even critical readings of weaponry rely on dominant models or images of weaponry. In specific, this piece challenges the wide-spread belief that remote warfare constitutes a novel development in weapon autonomy rather than a reproduction of many classical dynamics of weaponisation. In this way, the article makes a contribution by countering the claim that drones represent an unprecedented form of war.

Section 2. Weapon desire

This section develops a theory of weapon agency based in the work of JFC Fuller, Gilles Deleuze and Felix Guattari, and a number of other contemporary theorists of violence. It suggests that <mark>weapons are defined by distinctive affective potentials, which exert formative influence on social life rather than passive instruments of violence</mark>. The section uses this theory to demonstrate that <mark>weapons do not merely impact human calculations or facilitate political violence</mark>, <mark>but actively contribute to the production of subjectivity and,</mark> as a consequence, <mark>security and warfare</mark>. Drawing together Fuller’s understanding of war instruments with Deleuze and Guattari’s appreciation for the independence of objects provides an important avenue for interpreting the affective implications of weaponry as a distinct form of object. Indeed, as JFC Fuller puts it nearly a century ago: ‘From the javelin and the arrow to the super-fortress and the rocket-bomb, the very power to destroy… has intoxicated man’ (Fuller 1998, x). Fuller adds a post-human twist: ‘We are entranced by their cunning, their power and precision, also by the things they give us and the profits we reap by means of them. Yet, how they influence us, as living creatures, is left uncalculated’ (x–xi). Fuller’s description notes the various qualities of weapons such as sublimity, cunning, and power, which draw humans to weapons. Set next to this complex account of the influences of weapons on ‘living creatures’, Fuller’s definition of the weapon as ‘an instrument with striking power’ seems reductive (16). However, this definition of weaponry offers a valuable starting point to theorise the capacity of weapons to incite desire.
