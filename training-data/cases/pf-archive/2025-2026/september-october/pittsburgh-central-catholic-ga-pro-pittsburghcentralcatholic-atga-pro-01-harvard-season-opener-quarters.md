The EU and UK are lagging behind of competitors on emerging technologies.
Quattrucci and Arcila ’25 [Leonardo Quattrucci* Beatriz Botero Arcila**, *Adjunct Professor at The Paris School of International Affairs at Sciences Po and Master of Public Policy from the University of Oxford **Assistant Professor of law in the digital information economy at Sciences Po Paris and Associate at the Center for Internet & Society at Harvard University, Blavatnik School of Government Voices at the University of Oxford, "Europe has a competitiveness problem. The <mark>E</mark>uropean <mark>U</mark>nion <mark>lags in</mark> GDP and <mark>productivity</mark> growth compared to the United States, and its population is aging faster. The Draghi report on EU competitiveness showed that this gap <mark>resulted from</mark> the EU’s <mark>inability to take</mark> sufficient <mark>advantage of</mark> digital <mark>tech</mark>nologies from the Internet onwards.
 Today, the EU has virtually <mark>no hyperscalers</mark>; it does not lead on advanced Artificial Intelligence (<mark>AI</mark>) or the technologies that power it, such as <mark>cloud computing</mark>. Much of the policy debate has thus focused on how to own the industries of the future. Yet, an essential issue tends to be overlooked: diffusion capacity.
 Political scientist Jeffrey Ding has shown that what matters most for economic success in technological revolutions is not necessarily inventing new transformative technology—such as electricity or the internet—but being better and faster at adopting these inventions in all sorts of sectors. This is called “technological diffusion capacity,” the ability to drive the adoption of new technology at scale within and across the economy and society.
 Better diffusion capacity prompted the UK's success in the steam engine and iron era because it educated more technicians than France and the Netherlands. In the early 20th century, the United States filed fewer patents than European countries but was better at integrating chemical engineering within industrial processes. In a few decades, American GDP per capita surpassed the UK’s and Germany’s.
 These findings echo others from the private sector: according to the OECD, the most productive firms today are those who are better at AI adoption. Technological success is not only about being first, but being best at adopting technology.
 The importance of diffusion capacity for innovation is good news for Europe. Even if the EU does not lead in key sectors, it can still fare well. Technological revolutions and the diffusion processes of key technologies take a couple of decades everywhere. The <mark>EU</mark> can leverage its high levels of human capital to succeed.
 Yet, diffusion capacity <mark>depends on state capacity</mark>. Today’s <mark>institutions are not delivering</mark> and reforming them will take time. <mark>How can Europe build</mark> diffusion capacity to boost <mark>competitiveness</mark> in the AI era? The recipe is nascent, but two elements are central and urgent: <mark>broadening</mark> the pool of human capital <mark>and re-building organisations</mark> where talent can thrive.

Conventional wisdom holds that while the United States tends to embrace <mark>innovation</mark>, the <mark>E</mark>uropean <mark>U</mark>nion often <mark>holds</mark> it <mark>at arm’s length</mark>.
 Recent events seem to support this: In September, the EU’s General Court <mark>ordered Apple to pay</mark> 13 billion euros in taxes to Ireland, then later that month the European Court of Justice imposed a 2.4 billion euros <mark>fine on Google</mark> for manipulating its shopping search results. Furthermore, new EU <mark>legislation</mark> – the Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act – <mark>reverses</mark> the <mark>burden of proof</mark> for certain technological companies. And the upcoming <mark>A</mark>rtificial <mark>I</mark>ntelligence <mark>Act</mark> also <mark>presents</mark> new <mark>regulatory challenges</mark> for innovation. It does indeed seem the EU has a penchant for regulating innovation.
 Then again, the EU likes to regulate everything, often seeking to squeeze new advancements into established regulatory frameworks. Fundamentally, there are two approaches to handling innovation: It can either be permitted or prohibited. While the U.S. has traditionally fostered an environment of permission, the EU appears committed to the prohibitive approach, in the nuanced form of regulation.
 Two approaches to innovation
 The permissive approach to innovation accepts that deploying new technologies inevitably creates challenges, but these uncertainties are embraced as opportunities for growth. This perspective supports the evolution of partial solutions, allowing diverse technological pathways to develop both in competition and in parallel. In fact, the approach thrives on the unpredictability inherent in innovation, viewing the future as undetermined and open to multiple possibilities. By acknowledging the open nature of innovation and the future, it encourages an organic coevolution of problems and solutions.
 Conversely, the prohibitive approach is rooted in a fixed vision of innovation and the future. It assumes that regulators or bureaucrats have sufficient foresight to predict outcomes and identify societal needs and technological advancement. This perspective seeks to manage and preempt all potential problems with a comprehensive set of current solutions, often addressing scenarios that have yet to unfold. It subscribes to the belief that the trajectory of innovation development can be thoroughly planned and managed, suppressing uncertainty and the possibility of multiple competitive developments.
 The EU’s regulatory strategies for Big Tech exemplify this forbidding approach. Initiatives like the Digital Markets Act illustrate the EU’s intent to dictate technology companies’ behavior, imposing extensive rules to forestall potential problems rather than letting these problems be resolved as they emerge. This philosophy assumes regulators clearly understand how technology will evolve – a deterministic view of the future whereby a central authority believes it knows the optimal path for technological development. With this approach, regulations enacted today are assumed to be capable of regulating all future states.
 The EU’s aversion to risk and innovation
 The EU’s stance on innovation reveals a preference for control over progress. Instead of harnessing the disruptive potential of new technologies, the EU is <mark>more concerned with</mark> regulating and <mark>restraining</mark> them, motivated by a bureaucratic focus on consumer protection and perceived fairness. This regulatory overreach can <mark>impede competition and stifle innovation</mark>, as the EU often imposes rigid frameworks that prioritize hedging against hypothetical risks over seizing concrete opportunities for advancement. Rather than cultivating a vibrant ecosystem conducive to experimentation, the EU promotes a culture of risk aversion with its emphasis on stability and uniformity across its diverse member states.
 Such an approach is particularly problematic for innovation. The future of technology is inherently unpredictable; while new technologies inevitably create unforeseen challenges, they also lead to innovative solutions that regulators cannot possibly anticipate. The EU’s prohibitive regulatory approach stems from the misguided assumption that there is a “correct” path of technological evolution – one that regulators can control and manage. This mindset is fundamentally at <mark>odds with innovation’s unpredictable</mark> and often chaotic <mark>nature</mark>.
