<mark>Stringent rules on fiscal policy have been hardwired into</mark> the policy architecture of the common currency area, <mark>the</mark> eurozone, which consists of twenty <mark>EU member</mark> economie<mark>s </mark>(see table below). The currency union has from the outset been a neoliberal political project intended to free up Europe’s markets for private business and finance while restricting space for public policy and regulation.
Its structures rest upon a hierarchy of policymaking actors, in which monetary policy by the supranational European Central Bank (ECB) serves only to maintain price stability. Member states are to pursue policies promoting employment and economic growth at the national level by means of deregulated, flexible markets (first and foremost, the labor market), while rigid rules strictly circumscribe the scope for states to use fiscal policy in support of those objectives.
Encoded in the SGP, <mark>these</mark> rules <mark>force </mark>eurozone<mark> governments to</mark> maintain balanced budgets (over the business cycle),<mark> limit a </mark>(temporary) <mark>budget deficit to a maximum of 3 percent of GDP</mark>, and restrict their country’s public-debt trajectory to a maximum of 60 percent of GDP. The avowed purpose of these fiscal rules is to avoid potentially unsustainable public-debt trajectories that would result from “irresponsible” fiscal policy at the national level, because of their implications for the financial and monetary stability of the eurozone as a whole.
In reality, these one-size-fits-all rules have not prevented such unsustainable trajectories from developing, as illustrated by the cases of France, Spain, and Belgium. But they have ingrained austerity into the hearts and minds of policymakers in economies as diverse as Italy and Germany and led to grindingly slow growth, crumbling public infrastructure, and weakened social protection. Due to the SGP, the eurozone has become trapped in a deliberately depoliticized austerity mode and a corresponding democratic deficit.
The rigid fiscal rules became untenable in the wake of the COVID-19 pandemic and the subsequent energy crisis, as public spending was essential to navigate the recession caused by the breakdown of global supply chains, the lockdowns, and the Ukraine war. In effect, the SGP was suspended from 2020 until the end of 2023.
The pact was reinstated in April 2024, after a modest reform of the rules that offered eurozone countries some flexibility and respite to deal with “exceptional” emergencies in the form of “escape clauses.” Under these (general or national) escape clauses, eurozone countries subject to the excessive deficit procedure (EDP) may obtain permission to deviate temporarily from the standard fiscal rules and agreed-upon medium-term fiscal adjustment plans, in case of a severe economic downturn in the EU and/or a negative, temporary exogenous shock.

The aftermath of the 2008 economic crash kick-started a wave of austerity measures in Europe, as member states bore the brunt of the financial crisis and interventions to save the banks. In 2010, Greece was forced to implement harsh austerity measures. It started a movement of resistance that would lead to a tough standoff between the Syriza-led government and the <mark>European</mark> institutions in 2015.
Several other European countries, including Spain, Portugal, Ireland, and Italy, also implemented <mark>austerity</mark> programs. This turned out to be calamitous. This impacted availability as well as efficiency: longer waiting times for surgeries, overpopulated classes in school, and reduced access to social aid and benefits.
These austerity measures did not only cause social dramas, but also worsened and lengthened the crisis in Europe. The continent’s recovery after the 2008 financial crisis was a lot slower than it was in the United States. When the COVID-19 pandemic hit Europe, the <mark>under</mark>investment in health care services was exposed for all to see. Keir Starmer is not a man for visionary speeches. It is one of the criticisms most often levelled against him. His characteristic mode is to identify a problem, and then take small steps to resolve it. And Europhiles are suspicious of the man who championed Remain yet now insists there will be no return to EU membership. But <mark>quietly, things are changing</mark>. Boring regulations As always, much of the work of the EU is concerned with a constant evolution of detailed regulation. Every one of those regulations matters to someone: a group of businesses or citizens who get new rights, protections or burdens. But they are mostly highly technical, and difficult for most journalists to follow. So, they remain below the horizon even for those with a real interest in UK EU relations. This is why the work of the Britain in a Changing Europe thinktank (UKICE) is so important. Ever since Brexit they have been tracking every change in regulations on both sides, monitoring where we are pulling apart and where we remain in lockstep, and reporting the results. They have just published their first report on this since the change of government, and for Europhiles it has some encouraging messages. A change of direction This is their summary of the position: “In lots of small ways, the direction has been to close the gaps. There are already signs of a markedly changed approach to managing divergence. <mark>The pursuit of active divergence</mark> (where the UK moves away from the EU rulebook) <mark>has</mark> all but <mark>dried up</mark>, with just one case of active divergence in this tracker having been first initiated by the Labour government. Instead, <mark>the new government’s focus has been</mark> on delaying existing divergence (five cases) and laying the groundwork for <mark>active alignment with EU rules</mark> (two cases).” Some of the changes in the chart were made under the previous government, and even where there is apparent divergence, there are opportunities for alignment. UKICE highlights the UK’s Product Regulation and Metrology (PRM) Bill, now in the House of Lords, which allows the government to replicate EU regulations relating to the environmental impacts of products. The Bill’s explicit purpose is to avoid divergence which could create new costs for business, lead to UK standards ‘falling behind’ the EU’s, or negatively affect Northern Ireland’s place in the UK’s own internal market. Already, this principle is being adopted, with a proposal to replicate EU rules making USB-C chargers mandatory for electronic devices (something which will be welcomed by anyone with a drawer full of electronic devices and cables). Similarly, the UK’s Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, passed under the last government, can be used to maintain alignment. It gives the UK regulator powers to impose ‘conduct requirements’ on companies, in order to protect competition in digital markets. And the UK’s Online Safety Act has similar objectives to the EU’s Digital Services Act, to protect users from harm online. In other areas, plans for divergence of recycling labelling have been suspended, and ‘not for EU’ labels on meat and dairy products have been abandoned. Divergence remains But there are still issues. The EU is unhappy that four years after Brexit, the UK has still not implemented the border control regime for EU imports and customs controls on parcels. This is because facilities are not yet in place, and because of the inflationary impact they would have. But this appears to be hampering discussions on other issues like the UK’s hope of a veterinary agreement. There are also areas of ‘passive divergence’, where EU regulations have changed since Brexit. They include regulations on vehicles and chemicals, and monitoring of airline CO2 emissions. But many of these could be resolved using powers under the Product Regulation and Metrology Bill. And the passive divergence mainly affects the Northern Ireland Windsor Framework, where it seems that the creation of the new-formed Democratic Scrutiny Committee has, so far, avoided conflict. Growing closer The Europhiles who mistrust Starmer suspect that his claims about a closer relationship with the EU are merely hot air, designed to pacify the ‘red wall’. This report suggests that they are wrong. These are small steps, but they are steps to bring us closer. They are a far cry from a return to membership of the EU, or even to the customs union. And it is much less than recent polling in the UK and EU suggests people want. But this report is clear, we have changed direction. There is a long way to go, but for many of our readers, we are at least pointing in the right direction.
