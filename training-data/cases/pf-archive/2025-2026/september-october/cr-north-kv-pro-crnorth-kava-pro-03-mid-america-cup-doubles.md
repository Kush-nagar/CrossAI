Conclusions This study analysed the impact of Brexit on the UK’s foreign direct investment utilizing diverse empirical techniques like the synthetic control method, difference-in-differences, causal mediation analysis, and PARDL modelling. The comprehensive examination of a large sample of countries over 2013–2023 provided critical insights into Brexit’s repercussions on the UK’s capability to attract FDI. The key findings reveal that the UK’s decision to exit the EU has dampened the country’s appeal as an investment destination, leading to reduced FDI inflows. The empirical evidence demonstrates that the level of FDI attracted by the UK post-Brexit is significantly lower than the estimated values had the country remained within the EU. Specifically, the DID approach substantiates the negative influence of Brexit on the UK’s inward FDI flows. Moreover, the synthetic control method indicates that the UK attracted lower FDI after Brexit. These results validate preceding forecasts about Brexit’s unfavourable impact on FDI and establish Brexit as a political instability factor obstructing FDI flows to UK, at least in the short term. Additionally, the findings highlight nuances in the relationship between political stability in source countries and their FDI outflows to the UK. A negative association is observed between short-term stability and the UK’s attracted FDI, suggesting source countries’ preference for investing during periods of temporary stability rather than enduring stability. However, the economic impact of Brexit-induced political uncertainty on sample countries remains minimal. Overall, the study makes important contributions by substantiating Brexit’s adverse effects on the UK’s capability to attract foreign direct investment through robust empirical analyses. The research underscores the challenges posed by Brexit in retaining the UK’s long-established position as a foremost destination for FDI. The results align with and expand on existing theoretical perspectives about the determinants of foreign investment and the role of political stability. The findings underline significant policy implications for the UK government to redress the declines in incoming FDI. Strategic interventions like fostering trade partnerships, offering investment incentives, improving infrastructure, and promoting targeted sectors can reignite the UK’s investment appeal. Specifically, priority areas include upgrading trade agreements with major partners, providing tax incentives and subsidies for investors and key industries, increasing infrastructure spending, and designing sector-specific measures to boost competitiveness. This "pecking order" of trade adjustment indicates a shift in UK supply chains toward more geographically proximate and smaller trading partners within the EU. ## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
ABSENT: The case presents a series of independent economic harms (slow growth, low confidence, lost output, trade barriers, reduced FDI) but contains no pre-written impact calculus or framing mechanism that compares these impacts against each other or against likely Neg impacts (e.g., sovereignty costs, regulatory burden, immigration control). No card includes comparative language ("outweighs," "turns," "prerequisite") or a framing tag such as "Economic stability is a prerequisite for political sovereignty." A weighing card — e.g., an OECD or IMF study tagline "Long-run GDP per capita growth is the strongest predictor of fiscal capacity for public services, outweighing short-term regulatory autonomy" (OECD 2024) — placed as a Contention 0 or final card of the case would supply the missing embedded weighing.

### Offensive spikes
**Atkinson 23** — "Brexit Is Costing the UK £100 Billion a Year in Lost Output"  
This card functions as an offensive spike by directly rebutting the most common Neg claim that Brexit has created economic opportunities. The Bloomberg analysis quantifies the opportunity cost (£100B/year, 4% smaller GDP) and explicitly "chips away at Prime Minister Rishi Sunak's assertion that Brexit is a 'huge opportunity,'" preempting the Neg's growth-from-freedoms narrative with counterfactual G7-comparison data.

### Defensive spikes
ABSENT: The case offers no frontlines embedded in the constructive that answer specific Neg responses such as "rejoining requires adopting the euro," "rejoining means accepting free movement," "transition costs of regulatory realignment," or "EU budget contributions." No card is tagged to preempt these (e.g., "UK could rejoin single market without euro adoption — European Commission 2023" or "Transition costs of rejoining are one-time vs. permanent Brexit losses — Institute for Government 2024"). A defensive spike card belongs after the Atkinson/Chu trade evidence to insulate the solvency mechanism.

### Evidence quality
**Atkinson 23** — Bloomberg Economics (Andrade & Hanson), 2023  
High-quality evidence: authored by named PhD economists at a premier financial-data institution, uses a transparent counterfactual methodology against G7 peers, cites specific datasets (business investment at 9% vs. 13% G7 avg., 370k missing EU workers), and is recent enough to reflect post-pandemic normalization. The card can win an evidence-comparison tiebreak against generic "Brexit opportunities" op-eds or government press releases.

### Collapse flexibility
**Chu 25** — BBC Verify, "Five key impacts of Brexit five years on"  
This single card supplies three distinct, non-redundant link chains: (1) goods trade reduction via non-tariff barriers (6–30% export loss, OBR 15% long-run assumption), (2) services export resilience (shows the case doesn't overclaim), and (3) new trade deals only offset ~14% of EU trade loss. Combined with **Simionescu 24** (FDI decline via DID & synthetic control) and **Atkinson 23** (investment & labor supply), the Pro can collapse to trade *or* investment *or* labor supply *or* aggregate GDP without relying on one link story.

### Warrant depth
**Simionescu 24** — "Navigating uncertainty: the effects of Brexit on foreign direct investment in the United Kingdom"  
Every tagline in this card is backed by an explicit econometric warrant: the DID methodology shows "negative impact of Brexit on the UK's inward FDI flows"; the synthetic control method shows "UK attracted lower FDI after Brexit" vs. counterfactual; causal mediation analysis quantifies political stability's indirect effect (0.0053 probability points via FDI); PARDL modelling confirms GDP and political stability growth reduce FDI. No claim appears without a stated statistical mechanism.

### Internal consistency
The case is internally consistent. All five cards (MK 25, Irene 25, Atkinson 23, Chu 25, Simionescu 24) converge on a unified narrative: Brexit has imposed persistent, structural economic costs (output gap, investment shortfall, labor scarcity, trade friction, FDI decline) that are ongoing in 2025 and worsening under current policy uncertainty. No contention undercuts another; the framing uniformly treats Brexit as the independent variable causing multi-channel economic harm, and rejoining as the reversal of that variable.
