All that follows is of necessity speculative because Reform’s declared intentions are unformed and unclear, but let’s try. Purging the professionals The first move, it is a fair bet, is a Doge-style <mark>purge</mark> of <mark>government departments</mark>, because this is what Reform has already attempted in local authorities where it has taken control. The argument we hear is that civil servants are all woke lefties who do little work anyway so we can live with fewer of them. Given the current Reform MPs have zero experience in national office, this has every likelihood of misfiring. Civil servants are there, Nigel, to allow ministers to implement policy. Expect a degree of impotence across government departments as the first consequence of a Reform administration. Attempting to ship all migrants to Rwanda/Albania/Xanadu requires detailed and difficult negotiations with those countries. Who do you think carries out those talks, Nigel? It isn’t the ministers. The second, probably parallel, development will be severe downward pressure on the value of the pound. <mark>Investors hate</mark> any <mark>uncertainty</mark>, <mark>and</mark> the apparent emergence of an <mark>impotent Government will only add to this</mark>. Inward investment into the UK, including purchase of government debt, will largely cease. The economic consequences are catastrophic. A collapsing pound <mark>puts pressure on inflation</mark>, only partly because imported goods cost more, <mark>and</mark> on <mark>interest rates</mark>. The Bank of England’s job is to raise the base rate to attempt to counter rising inflation. Except… The Bank was made independent in 1997 to prevent politicians trying to influence voters by manipulating rates, such as cutting them to spark an economic boom just ahead of an election. However Tice has indicated Reform might do just that. This is dangerous territory. Already spooked markets would react even worse to any suggestion that the UK economy was being manipulated for political reasons. (This is pretty much what happened, by the way, after the Truss/Kwarteng Budget.) Crisis by design So here is the scenario. Inflation is running at 15 to 20% – it has been there before in living memory and was heading there after that Truss/Kwarteng debacle. Meanwhile Reform is pulling the levers at the Bank to keep rates at, for the sake of argument, a comfortable 3%. Why should the banks lend money, on mortgages, for example? The housing market would collapse, because no one could move house. Soaring inflation <mark>devalues the worth of</mark> existing <mark>housing</mark> stock, while new building would pretty much cease, because why put up homes if no one can buy them? All this is speculative. The next effect, on pensions, is less so. There are, you will know, two types of pension: final salary – or defined benefit – and defined contribution. Final salary pensions are index-linked and go up in line with inflation – except that a sustained bout of high inflation is probably unaffordable to those funds administering them. They either fail to raise payments, making pensioners poorer in real terms, or risk going bust. Defined contribution pensions, now more common, allow someone retiring to take on board a “pension pot”, a fixed sum of money, and invest it accordingly to pay for their old age. Soaring inflation <mark>diminishes the value of</mark> those pension pots and makes those pensioners poorer as well. So that’s <mark>pensions</mark> sorted. Broken Britain, again How about the economy as a whole? Well, while higher inflation makes exporting goods easier, imported food and other goods are much dearer. <mark>This</mark>, as well as that pressure on pensions, <mark>means a cost-of-living crisis</mark> way worse than the one we have just suffered through. We will all have to grit our teeth and buy food, though more will certainly go hungry. UK-focused business providing non-essential goods, though, will lose out. Pubs and restaurants will suffer as people cut back, as will those supplying them, along with retailers selling, for example, clothes and electrical goods. And the housing market has dried up. All those estate agents and solicitors hit, plus those retailers supplying goods to new home buyers – moving house is a significant trigger to consumer spending. The ripples will flow through the jobs market, as those affected firms lay off staff. Those all start to draw benefits, and go onto the public payroll – or will Reform just say there are no more benefits? The gravy train has come off the rails, suckers. Find work or starve.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** No card in the case includes pre-written comparative weighing language (magnitude, probability, timeframe, or prerequisite framing) that extends cleanly into the back half. The economy contention leads with a "soft landing" claim but never tags *why* that outweighs the affirmative’s likely growth-from-reentry narrative. A filling card would carry a tag like “Economic stability is a prerequisite to any growth — uncertainty destroys investment before policy changes take effect” (Graziano 2018 / Du 2025) and sit at the bottom of Contention 1.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case contains zero cards that proactively answer the affirmative’s core offense — e.g., “rejoining reverses Brexit losses,” “single-market access boosts services exports,” or “dynamic gains from regulatory alignment.” An offensive spike would be a card tagged “Rejoining cannot recover services losses — sunk-cost exit and regulatory divergence are irreversible” (Du 2025 extension) placed as a terminal defense sub-point under Contention 1.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines preempt the specific responses the aff will run (e.g., “new veterinary agreement solves SPS barriers,” “youth mobility scheme solves labor shortages,” “Starmer’s reset already negotiating dynamic alignment”). A defensive spike would read “Veterinary agreement only covers agri-food — 80% of services barriers untouched” (Kellner 2025 / LSE 2018) and live as a pre-emptive block at the end of Contention 2.

### Evidence quality
**Reuters 2025 (BoE Taylor “soft landing”)** — Author is a central-bank policymaker speaking on the record; source is primary and September-2025 current. Dataset = real-time BoE monitoring. **Wins evidence-comparison tiebreaks** on recency and authority for the “economy is up now” claim.  
**Graziano 2018 (NBER w25334)** — Author is tenured professor at Nottingham; NBER working paper = rigorous peer-review pipeline. Dataset = firm-level trade flows across 27 EU + 3 PTA countries, 1990-2016. **Wins on methodology depth** for uncertainty-mechanism warrants.  
**Du 2025 (J Int Bus Policy)** — Author is Professor at Aston; journal is peer-reviewed. Dataset = Orbis firm-level panel (1.2M UK firms) + synthetic control. **Wins on granularity and causal identification** for services-export loss.  
**BoE 2021 (Quarterly Bulletin)** — Institutional source; dataset = macro-financial integration metrics (trade/GDP >60%, foreign assets 5×GDP). **Strong for structural openness claim**, but 2021 vintage slightly weakens recency tiebreak vs. 2025 aff cards.  
**Cooke 2025 / Swinburne 2025 / Kellner 2025** — All are journalist/op-ed pieces; author quals = political reporters, not primary decision-makers. **Lose evidence-comparison tiebreaks** against aff cards citing ministers, EU negotiators, or Treasury forecasts.

### Collapse flexibility
**Du 2025 (services exports -9.2% annually, USD 146.8B cumulative)** — Provides a *second*, quantifiable impact lane (services) distinct from the goods-trade uncertainty mechanism in Graziano 2018. Allows collapse to “services collapse outweighs” if aff wins goods-trade defense.  
**Graziano 2018 (doubling Brexit probability → exports -10 to -20 log points, participation -10%)** — Gives a *probability-weighted* impact that functions even if reentry negotiations are “soft”; aff must prove probability stays near zero.  
**BoE 2021 (openness → spillover vulnerability)** — Structural link letting the Con argue *any* global shock (Trump tariffs, COVID-II, energy crisis) hits UK harder *because* of negotiation uncertainty. Three independent link chains = genuine collapse flexibility.

### Warrant depth
**Graziano 2018** — Tag: “complex, uncertain, and drawn-out Brexit negotiations harmed the European economy, re-entry negotiations would do the same.” Warrant in card: model shows *anticipated* regime-shift probability reduces trade flows *before* policy changes (Table 11, PTA sample). **Full warrant present.**  
**Du 2025** — Tag: “Brexit uncertainty led to 9.2% annual reduction in UK services exports.” Warrant: synthetic control on Orbis panel isolates uncertainty channel; SMEs exit, MNEs relocate. **Full warrant present.**  
**Reuters 2025** — Tag: “UK economy getting closer to soft landing.” Warrant: BoE policymaker cites incoming data, but card quotes only *claim*, not the underlying data (wage growth, inflation persistence, productivity). **Thin warrant — claim + cite only.**  
**Cooke 2025 / Swinburne 2025 / Kellner 2025** — Tags about MP dissatisfaction / voter unpopularity. Warrants = anonymous MP conversations, columnist interpretation, polling advisers’ advice. **No primary-source warrants; all claim + cite.**

### Internal consistency
**Contention 1 claims “UK economy is up now” (Reuters 2025, Wise 2025) while simultaneously arguing “re-entry negotiations would crash the economy via uncertainty” (Graziano 2018, Du 2025).** The case never resolves this tension — it does not explain *why* a booming economy is *more* fragile to uncertainty, or why the BoE’s “fragile moment” quote applies to re-entry specifically.  
**Contention 2 claims Labour MPs are “on the brink of no-confidence” (Swinburne 2025) but also that “rejoining is incredibly unpopular amongst MPs” (Kellner 2025).** If MPs hate rejoining, they *won’t* topple Starmer for pursuing it — the political-threat logic inverts. No framing card reconciles these.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 4/10** — The case assembles high-quality academic evidence for its uncertainty mechanism (Graziano, Du) and achieves genuine collapse flexibility, but it lacks any embedded weighing, offensive/defensive spikes, or internal-consistency guardrails, and half its political cards are journalist opinion that will lose evidence tiebreaks.
