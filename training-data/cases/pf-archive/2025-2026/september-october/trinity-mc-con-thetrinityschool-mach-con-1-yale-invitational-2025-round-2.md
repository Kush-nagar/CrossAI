Executive Summary The sixth anniversary of the June 2016 vote to leave the EU prompted a wave of negative commentary about the economic costs of Brexit. Much of this commentary continues to be exaggerated and often just plain wrong. The hard evidence is that <mark>leaving the EU has had</mark> remarkably<mark> little impact</mark> on the UK economy. Among the major advanced economies, the UK has had one of the faster rates of growth of total GDP since June 2016 and sits comfortably in mid table on growth in GDP per head.<mark> UK exports to the EU have recovered to long-term trend levels and</mark> the City of London has been only mildly impacted. Since June 2016 and up until the end of the second quarter of 2022, OECD data shows that the cumulative growth rate of real GDP in Italy was 4%, in Germany was 5.5%, in the U.K. was 6.8% and in France was 7.6%. Claims that UK investment has been very weak since 2016, based on extrapolating from 2009-2016, are misleading as this period featured a strong rebound in investment from the global financial crisis. In fact, <mark>investment is still close to its long-run trend</mark>, and some of the gap can be explained by the collapse of investment in North Sea oil and gas. It has also been common for UK investment recoveries after recessions to be delayed. Foreign direct investment (FDI) into the UK has held up well since 2016 in contrast to predictions that it would slump after Brexit. Greenfield FDI into the UK rose by a third between 2016 and 2021 and was the highest of any large European economy in every year in this period. Far from collapsing as some claim, <mark>UK trade with the EU has fully recovered after some initial disruption</mark>, despite increased trade frictions. Underlying trade levels are close to long-term trends. The UK’s trade balance with the EU has improved – implying a boost to growth – and even sectors like food and fish have seen exports to the EU proving remarkably resilient. Claims that trade trends imply the UK economy will be 4% smaller by 2030 than it would otherwise have been due to Brexit are particularly misleading. This 4% prediction made by the OBR is an average of other studies. A key claim underlying the prediction is that there is a strong link between trade intensity and productivity. But the evidence for such a link for the UK is at best very weak and at worst non-existent.

The Department for Business and Trade (DBT), with other government departments, has negotiated the United Kingdom’s (UK) accession to the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP). This comprehensive agreement will support UK businesses by making it easier for them to trade with CPTPP. It will facilitate innovation and provide consumers with more choice. The agreement could generate long-term benefits for both the UK and CPTPP, support UK jobs and provide opportunities for growth in sectors across the UK. CPTPP is one of the largest free trade areas in the world. The current members of CPTPP are Australia, Brunei, Canada, Chile, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, New Zealand, Peru, Singapore and Vietnam. The CPTPP free trade area is populated by half a billion people with a joint gross domestic product (GDP) of £9 trillion in 2022.[footnote 1] <mark>The</mark> combined GDP of the 11 CPTPP members and the UK was around £12 trillion in 2022. This Free Trade Agreement (FTA) spans the Asia-Pacific and the Americas and includes some of the world’s largest current and future economies. <mark>CPTPP will</mark> also <mark>provide the UK </mark>with<mark> a stepping stone to trade with the wider</mark> strategically important<mark> Indo-Pacific.</mark> It is expansionary at its heart. CPTPP has also been seen as a pathway to a potential pan-Asia-Pacific FTA. CPTPP is a modern and comprehensive agreement which aims to enhance the existing trading and investment relationship between the UK and CPTPP members. ## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case lacks pre-written impact calculus comparing the magnitude, probability, or timeframe of its three disadvantage scenarios (lost trade deals, Euro adoption, SME destruction). No card explicitly weighs "UK GDP loss from leaving CPTPP" against "EU regulatory burden" or establishes a hierarchy of impacts. ### Offensive spikes
Helm 2024 — "Government to Seek Global Trade Deals for UK at Expense of Formal EU Re-Entry" — Preempts the Aff's likely "political will/feasibility" contention by citing the sitting Labour government's explicit refusal to pursue EU re-entry, forcing Aff to defend a counterfactual policy the UK Prime Minister has already rejected.

BoB 2023 — "Ten Questions for Rejoiners" — Spikes the Aff's "single market access solves econ" narrative by preemptively listing nine rejoiner burdens (Euro adoption, budget payments, free movement, Indo-Pacific trade abandonment) that Aff must answer before claiming net benefit.

### Defensive spikes
Gudgin 2022 — "What impact is Brexit having on the UK economy?" — Frontlines the core Aff link ("Brexit hurt UK economy") with OECD GDP data showing UK outperformed Germany/Italy/France since 2016, FDI rising, and trade recovered to trend, making Aff's "rejoin to fix Brexit damage" link non-unique.

UK Government 2024 — "Impact Assessment of the UK's Accession to the CPTPP" — Preempts Aff "CPTPP is minor/insignificant" responses with £4.9B bilateral trade increase, £2.0B UK GDP gain, 19/23 sectors expanding, and £14T potential expanded CPTPP GDP, raising the opportunity cost of EU re-entry beyond Aff's likely impact claims.

Gudgin 2022 — Author is Cambridge Centre for Business Research associate + Oxford Economics senior advisor; uses OECD quarterly data through Q2 2022; comprehensive multi-indicator analysis (GDP, trade, investment, FDI, productivity). Wins evidence comparison on Brexit econ impact vs. typical Aff OBR/think-tank projections.

### Collapse flexibility
The case offers three independent disadvantage pillars — trade deal loss (CPTPP/DCTS), mandatory Euro adoption, and EU regulatory burden on SMEs — each with distinct links and impact chains. The 2NR can collapse to any single pillar (e.g., "Euro adoption alone loses £1,000/family/year + prevents counter-cyclical policy" per BoB 2023 + Coccia 2018) without relying on the others. DCTS/CPTPP evidence also provides expanding-indo-pacific-growth uniqueness that doesn't require the Euro or SME arguments to function.

Gudgin 2022 — Taglines ("UK exports to EU recovered to long-term trend," "investment close to long-run trend," "FDI rose by a third") are each backed by specific OECD/ONS data points in the card text (cumulative GDP growth percentages, Greenfield FDI 2016-2021 figures, trade balance improvement).
