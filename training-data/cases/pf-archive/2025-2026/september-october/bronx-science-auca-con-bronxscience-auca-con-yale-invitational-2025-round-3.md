How will states use such a newfound advantage? Technology rarely fundamentally changes the nature or objectives of states. More often, states use technology to advance preexisting geopolitical aims. Moreover, enhanced power can result in greater ambition. Given the geopolitical landscape deof the international system will behave differently with new military technologies than will revisionist powers, such as Russia and China. The spread of <mark>new techn</mark>ology to the United States and its Allies and partners <mark>would </mark>likely serve, on balance, to <mark>reinforce</mark> the existing sources of <mark>stability</mark> in the prevailing international system. At the end of the Cold War, the United States and its Allies and partners achieved a technological military advantage over its great power rivals, with the US using its unipolar position to deepen and expand a rules-based system. They also employed their military dominance to counter perceived threats from rogue states and terrorist networks. The United States, its Allies, and partners did not, however, engage in military aggression against great power, nuclear-armed rivals or their allies. In the future, these status quo powers are apt to use military advantages to reinforce their position in the international system and to deter attacks against Allies and partners in Europe and the Indo-Pacific. These states might also employ military power to deal with threats posed by terrorist networks or by regional revisionist powers such as Iran and North Korea. But it is extremely difficult to imagine scenarios in which Washington or its Allies or partners would use newfound military advantages provided by emerging technology to conduct an armed attack against Russia or China. Similarly, Moscow and <mark>Beijing would</mark> likely<mark> use any newfound </mark>military<mark> strength to advance</mark> their preexisting <mark>geopolitical aim</mark>s. Given their very different positions in the international system, however, these states are likely to employ new military technologies in ways that are destabilizing. These states have made clear their dissatisfaction with the existing international system and their desire to revise it. Both countries have ongoing border disputes with multiple neighboring countries. If Moscow developed new military technologies and operational concepts that shifted the balance of power in its favor, it would likely use this advantage to pursue revisionist aims. If Moscow acquired a newfound ability to more easily invade and occupy territory in Eastern Europe, for example (or if Putin believed Russia had such a capability), it is more likely Russia would be tempted to engage in aggression. Likewise,<mark> if China acquired an enhanced ability through new tech</mark>nology to invade and occupy Taiwan or contested islands in the East or South China Seas, Beijing’s leaders might also find this opportunity tempting. If new technology enhances either power’s anti-access, area-denial network, then <mark>it</mark>s leaders <mark>may be more confident in their ability to achieve a </mark>fait accompli<mark> attack against a neighbor and then block a US-led liberation</mark>. These are precisely the types of <mark>shifts in the balance of power</mark> that can <mark>lead to war</mark>. As mentioned previously, the predominant scholarly theory on the causes of war—the bargaining model—maintains that imperfect information on the balance of power and the balance of resolve and credible commitment problems result in international conflict.52 New technology can exacerbate these causal mechanisms by increasing uncertainty about, or causing rapid shifts in, the balance of power. Indeed as noted above, new military technology and the development of new operational concepts have shifted the balance of power and resulted in military conflict throughout history. Some may argue emerging military technology is more likely to result in a new tech arms race than in conflict. This is possible. But Moscow and Beijing may come to believe (correctly or not) that new technology provides them a usable military advantage over the United States and its Allies and partners. In so doing, they may underestimate Washington. If Moscow or Beijing attacked a vulnerable US Ally or partner in their near abroad, therefore, there would be a risk of major war with the potential for nuclear escalation. The United States has formal treaty commitments with several frontline states as well as an ambiguous defense obligation to Taiwan. If Russia or China were to attack these states, it is likely, or at least possible, that the United States would come to the defense of the victims. While many question the wisdom or credibility of America’s global commitments, it would be difficult for the United States to simply back down. Abandoning a treaty ally could cause fears that America’s global commitments would unravel. Any US president, therefore, would feel great pressure to come to an Ally’s defense and expel Russian or Chinese forces. Once the United States and Russia or China are at war, <mark>there would be</mark> a risk of<mark> nuclear escalation</mark>. As noted previously, experts assess the greatest risk of nuclear war today does not come from a bolt-out-of-the-blue strike but from nuclear escalation in a regional, conventional conflict.53 Russian leaders may believe it is in their interest to use nuclear weapons early in a conflict with the United States and NATO.54 Russia possesses a large and diverse arsenal, including thousands of nonstrategic nuclear weapons, to support this nuclear strategy. In the 2018 Nuclear Posture Review, Washington indicates it could retaliate against any Russian nuclear “de-escalation” strikes with limited nuclear strikes of its own using low-yield nuclear weapons.55 The purpose of US strategy is to deter Russian strikes. If deterrence fails, however, there is a clear pathway to nuclear war between the United States and Russia. As Henry Kissinger pointed out decades ago, there is no guarantee that, once begun, a limited nuclear war stays limited.56 There are similar risks of nuclear escalation in the event of a US-China conflict. China has traditionally possessed a relaxed nuclear posture with a small “lean and effective” deterrent and a formal “no first use” policy. But China is relying more on its strategic forces. It is projected to double—if not triple or quadruple—the size of its nuclear arsenal in the coming decade.57 Chinese experts have acknowledged there is a narrow range of contingencies in which China might use nuclear weapons first.58 As in the case of Russia,the US Nuclear Posture Review recognizes the possibility of limited Chinese nuclear attacks and also holds out the potential of a limited US reprisal with low-yield nuclear weapons as a deterrent.59 If the nuclear threshold is breached in a conflict between the United States and China, the risk of nuclear exchange is real. In short, if a coming revolution in military affairs provides a real or perceived battlefield advantage for Russia or China, such a development raises the likelihood of armed aggression against US regional allies, major power war, and an increased risk of nuclear escalation.

Boring regulations As always, much of the work of the EU is concerned with a constant evolution of detailed regulation. Every one of those regulations matters to someone: a group of businesses or citizens who get new rights, protections or burdens. But they are mostly highly technical, and difficult for most journalists to follow. So, they remain below the horizon even for those with a real interest in UK EU relations. This is why the work of the Britain in a Changing Europe thinktank (UKICE) is so important. Ever since Brexit they have been tracking every change in regulations on both sides, monitoring where we are pulling apart and where we remain in lockstep, and reporting the results. They have just published their first report on this since the change of government, and for Europhiles it has some encouraging messages. A change of direction This is their summary of the position: “In lots of small ways, the direction has been to close the gaps. There are already signs of a markedly changed approach to managing divergence. The pursuit of active divergence (where the UK moves away from the EU rulebook) has all but dried up, with just one case of active divergence in this tracker having been first initiated by the Labour government. Instead, <mark>the new government’s focus has been</mark> on delaying existing divergence (five cases) and laying the groundwork for <mark>active alignment with EU rules</mark> (two cases).” Some of the changes in the chart were made under the previous government, and even where there is apparent divergence, there are opportunities for alignment. UKICE highlights the UK’s Product Regulation and Metrology (PRM) Bill, now in the House of Lords, which allows the government to replicate EU regulations relating to the environmental impacts of products. The Bill’s explicit purpose is to avoid divergence which could create new costs for business, lead to UK standards ‘falling behind’ the EU’s, or negatively affect Northern Ireland’s place in the UK’s own internal market. Already, this principle is being adopted, with a proposal to replicate EU rules making USB-C chargers mandatory for electronic devices (something which will be welcomed by anyone with a drawer full of electronic devices and cables). Similarly, the UK’s Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act, passed under the last government, can be used to maintain alignment. It gives the UK regulator powers to impose ‘conduct requirements’ on companies, in order to protect competition in digital markets. And the UK’s Online Safety Act has similar objectives to the EU’s Digital Services Act, to protect users from harm online. In other areas, plans for divergence of recycling labelling have been suspended, and ‘not for EU’ labels on meat and dairy products have been abandoned. Divergence remains But there are still issues. The EU is unhappy that four years after Brexit, the UK has still not implemented the border control regime for EU imports and customs controls on parcels. This is because facilities are not yet in place, and because of the inflationary impact they would have. But this appears to be hampering discussions on other issues like the UK’s hope of a veterinary agreement. There are also areas of ‘passive divergence’, where EU regulations have changed since Brexit. They include regulations on vehicles and chemicals, and monitoring of airline CO2 emissions. But many of these could be resolved using powers under the Product Regulation and Metrology Bill. And the passive divergence mainly affects the Northern Ireland Windsor Framework, where it seems that the creation of the new-formed Democratic Scrutiny Committee has, so far, avoided conflict. Growing closer The Europhiles who mistrust Starmer suspect that his claims about a closer relationship with the EU are merely hot air, designed to pacify the ‘red wall’. This report suggests that they are wrong. These are small steps, but they are steps to bring us closer. They are a far cry from a return to membership of the EU, or even to the customs union. And it is much less than recent polling in the UK and EU suggests people want. But this report is clear, we have changed direction. There is a long way to go, but for many of our readers, we are at least pointing in the right direction.
