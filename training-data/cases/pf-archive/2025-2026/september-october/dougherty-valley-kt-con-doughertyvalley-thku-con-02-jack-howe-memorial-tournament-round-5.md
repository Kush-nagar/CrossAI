According to HST[1], a single predominant power (i.e. hegemon) possessing greatest material (e.g. military and economic) resources in the world typically creates the
global economic system and seeks to stabilize it (Kindleberger, 1973). With its preponderant material resources, a hegemonic power exercises global leadership so
that other countries accept and follow the rules that it created for the world. Also, a hegemon provides international public goods – e.g. “the provision of open

market for the goods and services free from political interventions” so that market actors can compete with each other via the price mechanism rather than via the
proximity to political power (Saull, 2010, p. 6) – which are unlikely to exist unless it is willing to bear full costs of their provision. The stable global order that the
hegemon creates benefits all participants in the international system, although the gains of smaller or weaker states who bear none of the costs of public goods
provision are much greater than those of the predominant power (Snidal, 1985, p. 581). HST explains the so-called Pax Americana since the end of the second World
War. Undoubtedly, the USA has been the preponderant power in the postwar world. It has created a liberal international order reflecting its principle and interests
and maintained it by providing public goods such as international stability[2] and international currency (i.e. the provision of dollar as the international economic
system’s primary reserve currency for payment). Allowing secondary states to “free ride,”[3] the USA as the most powerful state in the world acted to maintain the
essential rules governing interstate relations, and it had a will to do so (Keohane and Nye, 1977, p. 44). According to Layne (2018, pp. 104-105), US
pre-eminence is a necessary condition to manage and stabilize the liberal international order and the US exercised global
leadership “by acting as a security provider and geopolitical stabilizer; by maintaining an open, liberal international economy; and
by promoting global cooperation through upholding and revising the post-1945 liberal order.” China’s rise and PTT US hegemony over the last seven decades or so
has recently been challenged by China’s rise. Since the economic reforms launched in December 1978, China has achieved a remarkable economic development.
China’s real-Gross Domestic Product (GDP) growth rate over the last four decades is about 10 percent on average (World Bank, 2018; also see Kim, 2016b, p. 707).
While the size of China’s economy (in terms of GDP) is currently the number two in the world only after the USA, China already became the world’s largest exporter
in 2010. In 2011, China ranked as the world’s first destination for inward foreign direct investment (FDI) and the world’s first investor for outward FDI among
developing countries[4]. Also, in 2013, China became the world’s largest trading country in goods, overtaking the USA (Anderlini and Hornby, 2014). Moreover,
measured on a purchasing power parity basis that adjusts for price differences, China ranked in 2017 as the world’s number one (Central Intelligence Agency, 2018).
Given that China’s size of GDP on a PPP basis in 1980 was one-tenth that of the USA, China’s economic growth is truly remarkable (International Monetary Fund,
2018). China’s rapid ascendance as an alternative hegemonic power poses a significant challenge to US hegemony in the world. As a
matter of fact, competing with the USA in many areas, China has quickly strengthened its economic, technological and military power and as a consequence has
started to threaten US global hegemony. Accordingly, many scholars now often talk about a declining US hegemony and a forthcoming Chinese hegemony (see,
for instance, Layne 2018, 2012). Some of them even predict[s] a[n hegemonic war between the USA and China during the
hegemonic transition period[--] (see, e.g., Liff and Ikenberry, 2014; Mearsheimer, 2014; Friedberg, 2005). Underlying these arguments for an
inevitable war between the two superpowers is PTT. PTT originally formulated by Organski (1958) posits that war is likely when the power of the
dominant state in the international system (i.e. hegemon) is declining and that a dissatisfied rising challenger substantially reduces the power gap between the
hegemon and itself. Unlike balance of power theory, PTT argues that the war is most likely when there is near power parity between a dominant state and a rising
and dissatisfied challenger (Organski and Kugler, 1980, pp. 19-20)[5]. A rising power here is generally dissatisfied with the existing international order and initiates
war against a declining hegemon in order to impose orders that are more favorable to itself (Organski 1958, pp. 364-367). Layne (2018, p. 110) put these power
transition dynamics quite succinctly as follows: “Over time, however, the relative power of states changes, and eventually the international order no longer reflects
the actual distribution of power between or among the leading Great Powers. When that happens, the legitimacy of the prevailing order is called into question, and
it will be challenged by the rising power(s).” And when the balance of power between a dominant state and a rising challenger changes sufficiently, a new order
replaces an old one typically by a hegemonic war (2018, p. 104). Paying close attention to the growing Sino–US competition over hegemony in the twenty-first
century, therefore, Shirk (2007, p. 4), China specialist, argues that “History teaches us that rising powers are likely to provoke war.” On the other hand,
scholars like Gilpin (1981) contend that the power transition war between great powers is likely to occur when a hegemonic state whose power is declining due to
imperial overstretch[6] views “preventive war as the most attractive means of eliminating the threat posed by challengers” (Ned Lebow and Valentino, 2009, p. 391),
although they do acknowledge that there might be some “ways to prolong the period of its power preponderance vis-à-vis the rising challenger, so that the rapidly
rising power will not dare to challenge the hegemonic leadership” (Kim and Gates, 2015, p. 221). In this case, the initiator of war is a declining hegemon, rather than
a rising challenger. The declining hegemon who fears a rising challenger’s overtaking its power in the near future sees war as a better
option than other options of maintaining its hegemony such as reducing its commitments abroad and appeasing a rising challenger.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** No card or argument in the case explicitly weighs the nuclear-extinction impact against other potential impacts (e.g., economic, soft-power) or instructs the judge on how to prioritize magnitude vs. probability vs. timeframe. A card such as “Sagan 1983 – Nuclear winter causes human extinction; extinction outweighs all other impacts on magnitude and irreversibility” placed after the Dowd/Sarg cards would create extendable weighing for the back half.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case contains no cards that proactively answer likely Pro positions (e.g., “Rejoining EU restores single-market access → economic growth,” “EU regulatory alignment prevents trade friction,” “UK rejoining strengthens EU as a geopolitical counterweight to China”). An offensive spike would look like a card tagged “EU AI Act creates regulatory fragmentation that slows UK-US interoperability — Parsons 2025” placed in Contention 1 to undercut the Pro’s regulatory-harmony narrative before they read it.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** There are no embedded frontlines answering the specific responses Con will face: e.g., “UK can align with EU AI Act voluntarily without rejoining,” “UK-EU trade deal already covers data adequacy,” “US-UK AI partnership is independent of UK-EU status.” A defensive spike would be a card such as “UK can mirror EU AI rules unilaterally — House of Lords 2024” inserted after the Robson 25 card to preempt the “regulatory divergence” rebuttal.

### Evidence quality
The case cites a mix of high- and low-quality sources. **Singman 4-7-2025 (Fox News)** quotes a Trump White House official — partisan, primary-source advocacy, not peer-reviewed. **Parsons 7-5-2025 (Sunday Times op-ed)** is a tech-CEO commentary, not an empirical study. **GOV 25 (UK government press release)** is self-interested policy marketing. **Robson 25 (CCN.com)** is a crypto/tech news site with no clear peer review. **Aspen 25 (Aspen Digital report)** is a credible think-tank convening summary, but the excerpt is descriptive, not causal. **Meacham 23 (Harvard International Review student article)** is an undergraduate publication. **Kim 19 (Emerald/ITPD journal article)** is a peer-reviewed IR piece — the strongest source in the doc. **Dowd 15 (Providence magazine)** and **Sarg 15 (Foreign Policy Journal)** are opinion/advocacy outlets with no nuclear-physics credential for Sarg. **Conclusion:** Evidence would likely lose a head-to-head comparison against Pro cards from peer-reviewed journals, major IR think tanks (CSIS, Chatham House), or government intelligence assessments.

### Collapse flexibility
The case is **pinned to a single link chain**: UK rejoins EU → EU AI Act applies → UK regulatory burden increases → UK AI innovation slows → UK-US tech partnership weakens → US loses AI race to China → hegemonic transition → great-power war → nuclear extinction. No alternative impact scenarios (e.g., economic stagnation, loss of scientific talent, weakened NATO cohesion) and no secondary links (e.g., EU veto over UK foreign policy, budget contributions) are developed. A flexible case would add a Contention 2 tagged “Economic Competitiveness” with cards on EU budget drag / single-market regulatory costs, and a Contention 3 tagged “Diplomatic Autonomy” with cards on EU foreign-policy constraints.

### Warrant depth
Most taglines receive **claim + cite only**, not a causal warrant. Examples:
- “US is winning the AI race” → Singman 25 quotes Kratsios asserting dominance; no data on compute, publications, talent flow, or commercialization.
- “UK investment higher than it’s been in years” → GOV 25 cites aggregate pounds; no warrant that marginal £ translates to frontier-model competitiveness.
- “Brexit freed UK from EU AI Act” → Robson 25 describes regulatory difference; no warrant that lighter touch *causes* faster innovation.
- “Without UK the US will lose” → Aspen 25 says UK “uniquely excels in convening authority and talent”; no warrant that rejoining EU degrades those assets or that US cannot compensate.
- “GPW would turn nuclear” → Dowd 15 asserts likelihood; no warrant from deterrence theory or historical crisis data.
- “Extinction” → Sarg 15 speculates on atmospheric loss via “antigravitational effect” — fringe physics, no mainstream scientific consensus.

### Internal consistency
**Contradiction present:** The case simultaneously argues (1) **US is already dominant in AI** (Singman 25: “United States remains the world's dominant power in AI”) and (2) **US will lose the AI race without UK partnership** (Aspen 25: “Without the UK the US will lose — that's devastating”). If the US is already dominant, the marginal contribution of UK talent/convening authority is unlikely to be decisive; if US victory is contingent on UK, the “dominant” claim is overstated. The two cards undercut each other’s credibility and give Pro a clean cross-fire concession.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 3/10** — The case assembles a single, highly speculative extinction chain with mostly low-quality sources, no embedded weighing or spikes, a self-contradictory link story, and virtually no warrants — leaving it strategically brittle and evidentiarily weak.
