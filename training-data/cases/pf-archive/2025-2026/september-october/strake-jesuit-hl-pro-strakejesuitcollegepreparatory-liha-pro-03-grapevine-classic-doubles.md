Introduction
Recalling the thoughts of Jean Monnet and the main ideas of the Schuman Plan, the long-term target of the Union was to bring European states closer by entwining their economies and industries. It is not without irony that the complexity of the negations around Brexit proves how successful and far-going European integration has come already. When in June 2016, the people of the United Kingdom (UK) were asked in a referendum whether to remain or leave the European Union (EU), the majority (51.9%) – with a very small margin – voted in favor to leave the Union. Followed by complex negotiations and massive political turbulences, the UK formally left the EU in February 2020. However, after four years of Brexit negotiations and uncertainty, a phase of intense trading negotiations started to draw up the future relationship between the EU and UK until the end of the transition phase to find a final compromise for future cooperation and trade relations. Regardless of the outcome, the departure of Britain triggers severe changes and disturbances on both sides of the Channel. It will open a new chapter of cross-channel relations not only with long-lasting political and economic consequences but also for the environment and future environmental policy-making in Europe. Doubtless, the departure of Britain will change the face of the United Kingdom but it will also leave its traces on the European Union, causing distortions in the short but also in the long term.1
The <mark>EU</mark> is <mark>known for</mark> its <mark>leadership</mark> role <mark>in global environment</mark>al protection <mark>efforts</mark>, acknowledging that climate change and environmental degradation pose a real threat to human mankind and also urging to decarbonize production and consumption. In various areas, the EU came forward with some environmental policies that did not exist before or at least not in such a huge region (e.g., acid rain, the ozone layer, climate change, hazardous chemicals...). Over the past four decades, the EU developed and implemented major environmental concepts and principles that find application in environmental policymaking around the world (i.e., precautionary principle, burden sharing, waste hierarchy, proximity principle for treatment of waste, etc.). The Union has one of the most mature and coherent environmental frameworks in the world. Policy-making within the Union also helps member states to share best practices. In the phase of policy formation, Member States can learn from each other's challenges and policy responses. This process allows opting for the best-practice policy to be implemented. In the past, the realm of environmental policy-making served as a driver for advancing integration, as Member States were more willing to hand over policy environment-related competences to the EU than in other areas. European environmental policy-making is not only more long-term oriented than those of national governments but also allows large-scaled efforts. Furthermore, it is more resistant to failures.2 A major source of the EU’s international influence derives from its market and regulatory power based on common environmental standards.3 However, participation in the Union is based on compromises, and with the departure of a very powerful entity, the EU will be influenced in several aspects. Britain´s departure is expected to trigger serious changes to several realms of the Union, such as for the economy, the financial sector, international trade, international relations, but also for environment-related issues (e.g., climate change, renewable energies, circular economy).
In 2015, the EU adopted its first Circular Economy package that should bring the EU economy in line with sustainability. And with the newly proposed EU Green Deal in 2019, the EU set out an ambitious target to achieve a climate-neutral Europe by 2050. And this goal can only be achieved by rapid progress in establishing a circular economy.
Given that the EU has more experience with enlargement than with reduction, Brexit represents a quite unique phenomenon worth studying. While several countries have joined the Union over the yars, only the UK has left it. This paper seeks to provide answers on how Brexit will affect the EU environmental policy-making, in particular, it focuses on the consequences in the field of the circular economy. Therefore, the work analyzes and evaluates the consequences of Brexit in terms of establishing the European circular economy in two realms: economy and politics. The paper seeks to shed light on possible short-term and long-term consequences.
European Union, Circular Economy, and the EU Green Deal
The concept of circular economy (CE) has enjoyed widespread support from across Europe as it combines economic benefits (increased competitiveness, resource efficiency, new jobs, etc.) with environmental benefits (waste reduction, reducing greenhouse gas emissions output, etc.). Though not lucidly defined yet, the circular economy concept builds on economic, natural, and social capital and redefines growth by focusing on society-wide benefits.4 The concept of CE seeks to design out waste and pollution, decouple economic activities from material demand and consumption of resources, and keep valuable materials as long as possible in the system.5 Thus, CE aims at a reduction, reuse, recycling and recovery. CE redefines waste as input material for other industries and focuses on promoting the use of secondary raw materials. This not only includes recycling efforts but includes a view on the overall life-cycle of products. Besides, CE spurs innovation and provides opportunities for new business models and growth.6
In the current linear mode of production, growth is propelled by natural resource extraction that also drives up greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.7 So far, progress towards a circular economy is slow. The global circularity rate stands at about 9.1%. 8 However, with the global population projected to cross the 8.5 billion mark in 2030, demand for goods and services will necessarily grow. Annually, about 88 billion tonnes of materials are extracted from the ground, and about 2.01 billion tonnes of garbage is disposed of by urban residents worldwide.9 In 2019, emissions from industrial activities and the burning of fossil fuels will pump an estimated 36.8 billion metric tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere.10 And according to the U.N. World Meteorological Organization, the CO2 concentration levels in the atmosphere hit a new record in 2018 by rising to 407.8 parts per million. 11 Since material management accounts for about two-thirds of worldwide emissions, the establishment of a circular economy also supports climate mitigation and resource efficiency plays directly into climate action. 12 The reuse of materials (e.g., cement, steel, aluminum, and plastics) can be central to lower greenhouse gas emissions. Accordingly, <mark>improved circular loops play a crucial role in achieving</mark> the goals of the <mark>Paris</mark> Climate Agreement. If the current path is continued, there is no way to contain temperature rise to a 1.5ºC above pre-industrial levels as laid out in the UN Paris Agreement.
Based on a report by SYSTEMIQ (2017), <mark>scaling</mark> the <mark>c</mark>ircular <mark>e</mark>conomy <mark>in Europe could offer</mark> investment opportunities totaling <mark>€320 billion.</mark>13 In a circular economy, resources are used in a more sustainable way, the demand for primary raw materials is cut, resulting in increased material efficiency. Increased usage of secondary raw materials can substitute the industry’s demand for primary raw materials. While raw materials demand could be lowered by an estimated 20%, the EU <mark>GDP would increase by 3%.</mark> The promotion of circularity in the manufacturing sector of complex consumer durables could bring material savings worth some €553 billion in the EU alone.14 According to a report by European Parliament (2017), the transition to a circular economy could create between 1.2 million and 3 million new jobs by 2030 and cut greenhouse gas emissions by more than 500 million tonnes between 2015 and 2035. A study by Material Economics (2018) estimates that a circular economy could <mark>halve EU industrial emissions</mark> by 2050.15
Accordingly, circular economy efforts have become integral parts of recent environmental talks in Europe and beyond. In December 2015, the European Commission adopted its first Circular Economy Package aimed at supporting businesses and consumers to engage a transition to a more circular economy.16 The package was followed by the Circular Economy Action Plan and proposals that cover the full lifecycle of products, from design, production, and consumption to waste management and the development of a stronger secondary raw materials market. The initiatives resulted in a series of legislative and non-legislative actions, such as for waste management, plastics, fertilizers, marine litter, critical raw materials and indicators measuring circularity. The EU also set out targets for recycling (65% of municipal and 75% of packaging waste by 2030) and implemented a ban on landfilling of separately collected waste by 2030. According to the EU plans, the competitiveness of key industry sectors including manufacturing, waste management, and recycling will be strengthened by the Circular Economy Package.
The EU Green Deal
In December 2019, the new European Commission proposed its Green Deal with the aim to achieve carbon-neutrality by 2050. 17 However, achieving the net-zero GHG emissions objective by 2050 requires a profound transformation of value chains across the EU economy based on innovative production processes and new business models. To achieve this ambitious goal, industries in all member countries have to make significant contributions. The new Commission President von der Leyen claimed the European Green Deal to be "Europe's man on the moon moment"18, describing climate policy as Europe’s new growth strategy. According to von der Leyen, the former growth model based on fossil fuels and pollution is out of date. The new Green Deal which is based on ten major pillars not only includes climate-neutrality by 2050 but also the establishment of a circular economy (see Figure 1). As laid out by several studies, the establishment of a circular economy is a feasible option to reduce emissions.19 A large-scale switch from fossil-based technologies to low-carbon technology, the expansion of renewable energies, and the promotion of energy-efficiency measures are envisioned. Since efforts will result in increased material demands further triggering greenhouse gas emissions, a net-zero emission reduction objective is only achievable with a drastic system change in material and resource management based on circularity.
The New Green Deal for Europe comprises three distinct institutions: The Green Public Works (GPW) (an investment program), the Environmental Union (package of legislation to align policy with scientific consensus), and the Environmental Justice Commission (EJC) an independent body to research, monitor, and advise EU policymakers. The latter is designed as an international body to ensure that the green transition is also a just one. By its broad mandate, the EJC has the power to set new international standards for research and reporting on environmental injustices. However, the EJC takes only an advisory role, providing data on environmental changes for other EU institutions or developing new indicators to evaluate policies. The GPW aims at delivering Europe’s economic transformation through a large investment program by including aspects of environmental justice and focusing on decarbonizing Europe’s economy, reversing biodiversity loss and guaranteeing decent jobs across the continent. The program is financed entirely through green bonds issued by the European Investment Bank. The Environmental Union aims at a broader target – a systemic change by offering a comprehensive regulatory package to realign policies based on scientific consensus. It is aimed to foster Europe´s position as a global green leader.
In the Green Deal, the EU commits itself to lead by example, setting standards for sustainable growth across global value chains, and using diplomacy, trade and development cooperation to advance climate action. The promotion of a Circular Economy aims to further reduce GHG emissions but also to create economic growth and to spur global competitiveness. The new EU circular economy approach based on new waste and recycling laws will not only represent half of the EU´s efforts to achieve net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 but also include measures to ensure access to raw material supplies. Access to critical raw materials (CRMs) used in digital and clean technologies was named as a strategic security question in the European Green Deal. Certain CRMs are irreplaceable in solar panels, wind turbines, electric vehicles, and energy-efficient lighting, essential technologies for the establishment of a circular economy and the promotion of energy transition. With the mining sector on the decrease in the EU and global demand on the rise, urban mining practices and the circular economy approach gain spotlight in the EU. In times of limited access to material resources, closing industrial loops represents a promising reply to ensure the development of sustainable economy.

Consequences of Brexit for EU Circular Economy Efforts
Although a circular economy is perceived as a necessity to ensure the prosperity and wellbeing of human mankind in the future, its establishment is not self-evolving. Change requires strong political commitment and is often based on economic rationale. With the departure of the UK from the Union, we will see changes in the direction of how EU circular policies evolve.20 The section below elaborates in detail the expected consequences of Britain´s departure for establishing the EU Circular Economy by focusing on two major dimensions: the economic and the political realm.
The Economic Realm
The 2018 Global Environmental Performance Index lists solely European countries under the top 15.21 Switzerland ranks first, followed by France, Denmark, Malta, Sweden. The UK is put on the sixth position while Germany is ranked 13th. However, green and clean does not necessarily correlate with a high level of circularity. Burning waste for energy, as widely practiced in some countries, minimizes landfilling but is not improving recycling rates or establishing circular loops within the industry. In a circular economy, products remain as long as possible in the system, they are shared, reused, repaired and/or refurbished. But when they finally reach their end of life, they are dismantled, recycled and the materials are injected back in the economic cycle. However, CE not only focuses on waste management and recycling but taking a look at every stage of a product's life, starting from the mining of raw materials, design, production, consumption to waste management. By closing loops, a CE not only reduces the output of waste and emissions but also lower the overall demand for materials. To stay within planetary boundaries, by 2050 an average European will have to reduce by 80% the amount of natural resources they currently use for nutrition, housing, mobility and lifestyles (IEEP, 2019).
Based on the low self-sufficiency for raw materials in the EU, the physical trade balance is obviously asymmetric. By weight, the Union imports about three times more goods from non-EU countries than it exports. Hence, the Union is very dependent and vulnerable to the import of certain raw materials. According to Eurostat, import dependency climbed from 18.6% (2000) to 23.1% in 2018. To address the issue, in 2008 the Commission launched the Raw Material Initiative, a strategy to secure access to raw materials in the EU. In 2018, the Commission published a report highlighting the potential for a more circular usage of critical raw materials in the economy.22
A circular economy aims to maintain the value of products, materials and resources for as long as possible by returning them into the product cycle at the end of life while minimizing the production of waste. However, modern economies are heavily dependent on a steady and secure supply of raw materials and the demand for materials is increasing with economic development. A recent OECD (2019) report projects the demand for raw materials to more than double by 2060.23 The concept of CE stresses environmental and economic benefits gained by a more circular economic system, mainly by lowering the overall material demand and raw material consumption and increasing resource efficiency as well as developing a secondary raw material market.
The domestic material consumption (DMC) measures the annual amount of materials directly used by an economy.24 In 2000, the DMC of the Union was roughly 7,500 million tonnes (see Figure 2). After a decline due to the financial crisis in 2008, the EU-28 climbed to some 6,900 million tonnes in 2017. Across the EU, material consumption patterns vary largely depending on each Member States´ extraction of domestic resources, population density, living standards, economic development, living standards, the size of agriculture, manufacturing and construction industries, but also the size of its imports and exports market. The amount of consumed material per capita in the EU-28 varies largely. While Finland consumed the most material per (34.5), followed by Estonia (30.9) and Ireland (26.2), the UK only consumes 8.9 tonnes. Spain has the lowest consumption rate across the EU-28 with 8.4 tonnes. In 2018, the total DMC of the EU economy stood at about 13.8 tonnes per capita.25 And much of it still ends up as waste. In 2004, the UK´s waste production peaked at 602 kg. The top three waste producers are Denmark (766 kg), Malta (640 kg) and Cyprus (637). The top recycling countries are Germany (67.3%), Slovenia (58.9%), Austria (57.7%), while the UK shows a rate of 44.1%. Although not the core idea of a circular economy, recycling of materials helps to improve circularity as it can be re-introduced into the economy as new substances or products.

The Political Realm
During its membership, the UK realized its potentials by actively shaping policy-making through leading the discussions on EU policy instruments. From the 1990s onwards, London turned into a driver for setting the EU’s environmental agenda, in some areas pushing for more action, in some areas blocking it - depending on its own national interests. As part of the Green Growth Group, London was supportive to actions combating climate change, and it became a leader within on climate change mitigation efforts. With Brexit the group weakens significantly and this may result in a decline of influence on the matter.
The UK was a key driver for installing the EU emissions trading scheme and pushed for more ambitious GHG reduction targets emissions by 2020 and 2030.38 Furthermore, the <mark>UK has taken a prominent role</mark> in shaping EU external environmental and <mark>climate policy and diplomacy at the international level</mark>. As the departure of Britain may lead to a readjustment of the coalitions within the Council, it could lead to a situation where EU environmental policy itself becomes less ambitious.
Britain´s EU membership <mark>provided a framework promoting transboundary cooperation</mark>, with keeping some room for local policy innovation. The UK actively directed the discussions towards more market-based approaches (e.g., environmental auditing and eco-labels).39 Britain directly benefited from the membership through cleaner air, water, and oceans and the development of green market systems and turned from an environmental laggard into a progressive environmental power pro-actively steering the discussions. However, with about 80% of the environment-related legislation coming from Brussels, London never forgot to point on the regulatory burden of environmental action during its membership. Now, the UK needs to completely rebuild its institutional capacities regarding environmental protection (e.g., DEPA) and Brexit has also intensified internal political turmoil, such as constitutional disputes between the Scottish, Wallisian and UK governments. Given the large disagreements among people about Brexit (urban vs. rural, pro-EU vs. anti-EU), there is an environmental divide emerging between (largely anti-EU) rural areas in favor of lowering environmental standards to attract business and pro-European urban areas in favor of upholding/tightening high environmental standards. In the mid- and long-run, there are concerns that the break-up may weaken (or slow down) environmental protection efforts on the island. Since the UK will be occupied mainly with internal issues, the EU will not be able to get much support from the UK on certain global environmental issues (e.g., circular economy, climate change). Consequently, future coalition-building becomes more difficult, as the UK government has new immediate priorities. This is underlined by London´s lack of interest in participation in the European Environment Agency. Given the importance of the build-up a pan-European monitoring system for circular economy, the UK´s long-term strategy is questioned.
Implications of Brexit for Political Dynamics in the Union
During the enlargement process, the influence of each individual state on decision-making has declined gradually. Thus, for gaining leverage over EU decision-making, building winning coalitions is essential. In the EU, groups of member states with more or less close ties form coalitions to influence the decision-making process. Basically, such coalitions emerge at an early stage, often during the informal approach to formal talks in the European Council and the Council of the European Union. And since proposals can only be rejected by a group, Britain’s departure will have a major impact on relations in the Council of the European as new coalitions need to be formed to advance with CE. According to the Lisbon Treaty, approval of decisions in the Council requires at least 55% of votes from Member States and 65% from share-of-population votes.
Britain clearly utilized its EU membership to steer European policy-making in its favor.40 While the country supported more than 97% of the legislative measures adopted by the Council between 2004 and 2016, London voted against proposals more often and showed relatively higher abstention scores (see Figure 6) than others. Germany is the second-largest veto player but on different issues than the UK. Closest with the UK on passing proposals come Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands.
In regards to environment-related decisions between 2010 and 2020, the UK never voted against a Council proposal, with only one abstention.41 Most votes against environment-related proposals come from Poland (6), Bulgaria, Cypress, Denmark, Hungary (4 each), followed by Malta, Lithuania and Slovakia (3 each).42

A Franco-German Coalition for Circular Economy?
As a result of Brexit, the importance of large member states (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and Poland) increases, as their voting power gains are greater. A blocking minority can be achieved with 13 member states voting against, or at least four member states representing more than 35% of the EU’s population. France and Germany together represent 33.2% of the EU’s population. In a different constellation, France, Italy, and Spain together represent 39.24% of the European population and can therefore easily form a blocking minority (Kleinowski, 2019).
In the past, the Union's political integration was mainly driven by a Franco-German coalition. Paris and Berlin were the primary architects for closer integration in monetary, foreign, security, as well as justice affairs. 43 London often voiced skeptical and critical voices, attempting to steer discussions in its favor. Most of the British proposals have insisted that EU legislation should be efficient and market-oriented with a focus to reduce bureaucratic burden. London has relentlessly criticized the burden of the tight regulatory framework of the EU. Furthermore, British environmental NGOs have played a crucial role in the creation of EU-level ENGOs and influencing European environmental policy-making.44 London was an advocate for a reduction of regulations with the aim to reduce the bureaucratic costs for small and medium-sized enterprises in the internal market. Whereas London often called for more market-based instruments, Germany prefers approaches based on binding regulatory instruments. Brexit silences these critical voices, while others have more space. Poland is the largest Central and Eastern European member, and its solid economic performance provides Warsaw with legitimacy to join discussions. However, in the past Poland showed no strong engagement in promoting strict environmental policies (e.g., renewable energies). In the EU-27, the voice of climate laggards such as Poland, Hungary or the Czech Republic could turn louder. These countries blocked a new EU climate change target and refused to support writing the target into the EU’s strategic program for 2019-24. Sharing a communist past and poor environmental records, the Visegrád group could - led by Warsaw - further slowdown EU efforts in stepping up circular economy efforts.
In another option, we could see a coalition between Germany and the eight northern European member states. Finance ministers of these countries already met in 2018 to start talks about alternative proposals for reforming the Eurozone. On various matters, such as the environment, monetary, fiscal, EU budget, their viewpoints are close to Germany's. However, despite this close relationship with its Northern neighbors, Berlin will not risk its uniquely close bilateral relationship with Paris. Thus, the political realm about CE is still in limbo.
In times where the EU seeks to leave behind its linear production system, the departure of Britain triggers massive challenges. Modernization and promoting sustainability, such as decarbonizing production processes gain relevance but there are discussions on how to promote such a major systematic transition. Are large industrial actors the main drivers or is change driven by the integration of a regional ecosystem of resilient actors, enabling diversity of approaches (open source)? While it is not clear yet which approach prevails over the other, some countries prefer certain approaches. Larger investments in a future-oriented European industry are supported by several Member States (i.e., the Netherlands, Benelux Germany, France, Poland, Spain). However, the four large member states Germany, France, Italy and Spain show little interest in promoting the digitalization of their economies, mainly out of fear of a decline in their traditional manufacturing industries. On the other hand, the Netherlands, the Nordic countries, Ireland and Czechia, see digitalization as an opportunity, also promote a sustainable economy. Also, the UK´s strategy stresses the idea that a circular economy can be promoted by open platforms. While Britain's departure may lower pressure from competitors in the field of CE in the short term it could slow down necessary transition processes in the long run.

Covid-19 and the EU Budget
Among the policy areas where London took great interest during its membership were economic policies, global free trade, foreign affairs, security, defense, internal market consolidation and budgetary matters (e.g., the Multi-annual Financial Framework). The Union’s annual budget lays down all expenditures and revenues and ensures the funding of EU policies and programs. The largest source of EU revenues (67%) stems from member-states transfers based on gross national income (GNI). After Germany and France, the UK was the third largest net contributor to the EU budget. London contributed roughly €13bn (2018) to the total EU budget (about 9%), and Brexit rips a large hole in the EU budget. With difficulty, the EU managed to set up its 2020 budget of about €168.7bn (2019: €165.8bn) by rising the contributions from member states.46 The budget covers seven large areas, with the biggest part allocated to Sustainable Growth (€59.9bn), followed by Economic, social and territorial cohesion (€58.6bn), Competitiveness for growth and jobs (€25.3bn), Global Europe, and Administration (each €10.3bn). Large parts (ca. 40%) are spent on agricultural policy and farming. In the past, London heavily criticized the Union´s agricultural policy for its high costs. There is concern that agriculture continues to take up most part of the budget while other areas encounter cuts in their finances. After the massive stimulus programs due to Covid-19 outbreak, the Union needed to readjust its budget. Given that the EU is left with lower financial contributions and due to the massive economic distortions in 2020, environment-related programs could receive lower funding, including circular economy efforts. As of now, the concrete consequences of Covid-19 are not comprehensible and cannot be discussed in detail here. However, the pandemic clearly showed how vulnerable global supply chains are and how important the availability of locally produced goods is. In this respect, the virus outbreak is likely to bring back some production lines to Europe over the mid-term. While it is unrealistic to think that globalization stops completely, there are strong calls for strengthening the local or regional production. From a historical point of view, such external shocks proved to have the power to change the economy drastically. Future shortages of certain goods and raw materials due to the closures of borders in Europe and worldwide can only be avoided by promoting a strong local industry based on closed loops. However, the crisis takes its toll on companies´ investments in innovation and digitalization. As a result, European governments and the EU wrapped up previously unseen financial stimulus programs. However, calls from some EU Member States were loud to abandon the Green Deal in order to have more financial resources to restore the “conventional” economy. 47 Properly designed, such large financial supports could be used as instruments to stimulate green and sustainable circular efforts in the EU. As of now, it is too early to say how the programs will impact EU circular efforts.

Conclusions
From an environmental perspective, the membership of Britain in the EU benefited both sides. Great <mark>Britain turned</mark> from an environmental laggard <mark>into a</mark> major economic powerhouse and discussion <mark>leader in environmental policy-making</mark>. In recent years, Britain actively <mark>contributed to</mark> policy discussions favoring approaches based on market approaches and <mark>reducing bureaucracy. </mark>With the departure of Britain, the future direction of EU environmental policies is uncertain. Four decades ago, the focus of EU environmental protection efforts was mainly local or regional, and environmental challenges seemed to be more simple to address. However, today we face cross-regional and complex global challenges, such as climate change, resource depletion, growing material demand, or the mounting waste streams. With the rise in scope and complexity of ecological challenges, international cooperation among countries and regions increased relevance.
In regard to the establishment of a pan-European circular economy, the departure of the UK represents a blow to EU efforts. The Union has not only lost a wealthy, powerful and high advanced economy but also a progressive force in addressing environmental issues. And consequences will be felt in the economic but also in the political realm. In the past, the UK government has made progress in promoting CE by applying flexible and market-based approaches. Major consequences of Brexit will likely be observed in the mid- or long-run after the UK government overcomes immediate priorities after quitting the Union. There is concern that Britain may drift away from EU environmental legislation over the mid or long run and actively trying to undermine the EU environmental standards to improve its economic competitiveness. And the Covid-19 outbreak speeds up such developments. In 2019, the government announced its 25 Year Environmental Plan aimed at to delivering a green Brexit.48 While the plan includes ten target areas, it only peripherally points to the concept of circular economy and thus fails to include concrete measures promoting circularity of materials.

Factors influencing future warming
Climate-warming greenhouse gas <mark>emissions</mark> have not yet <mark>peak</mark>ed, but will likely do so soon <mark>as</mark> the economic advantages of <mark>renewable</mark> power generation <mark>become</mark> even more <mark>obvious</mark>, and ageing coal-fired generators in the electricity sector reach their use-by date. <mark>However</mark>, as discussed in sections 4 and 5 above, oil and gas production are likely to remain strong — and may increase — until mid-century. On present indications, the emissions <mark>decline</mark> over the next three decades <mark>will be slow</mark>.
This pattern is completely at odds with policy-makers’ stated intention of holding warming to 1.5–2°C. In 2017, a “carbon law” was articulated by a group of leading scientists who demonstrated that for a two-in-three chance of holding warming to 2°C, emissions would need to be halved every decade from 2020 to 2050; CO2 emissions from land use reduced to zero by 2050; and carbon drawdown capacity of five gigatonnes of CO2 per year be established by 2050.103 Clearly, given the current state of climate <mark>policymaking</mark>, we are <mark>not</mark> within cooee of <mark>holding warming</mark> to 2°C, with annual emissions likely rising between 2020 and 2030, rather than halving.
Future emissions are a primary determinant of the warming path over the medium to longer term; but so are other factors including accelerated sea-ice loss decreasing Earth’s reflectivity, and accelerating emissions from carbon stores (boreal and tropical forest fires, reduced ocean efficiency, and permafrost feedbacks, for example), though these are not systematically incorporated into model projections of future warming.
Another factor is the Earth’s Energy Imbalance (EEI), which has grown consistently over the last two decades: a positive EEI “confirms the lag of the climate system in responding to forcing and implies that additional global warming will take place even without further forcing change”.104 One of the drivers of increasing EEI has been a reduction in sulfate aerosol emissions, which are a by-product of burning fossil fuels, and have a strong cooling impact of 0.5–1°C, but are short-lived in the atmosphere. Aerosols have been “masking” some of the warming so far.105
Declining coal use and clean air policies reduce the aerosol impact. This is our “Faustian bargain”:106 as fossil fuel use declines, so will aerosol emissions which have been offsetting some warming, so that for the next two decades lower emissions will have little impact on the warming trend.107 One example: A 5% annual reduction in emissions of a single greenhouse gas, from 2020 and based on a middle-road-emissions path, has no statistically significant effect on warming for more than two decades, as compared to a no-mitigation pathway.108

07 The Physical Risks are Cascading and Systemic
The evidence from tipping points alone suggests that we are in a state of planetary emergency: both the risk and urgency of the situation are acute […] If damaging tipping cascades can occur and a global tipping point cannot be ruled out, then this is an existential threat to civilization. Prof. Tim Lenton and colleagues, “Climate tipping points — too risky to bet against”116
The physical risks are non-linear, cascading, systemic and largely irreversible on human time frames. Climate models do not adequately represent all processes and are likely to underestimate the risks.
Many elements of the climate system exhibit tipping points or thresholds at which a small change causes a larger, more critical change to be initiated, taking that system from one state to a discretely different state far less conducive to human survival and prosperity (see section 3 above). For example, a polar ice sheet may reach a temperature threshold beyond which continuing and accelerated ice mass loss occurs, even without any additional rise in temperature, and such a change may take decades to centuries to be fully realized before a new (ice-free) system stability occurs.
Such threshold changes may be abrupt and irreversible on relevant time frames. And once the threshold is passed, returning conditions to pre-threshold conditions may not restore the system. This is known as hysteresis, or bifurcation of a system, where it may be more difficult, or impossible, for a system to return to its previous state. Put more simply: the path from A to B is not the same as the path from from B to A. Ice sheets are a good example.
A chilling 2015 report on Thresholds and closing windows: Risks of irreversible cryosphere climate change warned that the Paris commitments will not prevent the Earth “crossing into the zone of irreversible thresholds” in polar and mountain glacier regions, and that crossing these boundaries may “result in processes that cannot be halted unless temperatures return to levels below pre-industrial” (emphasis added).117 For example, the tipping point for the most vulnerable West Antarctic glaciers is probably between 0.5°C and 1°C, but cooling the planet back to that range would not create the conditions for their re-establishment.
And in 2024, researchers again warned of “overconfidence in climate overshoot”, that is, exceeding a temperature target for decades, on the assumption that negative emissions technology will be able to later reduce the heating and restore conditions as if there had been no overshoot. Extinctions caused by overshoot are one example. They show that “global and regional climate change and associated risks after an overshoot are different from a world that avoids it… the possibility that global warming could be reversed many decades into the future might be of limited relevance for adaptation planning today [because] temperature reversal could be undercut by strong Earth-system feedbacks resulting in high near-term and continuous long-term warming… Only rapid near-term emission reductions are effective in reducing climate risks.”118
Changes in one element of the climate system may also trigger an unforeseen chain or cascade of events in which one event in a system has a negative effect on other related components. For example, the mutual interaction of individual climate tipping points and/or abrupt, non-linear changes, may lead to more profound changes to the system as a whole, and interactions between these climate systems could lower the critical temperature thresholds at which each tipping point is passed.119
Together, tipping point thresholds, non-linear change and cascading events represent systemic risks, that is, the risk of a breakdown of an entire system rather than simply the failure of individual parts:
More frequent and intense extreme weather and climate-related events, as well as changes in average climate conditions, are expected to continue to damage infrastructure, ecosystems, and social systems that provide essential benefits to communities… Extreme <mark>weather</mark> and climate-related impacts on one system can <mark>result in</mark> increased risks or <mark>failures in</mark> other critical systems, including <mark>water</mark> resources, food production and distribution, energy and transportation, public health, international <mark>trade, and</mark> national <mark>security</mark>. The full extent of climate change risks to interconnected systems, many of which span regional and national boundaries, is often greater than the sum of risks to individual sectors.120
Systemic change means climate elements can tip from one state to an entirely different one with a sudden shock that may permanently alter the way the planet works.121 In the physical interactions among the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation and the Amazon rainforest, the polar sheets are often the initiators of cascade events,122 with Greenland and West Antarctica at risk of passing their tipping points within the 1.5°C–2°C Paris range (and there is evidence they have already done so).
Such changes are not adequately incorporated into climate models: “Change can come about abruptly and even catastrophically… Predictive models are the lifeblood of climate science, and the foundation upon which political responses to the climate and ecological crisis are often based. But their ability to predict such large-scale disruptive events is severely limited… The IPCC’s estimates of how much CO2 we can still emit to be on the safe side explicitly leave out many known large-scale disruptions or tipping points because of insufficient understanding or because models cannot capture them.”123
Researchers have also investigated how changes in forest degradation and monsoon circulation are interlinked: “It turns out that forest loss caused by direct deforestation, droughts, and fires might vastly contribute to a changing climate in South America and could drive the coupled Amazon rainforest/ South American monsoon circulation system past a tipping point [and] suggest an upcoming regime shift of the Amazon ecosystem.”124
If cascades coalesce, there is the possibility of “Hothouse Earth”. In 2018, a group of eminent scientists explored the potential for self-reinforcing positive <mark>feedbacks</mark> in major elements of the climate system feedbacks and their mutual interaction to <mark>drive the Earth</mark> System climate <mark>to</mark> a point of <mark>no return</mark>, whereby further warming would become self-sustaining (that is, without further human perturbations), and prevent temperature stabilization, driving the system to what they termed a “Hothouse Earth”.125 In plain terms, <mark>humans</mark> would lose control and <mark>lack</mark> the <mark>capacity to stop</mark> cascading <mark>warming</mark> and the researchers warned that “we are in a climate emergency… this is <mark>an existential threat</mark> to civilisation”.126 This planetary threshold <mark>could exist at</mark> a temperature rise as low as 2°C, possibly even in the <mark>1.5°</mark>C–2°C range.127 In other words, we may have already arrived at this point, but conclusive evidence of this moment requires hindsight.

Raising <mark>resource efficiency</mark> would be the <mark>fastest way to de-risk</mark> the European <mark>economy</mark>. It would <mark>reduce dependence on</mark> authoritarian regimes and <mark>fragile supply chains</mark>, and make the EU less vulnerable to volatile commodity prices (Letta, 2024; WEF, 2024). Circular solutions will become more important as energy infrastructure dependencies shift from imports of fossil fuels (commodities) to the import of critical raw materials (CRMs; to create capital assets in renewable energy). This does not mean that the EU should aim to move to autarky and become a self-sufficient economy or introduce protectionist policies. Rather, the elimination of wasteful use of materials and energy has the potential to support the economic-security objectives of reducing import dependencies for critical products and deepening the single market and making it more flexible (Pisani-Ferry et al, 2024). A well-functioning circular single market would increase the EU’s resilience against import shocks. Critical dependencies can be reduced by bringing down demand for imported virgin materials, by substituting them with local, re-used and recycled materials and by using fewer materials thanks to efficiency-by-design or repair opportunities.
In energy, the <mark>recovery of</mark> biodegradable <mark>waste</mark> could <mark>contribute significantly to</mark> supply <mark>diversification</mark>. Biogas and biomethane could meet 10 percent of natural gas demand by 2030, to which better food-waste collection and processing could contribute. Biogas production emits little carbon and prevents organic material from going to landfill. Given that organic waste accounts for up to 50 percent of municipal waste (ECN, 2022) and 10 percent of all greenhouse gas emissions (WWF, 2021), that would reduce environmental damage while contributing to energy security.
The next decades are likely to be characterised by supply bottlenecks as <mark>climate shocks</mark> proliferate, economies race to decarbonise <mark>and trade rivalries disrupt flows</mark>. But even in the very short term, resource efficiency can offer a buffer to protect the EU’s economic security. For CRMs, <mark>supply deficits</mark> are already expected for 2024 (Stewart et al, 2023). In the medium term, there are <mark>forecasts</mark> of potential supply gaps for copper and lithium of <mark>up to</mark> 10 percent and <mark>49 percent</mark> respectively <mark>in 2030</mark> (ETC, 2023).
Commodity price volatility is another threat to economic security. Since 2020, the prices of critical minerals used in batteries underwent both dramatic increases and large declines (IEA, 2024). Certain battery-grade lithium compounds increased in price by 800 percent to 1000 percent in just over one year (Riofrancos et al, 2023). This volatility is likely to increase because of climate impacts, with 25 percent of mining executives expecting climate risks to be very significant in their operations in the next five years (KPMG, 2023).
To ensure that decarbonisation is not disrupted, the circular market for CRMs needs to expand faster. Through end-of-life recovery, design for materials efficiency and extended product lifetimes, more reliable markets for critical minerals can be developed that would improve economic security. According to one estimate, secondary materials could meet up to 91 percent of US demand for CRMs in low-carbon technologies by 2050 (Karali and Shah, 2022). The value embedded in disused electronic devices globally is around $57 billion. To achieve economic security, end-of-life treatment must be pursued in conjunction with other measures to reduce the volume of CRMs needed further up the production chain.
Beyond CRMs, design of products so that they use less raw materials, and resource efficiency through repair and durability have great potential to contribute to economic security. Many household appliances are treated as obsolete far before their designed lifetimes are over, partly driven by a culture of constant upgrading to the latest model (EEA, 2024). Design also has a role to play in developing clean circular loops. This ensures that products are designed to minimise the use of substances of concern and other pollutants, to facilitate end-of-life recovery.
<mark>Circularity</mark> would contribute to other aspects of security. By reducing biodiversity loss, it would also diminish bio-risks to health, such as epidemics, and environmental disasters due to extraction, such as landslips. It would also <mark>reduce</mark> the <mark>drivers of armed conflict</mark>, as shown in the Environmental Justice Atlas, which maps how the extraction of resources and resource management drive conflict. These <mark>conflicts</mark> will <mark>proliferate</mark> with the increase in extraction for decarbonisation, given that 32 percent to 40 percent of this extraction is expected to occur in countries<mark> with</mark> weak to <mark>failing</mark> resource <mark>governance</mark> (Watari et al, 2021).

The EU, Democratic Institutions, and Peaceful Relations
Social scientists have been interested in the eu as an international actor for decades (Bosse 2009; Duchêne 1972; Freedom House, 2020; Kagan, 2 DEMOCRACY IN EUROPE: ENLARGED … 27 2004). One issue on which scholars have focused is whether the EU member states are capable of acting cohesively (Mogherini, 2016). Some researchers, such as Robert Kagan (2004), have questioned the unity of European countries and their ability to speak with one voice; but others—François Duchêne (1972) among them, as well as Ian Manners (2002)—have stressed the uniqueness of European integration, which has created a collective actor in the form of the eu, <mark>with</mark> its <mark>shared economic and political capabilities</mark>. While the dominant tendency among scholars of international politics has been to stress the importance of countries’ power for the shaping of world politics, some researchers—such as John Ikenberry (2000; see also Keohane & Nye, 1977)—have argued that the nations of the world have become ever more intertwined through shared norms and values and that common rules, laws, and institutions have gained greatly in importance. It has been said that the eu, as a normative community, is based on certain fundamental norms: peace, liberty, democracy, human rights, and the rule of law (Manners, 2002; Pace, 2007). European integration has developed in accordance with the peace standard set out in the treaties that established the European Coal and Steel Community (ecsc) and the European Economic Community (eec), in 1951 and 1957, respectively; and on the basis of the principles of liberty, democracy, human rights, and the rule of law enshrined in the Maastricht Treaty (1992). Further elements of integration include a common development policy, the Common Foreign and Security Policy, and the so-called Copenhagen criteria adopted in 1993 for prospective member states. Article 6(1) of the Treaty on European Union (TEU) states that: ‘The Union is founded on the principles of liberty, democracy, respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms, and the rule of law, which are common to the Member States’ (TEU, 2007). In 2009, through the Lisbon Treaty, the eu Charter of Fundamental Rights that was enacted in 2000 was made legally binding on the Union. The Charter proclaims that the Union is based on universal values of human dignity, freedom, equality, solidarity and the principles of democracy and the rule of law (2000).
The eu’s democratic community has drawn a great deal of attention from researchers in political science (see, for instance, Russett, 1993; 28 D. SILANDER Russett & Oneal, 2001). The idea that liberal democracies can cooperate peacefully dates back to the late eighteenth century as developed in Immanuel Kant’s concept of democratic peace (1991). It was not until the 1960s, however, that rigorous studies were first conducted on this issue (Russett, 1993). The basic idea of the democratic peace is that peace is promoted if countries have free and fair elections, with power resting in the hands of the people. So, if more countries adopt democratic rules of the game, peace will prevail more broadly as well. Bruce Russett (1993) contends that the democratic requirement of <mark>popular support makes it difficult</mark> for leaders <mark>to wage war</mark>, due to the checks and balances embodied in democratic institutions. Furthermore, the political culture of democracies rests on debate, <mark>negotiation</mark>, and peaceful political competition—features that <mark>facilitate compromise</mark> with other democratic countries. The transparency of a democratic system, moreover, minimizes the risk of surprise and misunderstanding vis-à-vis other countries; while its <mark>openness encourages cooperation</mark> and integration. Trade, investment, culture, and communication generate interdependency and further bonds of friendship between democracies. Human beings, in this perspective, are basically rational and good. When representing their citizens, therefore, political leaders in democracies will tend to safeguard peace, to defend freedom, and to promote the common good (Kant, 1991; Russett, 1993).
Needless to say, the idea of the democratic peace has come in for criticism (see Dafoe et al., 2013). First and foremost, how are democracy and war to be defined? Furthermore, some critics have also claimed that democracies can act aggressively towards non-democracies. Notwithstanding these criticisms, however, research on the idea of the democratic peace has been of great importance for our understanding of European integration and the development of the eu. Political and economic cooperation between elected European governments, deepening over time, has resulted in the establishment of common rules, laws, and institutions. In 2012, the achievements of the Union’s democratic peace drew the attention of the Norwegian Nobel Committee, which awarded the Peace Prize to the EU (2012). The Committee acknowledged how the Union historically had promoted European peace and reconciliation as well as democracy and human rights and how an increasing number of countries had become bound together in friendship (2012).

In a European context, the role of the eu in promoting democracy can be seen in its influence on the political development of non-member states in the proximity to the Union’s democratic community. The promotion of norms has operated through conditional political and economic cooperation with the countries in question, with an eye to furthering their democratic development. The democratization process seen in large parts of Europe should be understood, in the view of these scholars, as in large part resulting from how the eu has acted as an international agent of socialization, promoting democratic norms and values in non-member states (Florini, 1996; Linden, 2002; Silander, 2005).

The Enlargement of the Union and the Expansion of Democracy¶ Researchers such as Ann Florini (1996) have analysed the international promotion of norms. Scholarly interest has been particularly strong in the eu’s efforts to promote democracy in Southern Europe in the 1970s and in Central and Eastern Europe in the 1990s. These dramatic events surprised a majority of scholars. During the 1980s, many prominent researchers—Samuel Huntington, for instance—considered democratization in Central and Eastern Europe to be impossible (1984), due to the great anti-democratic influence of the Soviet Union. In the course of just a few years, however, the picture changed drastically. In 1989, there were 23 communist countries in the world; five years later, only five were left— and none in Europe as communist rule continued in China, Cuba, Laos, North Korea, and Vietnam (Silander, 2005).¶ In the great eastern enlargement of 2004, ten countries—Cyprus, Czechia, Estonia, Hungary, Latvia, Lithuania, Malta, Poland, Slovakia, and Slovenia—joined the eu at the same time. This was a huge success for the Union. During the decades of the Cold War, Europe had been divided between East and West, between communism and democracy. The eu emerged over time as a symbol of cooperation among like-minded countries on the basis of democracy and market economy—with peace, liberty, and fraternity as the result. The great enlargement to the east— the so-called big bang—became a symbol of the West’s victory in the Cold War. The countries of Central and Eastern Europe had been kidnapped by authoritarian forces, but freedom and independence were now theirs for the taking. The Union offered membership on the basis of national reforms and adoption of its norms and values. The so-called Copenhagen criteria from 1993, adopted at a meeting of the European Council in the same city, served as a guide for countries seeking full eu membership. The political criterion demanded that new member states must have a stable democratic system, rule of law, respect human rights, and protect minorities. The economic criterion comprised a functioning market economy and the capacity to withstand competitive pressure and market forces on the EU’s internal market. The legal criterion, finally, stipulated that they must be able meet the obligations of membership by adhering to the goals of the political, economic, and monetary union (EUR-Lex, Accession Criteria; Janse, 2019).¶ It was the task of the European Commission to oversee the membership negotiations and monitor the candidate states’ national progress. In many cases, autocratization has resulted in ‘electoral authoritarian regimes’. Regimes of this kind use elections to legitimize their power, but they make sure beforehand—through various anti-democratic means—that the outcome is the one they prefer. These methods include censoring the media, imposing restrictions on civil society, harassing opposition leaders and activists, and undermining independent electoral mechanisms and election observers (Diamond et al., 2016; Rutzen, 2016; Schedler, 2002). A gradual decline of democracy is often difficult to detect, because it rarely involves violent attacks on democratic institutions, which might otherwise provoke national and international criticism. The mechanisms employed are subtler and more sophisticated, and they operate within the framework of a continuing multi-party system with regular elections (Lührmann & Lindberg, 2019). ¶ Autocratization—the opposite of democratization—can take place in both democratic and undemocratic systems (V-Dem Institute, 2021).
