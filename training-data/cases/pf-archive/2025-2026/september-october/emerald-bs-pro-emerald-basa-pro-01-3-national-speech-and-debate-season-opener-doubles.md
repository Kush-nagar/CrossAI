The single market is a set of common rules on product standards, consumer protection,
environmental norms, workers’ rights and fair competition. This means products made
legally in one member state are legal in all of them as they are made to the same rules.
They can be traded without further ado, with no need for further certification, safety
tests, or border checks. Common legal remedies are available should there be any disputes.
Britain is no longer part of this system as the Johnson government wanted to “actively
diverge” to undercut the EU by having lower standards. That means that British goods
entering the single market often require checks, certification tests, paperwork, and, for
food products, health certificates. The customs union eliminates all tariffs (border taxes)
between its members, with a common external tariff for imports from third countries.
That in turn means that the EU negotiates trade deals collectively – with the clout of
being the world’s largest single market. It therefore often gets good trade deals. When
Britain left and had to replace the deals it had, as part of the EU, by its own separate
deals with countries across the world, it struggled to even get roll-overs of the same
terms, let alone any better ones. It also had to make a trade agreement with the EU itself,
which is by far its most important export market (and supply chain source). While it did
secure a no-tariff agreement, British goods entering the EU are now subject to “rules of
origin” checks which involves looking at their components and calculating the percentage of
added value that is British. It’s not just EU rules, but WTO rules which require this (in

both directions). For exporters, the upshot of leaving both is reams of paperwork, extra
costs and frequent delays at the border. Faced with this, many small businesses have
ceased to export. Fresh products (agriculture and fish) are particularly vulnerable to
delays making them worthless. The Labour government is seeking to reduce barriers to
trade by aligning with single market rules. But it is constrained by the exceedingly cautious
words it put in its manifesto, ruling out joining the single market or the customs union.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
ABSENT: The case contains no explicit impact calculus or pre-written weighing mechanisms (e.g., magnitude, probability, timeframe, or scope comparisons). Cards like HCL 20 (4% GDP loss) and Gilchrist 24 (5% growth underperformance) provide magnitude evidence but lack tags or warrants that comparatively weigh economic harms against opponent impacts (e.g., "Economic stability outweighs sovereignty claims because GDP contraction affects 67M Britons daily"). A weighing card would carry a tag like "Economic magnitude outweighs — 4-8% GDP loss affects every household vs. abstract sovereignty gains" and sit as a standalone contention or as embedded taglines on each economic card.

### Offensive spikes
ABSENT: No cards preemptively answer likely Con positions such as "sovereignty benefits," "regulatory autonomy gains," "new trade deals with US/India," or "democratic legitimacy of the 2016 referendum." The Corbett 7/20 card acknowledges the "reset" negotiations but does not spike the "regulatory freedom" argument — it merely describes alignment costs. An offensive spike would be a card tagged "Regulatory autonomy myth — UK still follows EU rules to export but loses voting power" (citing Institute for Government 2024) placed in Contention 1 to undercut the Con's core offense before they read it.

### Defensive spikes
ABSENT: The case lacks frontlines embedded in the constructive against responses it will face: "rejoining requires Euro adoption," "EU won't accept UK back," "referendum mandate must be respected," or "transition costs of rejoining." Dihfford 25 (55% say Brexit wrong) and Corbett 7/20 (62% favor rejoining) serve as public opinion evidence but are not framed as defensive spikes (e.g., "Democratic mandate reversed — 62% now support rejoining, negating 2016 referendum claim"). A defensive spike card would carry a tag like "Rejoin referendum mandate — current majority support legitimizes reversal" and sit in Contention 2 or 3.

### Collapse flexibility
**Present but narrow:** The case offers three distinct link chains — (1) GDP/growth underperformance (HCL 20: 4% GDP loss; Gilchrist 24: 5% growth gap; Pettinger 7/18: stagflation signals), (2) Consumer cost-of-living (Davey 23: 1/3 food inflation from Brexit; 250£/household), (3) Democratic legitimacy shift (Dihfford 25: 55% say Brexit wrong; Corbett 7/20: 62% favor rejoining). However, all three collapse to "economic harm + public opinion" — no independent impact scenarios (e.g., national security, scientific research collaboration, Northern Ireland protocol stability, climate policy coordination, financial services passporting). If Con wins "economic models uncertain" or "voters prioritize immigration control," the entire case collapses. A fourth contention on "Geopolitical influence" (e.g., UK loses voice in EU standards-setting affecting global tech/AI regulation) or "Northern Ireland peace dividend" would provide true collapse flexibility.

### Warrant depth
**Mixed — strong on mechanism cards, weak on claim-only cards:** **HCL 20** provides full warrant: "higher barriers → larger negative impact" with OBR methodology (external study meta-analysis) and quantified scenarios (deal: -4% GDP; no-deal: -6%). **Davey 23** provides full warrant: CEP diff-in-diff design comparing EU vs. non-EU food price trends, isolating 3.5pp Brexit effect. **Gilchrist 24** provides full warrant: Goldman's three-channel decomposition (trade -15%, investment shortfall, labor supply elasticity reduction) with counterfactual comparator countries. **Barfield 21** provides mechanism warrant: "no mutual recognition chapter → safety testing/checks → 8-9% ad valorem equivalent." **Adam 18** is claim-dense but warrant-thin: asserts "Brexit is a mess" and "self-fulfilling policy" with polling snapshots but no causal mechanism for economic harm. **Pettinger 7/18** is claim-heavy: "unexpectedly bad news," "underperforming for years," "stock market not real economy" — no causal links to Brexit specifically (conflates COVID, energy crisis, policy errors). **Corbett 7/20** describes single market mechanics well (warrant for alignment costs) but asserts "GDP 4-5% smaller" without citing the specific study. **Dihfford 25** and **Andrianova 16** are pure claim/cite — polling numbers and Nobel laureate quote without warrants for *why* opinion shifted or *why* Pissarides predicts "massive bump."

### Internal consistency
**Consistent framing, no direct contradictions:** All cards support "Brexit caused net economic harm" and "public opinion has shifted toward rejoining."
