3] Moral responsibility requires free will, Van Inwagen 83:
van Inwagen [van Inwagen, Peter. “An Essay on Free Will.” Published 1983] //ReNSU SF
The answer to this question is a philosophical commonplace. If we do not have free will, then there is no such thing as moral responsibility. This proposition, one might think, certainly deserves to be a commonplace<mark>. If someone charges you with</mark>, say, <mark>lying</mark>, and if <mark>you can convince him that it was</mark> simply <mark>not within your power not to lie</mark>, then it would seem that you have done all that is necessary to absolve yourself of responsibility for lying. Your accuser cannot say, "I concede it was not within your power not to lie; none the less you ought not to have lied". Ought, as the saying goes, implies can. (Of course, it is unlikely that anyone would believe you if you said that it was not within your power not to lie, but that is not the point.) Similarly, if someone charges you with not having done something he maintains you ought to have done, he must withdraw his charge if you can convince him that you couldn't have done it. If, for example, he charges you with not having spoken up when a word might have saved Jones's reputation, he must withdraw his charge if you can convince him that you were bound and gagged while Jones was being maligned. (These simple facts are actually a bit too simple. An agent may have been unable to perform a certain act at a certain time, but—owing to his abilities with respect to acts that were or might have been performed at earlier times—he may once have been able so to arrange matters that he would have been able to perform that act at that time. For example, I may have been unable to contribute to a certain charity yesterday because I was locked in a bank vault that can't be opened from the inside. But if it should transpire that I had shut myself into the vault in order to avoid the representatives of the charity, few people would regard my having been locked in the vault as providing me with an adequate excuse for not contributing. The reason is easy to see: though there may be a sense in which it is true that I couldn't have contributed to the charity, there was none the less a time-- before I shut myself in—at which I could so have arranged matters that I should have been able to contribute to it when the time to do so rolled round. In the sequel, I shall ignore the possibility of cases like the "bank vault" case in order to avoid unnecessary detail in the statement of my argument.) It would seem to follow from these considerations that without free will there is no moral responsibility: if moral responsibility exists, then someone is morally responsible for something he has done or for something he has Ieft undone<mark>; to be morally responsible for some act</mark> or failure to act <mark>is at least to be able to have acted otherwise</mark>, whatever else it may involve; to be able to have acted otherwise is to have free will. Therefore, if moral responsibility exists, someone has free will. <mark>Therefore, if no one has free will, moral responsibility does not exist.</mark>

“Expert Political Judgment” is not a work of media criticism. Tetlock is a psychologist—he teaches at Berkeley—and his conclusions are based on a long-term study that he began twenty years ago. He picked <mark>two hundred</mark> and <mark>eighty-four people</mark> <mark>who</mark> made their living “commenting or <mark>offeri</mark>ng <mark>advice</mark> on political and economic trends,” and he started asking them to assess the probability that various things would or would not come to pass, both in the areas of the world in which they specialized and in areas about which they were not expert. Would there be a nonviolent end to apartheid in South Africa? Would Gorbachev be ousted in a coup? Would the United States go to war in the Persian Gulf? Would Canada disintegrate? (Many experts believed that it would, on the ground that Quebec would succeed in seceding.) And so on. By the end of the study, in 2003, the experts had made 82,361 forecasts. Tetlock also asked questions designed to determine how they reached their judgments, how they reacted when their predictions proved to be wrong, how they evaluated new information that did not support their views, and how they assessed the probability that rival theories and predictions were accurate. Tetlock got a statistical handle on his task by putting most of the forecasting questions into a “three possible futures” form. The respondents <mark>were</mark> asked <mark>to rate</mark> the <mark>probability of three</mark> alternative <mark>outcomes</mark>: the persistence of the status quo, more of something (political freedom, economic growth), or less of something (repression, recession). And he measured his experts on two dimensions: how good they were at guessing probabilities (did all the things they said had an x per cent chance of happening happen x per cent of the time?), and how accurate they were at predicting specific outcomes. <mark>The results were unimpressive</mark>. On the first scale, <mark>the experts performed worse than</mark> they would have <mark>if they</mark> had simply assigned <mark>an equal probability to all</mark> three <mark>outcomes</mark>—if they had given each possible future a thirty-three-per-cent chance of occurring. Human beings who spend their lives studying the state of the world, in other words, are poorer forecasters than dart-throwing monkeys, who would have distributed their picks evenly over the three choices. Tetlock also found that specialists are not significantly more reliable than non-specialists in guessing what is going to happen in the region they study. Knowing a little might make someone a more reliable forecaster, but Tetlock found that knowing a lot can actually make a person less reliable. “We reach the point of diminishing marginal predictive returns for knowledge disconcertingly quickly,” he reports. “In this age of academic hyperspecialization, there is no reason for supposing that contributors to top journals—distinguished political scientists, area study specialists, economists, and so on—are any better than journalists or attentive readers of the New York Times in ‘reading’ emerging situations.” And the more famous the forecaster the more overblown the forecasts. “Experts in demand,” Tetlock says, “were more overconfident than their colleagues who eked out existences far from the limelight.”

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The 1NC contains no pre-written impact calculus, magnitude/probability/timeframe comparisons, or "even if" framing that would allow cards to extend weighing in the back half. Every contention ends at the internal link (no free will, no objective ethics, rule-following paradox) without connecting to a terminal impact or explaining why that internal link outweighs the affirmative’s likely rejoining-EU advantages (e.g., economic growth, geopolitical stability). A card filling the gap would be tagged **"Weighing – Moral skepticism outweighs policy outcomes: without objective ought, no policy action can be justified, making EU rejoining incoherent"** (author/year) and placed after the Street 06 or Van Inwagen 83 evidence to turn the philosophical framework into offensive weighing.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No card in the 1NC preempts or interacts with affirmative positions specific to the resolution (e.g., EU single-market benefits, Northern Ireland protocol resolution, regulatory alignment, geopolitical influence). The Langseth 08 and Street 06 evidence are generic meta-ethical critiques that do not mention the EU, the UK, or rejoining. A spike would look like **"EU rejoining requires objective moral obligation – Street 06 proves reflective equilibrium cannot ground intergovernmental duties"** (author/year) placed as a sub-point under Contention 2 to directly answer the affirmative’s "ought" framing.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The 1NC has no embedded frontlines to anticipated affirmative responses (e.g., "compatibilism solves free will," "pragmatic ought ≠ moral ought," "rule-following paradox doesn’t apply to institutional rules"). The Kiekeben 96 and Coyne 12 cards assert hard determinism but include no "even if compatibilism" or "even if ought implies can is conventional" blocks. A defensive spike would be a card tagged **"Compatibilist free will sufficient for moral responsibility – Fischer 94: guidance control requires only reasons-responsiveness, not alternate possibilities"** (author/year) inserted after Van Inwagen 83 to preempt the most common 2AC rebuttal.

### Evidence quality
**Street 06** – Sharon Street, “A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value,” *Philosophical Studies* (January 2006), pp. 118-121. **Executes criterion:** Peer-reviewed philosophy journal article by a tenured NYU philosopher; the excerpt shows the full warrant chain (evolutionary contamination → rational reflection contaminated → evaluative judgments likely false) rather than a mere claim. However, the evidence is 19 years old and the case offers no newer meta-ethics updates (e.g., Enoch 2011, Bedke 2020), so it may lose a recency tiebreak.

Would lose an evidence-comparison tiebreak against a 2023 *Nature Neuroscience* study or a 2021 *Philosophical Review* compatibilist defense.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The 1NC is structurally pinned to a single link chain: *no free will → no moral responsibility → no "ought" → resolution negated*. There are no alternative impact scenarios (e.g., economic sovereignty DA, democratic deficit DA, regulatory burden DA) or independent philosophical frameworks (e.g., contractualism, rule-consequentialism) that would allow the 2NC to collapse to a different contention if the determinism link is beaten. A second contention tagged **"Rejoining sacrifices UK regulatory autonomy – Booth 2023: EU state-aid rules block green industrial policy"** (author/year) would give the 2NC a policy-based collapse option.

### Warrant depth
**Langseth 08** – Jonathan Langseth, “Wittgenstein's Account of Rule-Following and Its Implications,” *Stance* Vol. 1 (April 1, 2008). **Executes criterion:** The excerpt quotes the actual Wittgensteinian mechanism: "a rule, in order to be a rule, must be able to be broken… If interpretation was what determined correct use, there would be no incorrect application of a rule." This explains *why* rules underdetermine application (the paradox) rather than merely asserting it.

**Van Inwagen 83** – Peter van Inwagen, *An Essay on Free Will* (1983). **Executes criterion:** The card walks through the "ought implies can" principle with concrete examples (lying, bound-and-gagged, bank-vault) to show the logical entailment: moral responsibility → ability to do otherwise → free will. Each step is warranted, not just claimed.

**Menand 05** – Louis Menand, “Everybody's an Expert,” *The New Yorker* (Nov. 28, 2005). **Fails criterion:** The tag "Consequences empirically impossible to predict" is not warranted by the excerpt. The evidence describes Tetlock’s finding that *experts* forecast poorly, not that *consequences of EU rejoining* are unpredictable. No bridge from political forecasting in general to this specific policy is provided.

### Internal consistency
**INCONSISTENT:** The case simultaneously argues (1) moral rules cannot be objectively followed (Langseth 08), (2) all evaluative judgments are likely false (Street 06), and (3) "ought implies can" is a binding conceptual truth (Van Inwagen 83). If Street 06 is correct, the very judgment "ought implies can" is an evaluative judgment contaminated by evolutionary influences and therefore likely false—undermining the Van Inwagen warrant. The ASPEC shell ("The affirmative… must specify which United Kingdom") also presumes a determinate semantic rule for "United Kingdom," contradicting Langseth’s claim that rules underdetermine application. No framing card reconciles these tensions.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 3/10** — The case has two well-warranted philosophy cards (Langseth, Van Inwagen) but lacks embedded weighing, offensive/defensive spikes, collapse flexibility, and internal consistency; evidence quality is mixed with a weak op-ed and outdated meta-ethics; the ASPEC shell contradicts the case’s own rule-skepticism.
