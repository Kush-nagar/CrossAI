Baudrillard interpreted talk of the end of history to mean that history had vanished rather than coming to an end, and he proposed three explanations for that disappearance. During the long phase of our progressive liberation, we were able to measure that liberation against its preexisting limits—boundaries of capital accumulation and exchange, boundaries of sexual propriety that gave meaning to the question of removing them. <mark>What makes history</mark> in the usual sense, Baudrillard suggested, <mark>was</mark> “a degree of <mark>slowness</mark>” through which successive <mark>events could be measured and processed</mark>, <mark>acquiring meaning </mark>and direction. But he sensed that a tendency to the “total dissemination and circulation” of every event, a tendency enabled by modern media, would undo the ability of history to put events together in a meaningful way. Indeed, he claimed that the means for a disorienting acceleration of events was present “already, here and now—in the shape of our computers, circuits and networks.”7 Writing about the transformation of events into particles that could achieve immediate, worldwide transmission would have seemed far-fetched in 1992. But our disaggregation of news into separately shared stories, tweets, snaps, and posts tracks Baudrillard’s sense rather well. The same “multiplication and <mark>saturation of exchanges</mark>,” he suggested, would eventually be associated not with the acceleration of events but with a <mark>slow</mark>ing <mark>down of events through</mark> the <mark>difficulty</mark> of <mark>distinguishing</mark> the <mark>meaningful ones from</mark> the <mark>minutiae</mark>. The <mark>effects of history “are accelerating,”</mark> he wrote, “<mark>but</mark> its <mark>meaning is slowing</mark> inexorably.8¶ The proximate occasion of end-of-history talk was the end of the Cold War through the internal collapse of one of its two sides. This opening up of the Eastern bloc thrilled Western commentators who envisioned an imminent new birth or rebirth of liberty. Two hundred years after the French Revolution, when the glory and power of revolution had become only a memory in the West, it would be possible to witness it anew. Baudrillard’s pessimism was dismissed as a characteristic feint, but what he described was not a merely negative view but rather a paradox of liberation experienced in distinct ways. A culture that had already liberated itself from every constraint encountered the post-Soviet world in which liberty had been killed and its memory frozen. The result, Baudrillard suggested, would be very uncertain: the West did not have on offer the steady sequence of events that had built Western liberty, but the end-stage products of a culture drowning in pornography and used cars, searching for a new market. Undoing the twentieth-century totalitarianisms would in a strange way lead to a whitewashing of the previous century, “as if everything that had taken place . . . were merely a hopeless imbroglio.”9¶ The opening of Eastern Europe to Western markets would take on an unusual character too, Baudrillard thought, because the West had already completed its major internal processes of liberation. The liberal economy of the post–Cold War West was, he wrote, “a market economy with no social force to battle against it, no competitive force to drive it on, no collective project to propel it into the future.”10 The key features of the modern economy—“production, the market, ideology, profit and utopia”—had already given way to a market “unreal and speculative, lacking even the notion of production, profit and progress.”11 Liberation from Soviet rule would not bring about the end of history in the sense of the completion of Western achievement. Instead it would accelerate the erasure of opposition to the financialized and increasingly surreal economy of the Western world.¶ Baudrillard’s criticism targeted the disjunction between Western rhetoric about liberty and the realities, present and future, of a world that had already liberated itself—a world “after the orgy,” with little remaining to do other than to clean up the mess and to recycle the signs of its past exploits. Much of the uniqueness of Baudrillard’s argument stems from his application of Marcel Mauss’s thought in The Gift (1925), especially its analysis of the potlatch ceremonies, in scenarios far afield from indigenous anthropology.12 In everything from the to and fro of great power conflict to the exchange of goods and signs in digital marketplaces, Baudrillard found the destructive one-upmanship of the potlatch ceremony.¶ The end of the Cold War, in this interpretation, was not the victory of liberalism and markets, and so it could not herald a world (as Fukuyama expected) in which liberal democracy offered the last remaining terms of political legitimation. Instead the Soviet Union offered the spectacle of its own dramatic implosion, a gift that could only be met by a similarly dramatic final offering—the “contamination” of the East with the final stage of our own economy, one ever more centered on the production and exchange of images rather than goods. The collapse of the East was a shocking event that suddenly forced the West to give everything it had.¶ The “end of history” thesis was a triumphant one because it saw no available political justifications except for liberal democracy. Baudrillard cautioned, however, that the political dissolution of the East, because it came about for internal reasons, presaged a period of further destabilization rather than the consolidation of liberal democracy. Democratic values had not suddenly become triumphant, for there had been no new battle or victory to make them “held dear and dearly bought.”13 The seeming inevitability of democratic values was instead a sign of their cheapness, their liquid and easy availability—a frenzy of democratic speculation that foretold an eventual crash. The crash, Baudrillard suspected, would come about through the resistance of the vanquished to the sincere adoption of democratic values, and the viral spread, in the West, of the traits which had made Eastern bloc countries uninterested in Western values for so long. “It is possible, then,” he observed, “that the countries of Eastern Europe will pass on to us this model of viral collapse, of a virulence deconstructive of power. In exchange, we might pass on to them our liberal virus, our compulsion for objects and images, media and communication, a virus, in our case, which devastates civil society.”14 What might move in the Western direction, he went on to suggest, would be “the end of all democratic illusions” and the adoption of the East’s lack of democratic faith.15 Robust belief in liberal democracy as a “Good” had normally required framing it against an identifiable “Evil”—the specter of Communism and its political location in the Soviet Union. Breaking down the barrier between the West and the East, Baudrillard cautioned, would instead lead to the spread of Communism’s “model of dysfunctioning and of sudden, violent destructuring.”16 Freed from having to face a political enemy squarely, capitalism would even more aggressively achieve the “generalized exchange” at which Communism had aimed.¶ Baudrillard was outlining a dynamic in which market-based exchange would spread to the four corners of the earth without any longer experiencing the resistance that had been required to legitimate it in the first place. Accordingly—and here lay Baudrillard’s twist—that spread would occur without ideological battle and thus without triumph. Liberal political economy and democratic mechanisms of government would advance—but only because they found no resistance. Not one to shy away from further twists, Baudrillard added that the lack of belief in liberal democracy would in fact benefit its spread.¶ Baudrillard’s position diverged from the typical postmodern accounts, especially as they were received in the United States, that interpreted Jean-François Lyotard’s “incredulity toward metanarratives” as the sign of a coming ennui. This ennui quickly came to be understood by American critics of postmodernism as a kind of luxury good of the tenured and disaffected, insufficient to describing the thrilling era that had begun with the Netscape IPO. Baudrillard instead anticipated a combination of the wild spread of communications technology and an internal doubt about the meaning or purpose of the West. “Beneath their apparent mobility and acceleration,” he said, “they have come to a stop in their hearts and their aims. That is, indeed, why they are accelerating, but they are doing so out of inertia.”17 Freed from the burden of ideology, the liberalism of markets and human rights could spread for lack of credible alternatives, because credible opponents had disappeared. Uncredible opponents, however, could become more numerous and even proliferate.¶ The Play of Global Antagonism¶ In the dynamic Baudrillard identified, the commercial phenomena cited early on as evidence for the spread of Western values were not proof of the triumph of liberal democracy. Instead Baudrillard spoke of a global “hegemony” characteristic of the growth of networks aided by and reinforcing the communications technologies that built them. Decades earlier Baudrillard had already rejected the Marxist analysis of “capitalism” as a political-economic system of domination that was capable of critique.18 Faced with the failure of the May 1968 student revolts in France, Baudrillard concluded that resistance and critique had become impossible because consumer technology—first television and later the internet in all its forms—was immersive rather than alienating. The proper way to think about the breakdown of trade barriers from the 1990s onward was, then, as the expansion of an immersive system of consumption designed to facilitate the spread of global brands, and the replacement of traditional systems of cultural reference with a new set of signs provided by marketers. “Production, the market, ideology, profit and utopia,” wrote Baudrillard in 1992, “were all modern. The competitive capitalist economy was modern. Ours, unreal and speculative, lacking even the notion of production, profit and progress, is no longer modern, but post-modern.”19¶ The dramatic expansion of markets justified itself in the name of goals from the heroic stage of capitalist advance. It used (and uses) the terms of production, profit, competition, and entrepreneurship, even while the tendency of established Western firms has instead been toward reducing labor cost, preserving favorable tax advantages, and rent seeking. For many new firms in the technology space, the entrepreneurial goal has been to “monetize” as many types of human activity as possible. In recent years that goal has tended toward the creation of brand platforms whose aim is to touch human activity at an ever-increasing number of fee-generating points. The reason that straightforward critique or exposure of this strategy will not work, said Baudrillard, was that capitalists themselves had become cynical about many features of the consumer market. “Your money interests me!” was the openly cynical slogan of the Banque Nationale de Paris in the 1970s, he noted. “Denouncing capital and all of the banking mechanisms was nothing new, the scandalous feature was that the banker himself had said it. . . . It came from the dominant power and enjoyed complete immunity. It could admit its ‘crime’ in broad daylight.”20 Baudrillard coined the playful phrase “the intelligence of evil” to describe the new, more effective cunning of an approach to innovation that consisted primarily in the opening up (whether they wanted it or not) of new consumer markets, combined with the “transparency of evil” in which cynical motives were openly paraded as slogans. Such slogans continue to appear, as in Cadillac’s recent launch of a subscription car service with the slogan “Access is the new ownership”: not only did they openly parade their motive (to remain the owners and sell access) but they used it as advertisement.¶ Only occasionally, as in the recent political controversy surrounding Facebook, have events become configured such that this open secret of contemporary capitalism is considered shocking. It was precisely the rapid expansion of network technology that Baudrillard expected to become “viral”—a term he used long before it characterized how trends and information now spread. When addressing the role networks would play in his 1997 interview Paroxysm, he would say that the individual subject had “lost its freedom, it’s no longer master of its origins or its ends, it’s the hostage of the network. Priority is with the network, not with the subscribers to the network. Identity is on the network’s side, not on that of the individual.”21 At a time when most users’ experience of the internet was limited to email (instant messaging would only begin that year), Baudrillard’s claim would have seemed fantastical. But now it is becoming more obvious that networks and their owners have considerable “power”—something that they were not supposed to have so long as the classical model of liberal market interactions was considered adequately descriptive.¶ While the stated trends of the 1990s were toward the growth of multinational brands (leading to such amusements as the Golden Arches Theory of Conflict Prevention), the advent of networks would soon offer the illusion of a return of individuality. From the beginning, Silicon Valley’s most successful networking companies followed a two-pronged strategy: to increase the individualization or personalization of the user’s experience, and to monetize that personalization based on the new acquisition of detailed user information. “Behind the immateriality of the technologies of virtual reality, of the digital and the screen,” Baudrillard wrote in 2004, “there lies a hidden injunction . . . : that of a heightened participation, of an interactive investment that may reach dizzying proportions.”22 This strategy was the open secret of Facebook and, though in a different manner, Google as well. The shift toward a highly personalized consumer experience seemed to disprove the expectation that mass production of consumer goods would lead to a type of conformity. In reality it led to a more sophisticated classification of existing consumer types, as companies sought to know the consumer better than the consumer knew himself.¶ The ideology projected over this new stage of consumer experience was one of empowering individual choice and, above all, “transparency.” Baudrillard had identified “immersion, immanence and immediacy” as the signal characteristics of the digital world.23 But each of these, he thought, were in the process of creating an economic world markedly different from the classical model in which the laborer’s exploitation was experienced as alienation from the product one had helped to create. Consider by contrast the type of exchanges mediated by the technology platforms that have established themselves over the last fifteen or twenty years. Individuals or consumers now trade a limitless amount of information about themselves in exchange for information about the world around them (e.g., traffic), information about other people, and an increasingly enveloping stream of (apparently) highly tailored products and services. Baudrillard described this economic form as “an exchange that is not only general but total—completely freed from value and markets.”24 The hiddenness of these data exchanges (imagine if your phone screen flashed like a traffic camera every time it collected information on you) indicates how far markets have drifted from how they were classically conceived, with buyer and seller haggling over some product. Now the exchange is hidden by the end-user license agreement, and peeks through only at moments, like the recent Facebook scandal, when political circumstances warrant.¶ Baudrillard was attempting to diagnose a cultural condition that prioritized image over substance, appearance over reality, media consumption over firsthand experience, surgical strikes and “kinetic military action” over war, production over seduction, even (as would also prove prescient) pornography over sex. The universal exchange and idealization of transparency which facilitated this condition was a utopian project that had a home, Baudrillard thought, in America.¶ America between the Utopias¶ Like his account of the illusory end, however, Baudrillard’s trip to America, recounted in his book Amérique (published in English thirty years ago), produced observations out of place in the new American morning of the 1980s.25 America, Baudrillard said baldly, was “utopia achieved,” a society “built on the idea that it is the realization of everything the others have dreamt of—justice, plenty, rule of law, wealth, freedom.”26 But it is the conjunction of utopianism and pragmatism that make Baudrillard’s America: it is the place where religious sectarianism had the chance to build its utopia—forced by the emptiness of America to meet the challenge through the pragmatic realization of utopia. The sects in Europe that aspired to build a world in their own name never had the chance. But even the pragmatic task of town planning across the American West had the feel of the realization of a sacred destiny. Even smaller expressions of American freedom—our many small associations driven intently by a particular cause—would in Baudrillard’s view pursue “moral” or perhaps humanitarian ends. In their desire to do good effectively, in their freedom to do it, in their insistence on their righteousness—in all of these ways they include the elements that previously would have been proper to sects.¶ In his account of a “utopian” America, Baudrillard might be said to be describing something passé, naively driven by the promise of further commercial enterprise and more effective government. But he indicates that the projects that Americans undertook naturally changed or evolved after the, so to speak, initial building. According to Baudrillard, what is most characteristic about American society now is its proliferation of technology. “It is not so much,” he says, “in the operation of institutions as in the freeing of technologies and images that the glorious form of American reality is to be found: in the immoral dynamic of images, in the orgy of goods and services, an orgy of power and useless energy . . . in which the spirit of advertising is more to the fore than Tocqueville’s public spirit.” “The liberated man,” he concludes, “is not the one who is freed in his ideal reality, his inner truth, or his transparency; he is the man who changes spaces, who circulates, who changes sex, clothes, and habits according to fashion, rather than morality, and who changes opinions not as his conscience dictates but in response to opinion polls.”27¶ Today there seems to be a chasm opening open between those who are satisfied with this liberation and those who are not, between those who revel in it and those who do not. The liberation Baudrillard describes is, in one sense, a much wider phenomenon than one might think from his description of the “liberated man”: it is not someone who has freed himself from any internal pressures or obligations that might limit him, but rather someone who gives way to the universal circulation, movement, and parade of imagery that is characteristic of hypermodern societies. In this sense, too, the world of 2018 is more suffused with constant imagery, presented in ever more tailored ways to specific persons—whether as consumers or as citizens—than it was when Baudrillard described it in 1986. But there is a sharp distinction between those who can maintain the pretense of thriving in the world of images, who seem to move with ease among different locations and different “games”—hard-nosed politico by day, committed Christian by weekend—and those who cannot.¶ Baudrillard in 1986 described or predicted a situation in which economic success in parts of the First World would lead to the “disintensification” of other parts:¶ Human rights have been won everywhere. The world is almost entirely liberated; there is nothing left to fight for. And yet at the same time entire social groups are being laid waste from the inside (individuals too). Society has forgotten them and now they are forgetting themselves. . . . This is the Fourth World. Entire sectors of our modern societies, entire countries in the Third World now fall into this Fourth World desert zone. But whereas the Third World still had a political meaning (even if it was a resounding world-wide failure), the Fourth World has none. It is transpolitical. When in June 2016, the people of the United Kingdom (UK) were asked in a referendum whether to remain or leave the European Union (EU), the majority (51.9%) – with a very small margin – voted in favor to leave the Union. Followed by complex negotiations and massive political turbulences, the UK formally left the EU in February 2020. However, after four years of Brexit negotiations and uncertainty, a phase of intense trading negotiations started to draw up the future relationship between the EU and UK until the end of the transition phase to find a final compromise for future cooperation and trade relations. Regardless of the outcome, the departure of Britain triggers severe changes and disturbances on both sides of the Channel. It will open a new chapter of cross-channel relations not only with long-lasting political and economic consequences but also for the environment and future environmental policy-making in Europe. Doubtless, the departure of Britain will change the face of the United Kingdom but it will also leave its traces on the European Union, causing distortions in the short but also in the long term.1
The <mark>EU</mark> is <mark>known for</mark> its <mark>leadership</mark> role <mark>in</mark> global <mark>environment</mark>al protection efforts, acknowledging that climate change and environmental degradation pose a real threat to human mankind and also urging to decarbonize production and consumption. In various areas, the EU came forward with some environmental policies that did not exist before or at least not in such a huge region (e.g., acid rain, the ozone layer, climate change, hazardous chemicals...). Over the past four decades, the EU developed and implemented major environmental concepts and principles that find application in environmental policymaking around the world (i.e., <mark>precautionary principle</mark>, <mark>burden sharing</mark>, <mark>waste hierarchy</mark>, proximity principle for treatment of waste, etc.). The Union has <mark>one of the most mature</mark> and coherent environmental <mark>frameworks in the world</mark>. Policy-making within the Union also helps member states to share best practices. In the phase of policy formation, Member States can learn from each other's challenges and policy responses. This process allows opting for the best-practice policy to be implemented. In the past, the realm of <mark>environmental policy-making</mark> served as a <mark>driver for advancing integration</mark>, as Member States were <mark>more willing to hand over</mark> policy environment-related <mark>competences</mark> to the EU <mark>than in other areas</mark>. European environmental policy-making is not only more long-term oriented than those of national governments but also allows large-scaled efforts. Furthermore, it is more <mark>resistant to failures</mark>.2 A major source of the EU’s international influence derives from its market and regulatory power based on common environmental standards.3 However, participation in the Union is based on compromises, and with the departure of a very powerful entity, the EU will be influenced in several aspects. Britain´s departure is expected to trigger serious changes to several realms of the Union, such as for the economy, the financial sector, international trade, international relations, but also for environment-related issues (e.g., climate change, renewable energies, circular economy).
In 2015, the EU adopted its first Circular Economy package that should bring the EU economy in line with sustainability. And with the newly proposed EU Green Deal in 2019, the EU set out an ambitious target to achieve a climate-neutral Europe by 2050. And this goal can only be achieved by rapid progress in establishing a circular economy.
Given that the EU has more experience with enlargement than with reduction, Brexit represents a quite unique phenomenon worth studying. While several countries have joined the Union over the yars, only the UK has left it. This paper seeks to provide answers on how Brexit will affect the EU environmental policy-making, in particular, it focuses on the consequences in the field of the circular economy. Therefore, the work analyzes and evaluates the consequences of Brexit in terms of establishing the European circular economy in two realms: economy and politics. The paper seeks to shed light on possible short-term and long-term consequences.
European Union, Circular Economy, and the EU Green Deal
The concept of circular economy (<mark>CE</mark>) <mark>has</mark> enjoyed <mark>widespread support</mark> from across Europe as it combines economic benefits (increased competitiveness, resource efficiency, new jobs, etc.) with environmental benefits (waste reduction, reducing greenhouse gas emissions output, etc.). Though not lucidly defined yet, the circular economy concept builds on economic, natural, and social capital and redefines growth by focusing on society-wide benefits.4 The concept of CE seeks to design out waste and pollution, decouple economic activities from material demand and consumption of resources, and keep valuable materials as long as possible in the system.5 Thus, CE aims at a reduction, reuse, recycling and recovery. CE redefines waste as input material for other industries and focuses on promoting the use of secondary raw materials. This not only includes recycling efforts but includes a view on the overall life-cycle of products. Besides, CE spurs innovation and provides opportunities for new business models and growth.6
In the current linear mode of production, growth is propelled by natural resource extraction that also drives up greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions.7 <mark>So far, progress</mark> towards a circular economy <mark>is slow</mark>. The global circularity rate stands at about 9.1%. 8 However, with the global population projected to cross the 8.5 billion mark in 2030, demand for goods and services will necessarily grow. Annually, about 88 billion tonnes of materials are extracted from the ground, and about 2.01 billion tonnes of garbage is disposed of by urban residents worldwide.9 In 2019, emissions from industrial activities and the burning of fossil fuels will pump an estimated 36.8 billion metric tonnes of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere.10 And according to the U.N. World Meteorological Organization, the CO2 concentration levels in the atmosphere hit a new record in 2018 by rising to 407.8 parts per million. 11 Since <mark>material management accounts for</mark> about <mark>two-thirds of</mark> worldwide <mark>emissions</mark>, the establishment of a circular economy also supports climate mitigation and resource efficiency plays directly into climate action. 12 The reuse of materials (e.g., cement, steel, aluminum, and plastics) can be central to lower greenhouse gas emissions. Accordingly, <mark>improved circular loops</mark> play a <mark>crucial</mark> role in achieving the goals of the Paris Climate Agreement. If the current path is continued, there is no way to contain temperature rise to a 1.5ºC above pre-industrial levels as laid out in the UN Paris Agreement.
Based on a report by SYSTEMIQ (2017), <mark>scaling</mark> the circular economy in Europe <mark>could offer</mark> investment opportunities totaling €320 billion.13 In a circular economy, resources are used in a more sustainable way, the demand for primary raw materials is cut, resulting in increased material efficiency. Increased usage of secondary raw materials can substitute the industry’s demand for primary raw materials. While raw materials demand could be lowered by an estimated 20%, the EU <mark>GDP</mark> would <mark>increase by 3%.</mark> The promotion of circularity in the manufacturing sector of complex consumer durables could bring material savings worth some €553 billion in the EU alone.14 According to a report by European Parliament (2017), the transition to a circular economy could <mark>create</mark> between 1.2 million and <mark>3 million new jobs</mark> by 2030 <mark>and</mark> cut greenhouse gas emissions by more than 500 million tonnes between 2015 and 2035. A study by Material Economics (2018) estimates that a circular economy could <mark>halve</mark> EU industrial <mark>emissions</mark> by 2050.15
Accordingly, circular economy efforts have become integral parts of recent environmental talks in Europe and beyond. In December 2015, the European Commission adopted its first Circular Economy Package aimed at supporting businesses and consumers to engage a transition to a more circular economy.16 The package was followed by the Circular Economy Action Plan and proposals that cover the full lifecycle of products, from design, production, and consumption to waste management and the development of a stronger secondary raw materials market. The initiatives resulted in a series of legislative and non-legislative actions, such as for waste management, plastics, fertilizers, marine litter, critical raw materials and indicators measuring circularity. The EU also set out targets for recycling (65% of municipal and 75% of packaging waste by 2030) and implemented a ban on landfilling of separately collected waste by 2030. According to the EU plans, the competitiveness of key industry sectors including manufacturing, waste management, and recycling will be strengthened by the Circular Economy Package.
The EU Green Deal
In December 2019, the new European Commission proposed its Green Deal with the aim to achieve carbon-neutrality by 2050. 17 However, achieving the net-zero GHG emissions objective by 2050 requires a profound transformation of value chains across the EU economy based on innovative production processes and new business models. To achieve this ambitious goal, industries in all member countries have to make significant contributions. The new Commission President von der Leyen claimed the European Green Deal to be "Europe's man on the moon moment"18, describing climate policy as Europe’s new growth strategy. According to von der Leyen, the former growth model based on fossil fuels and pollution is out of date. The new Green Deal which is based on ten major pillars not only includes climate-neutrality by 2050 but also the establishment of a circular economy (see Figure 1). As laid out by several studies, the establishment of a circular economy is a feasible option to reduce emissions.19 A large-scale switch from fossil-based technologies to low-carbon technology, the expansion of renewable energies, and the promotion of energy-efficiency measures are envisioned. Since efforts will result in increased material demands further triggering greenhouse gas emissions, a net-zero emission reduction objective is only achievable with a drastic system change in material and resource management based on circularity.
The New Green Deal for Europe comprises three distinct institutions: The Green Public Works (GPW) (an investment program), the Environmental Union (package of legislation to align policy with scientific consensus), and the Environmental Justice Commission (EJC) an independent body to research, monitor, and advise EU policymakers. The latter is designed as an international body to ensure that the green transition is also a just one. By its broad mandate, the EJC has the power to set new international standards for research and reporting on environmental injustices. However, the EJC takes only an advisory role, providing data on environmental changes for other EU institutions or developing new indicators to evaluate policies. The GPW aims at delivering Europe’s economic transformation through a large investment program by including aspects of environmental justice and focusing on decarbonizing Europe’s economy, reversing biodiversity loss and guaranteeing decent jobs across the continent. The program is financed entirely through green bonds issued by the European Investment Bank. The Environmental Union aims at a broader target – a systemic change by offering a comprehensive regulatory package to realign policies based on scientific consensus. It is aimed to foster Europe´s position as a global green leader.
In the <mark>Green Deal</mark>, the EU <mark>commits </mark>itself <mark>to lead by example</mark>, setting standards for sustainable growth across global value chains, and using diplomacy, trade and development cooperation to advance climate action. The promotion of a Circular Economy aims to further reduce GHG emissions but also to create economic growth and to <mark>spur global competitiveness</mark>. The new EU circular economy approach based on new waste and recycling laws will not only represent half of the EU´s efforts to achieve net-zero carbon emissions by 2050 but also include measures to ensure access to raw material supplies. Access to critical raw materials (CRMs) used in digital and clean technologies was named as a strategic security question in the European Green Deal. Certain CRMs are irreplaceable in solar panels, wind turbines, electric vehicles, and energy-efficient lighting, essential technologies for the establishment of a circular economy and the promotion of energy transition. With the mining sector on the decrease in the EU and global demand on the rise, urban mining practices and the circular economy approach gain spotlight in the EU. In times of limited access to material resources, closing industrial loops represents a promising reply to ensure the development of sustainable economy.

Consequences of Brexit for EU Circular Economy Efforts
Although a circular economy is perceived as a necessity to ensure the prosperity and wellbeing of human mankind in the future, its establishment is not self-evolving. Change requires strong political commitment and is often based on economic rationale. With the departure of the UK from the Union, we will see changes in the direction of how EU circular policies evolve.20 The section below elaborates in detail the expected consequences of Britain´s departure for establishing the EU Circular Economy by focusing on two major dimensions: the economic and the political realm.
The Economic Realm
The 2018 Global Environmental Performance Index lists solely European countries under the top 15.21 Switzerland ranks first, followed by France, Denmark, Malta, Sweden. The UK is put on the sixth position while Germany is ranked 13th. However, green and clean does not necessarily correlate with a high level of circularity. Burning waste for energy, as widely practiced in some countries, minimizes landfilling but is not improving recycling rates or establishing circular loops within the industry. In a circular economy, products remain as long as possible in the system, they are shared, reused, repaired and/or refurbished. But when they finally reach their end of life, they are dismantled, recycled and the materials are injected back in the economic cycle. However, CE not only focuses on waste management and recycling but taking a look at every stage of a product's life, starting from the mining of raw materials, design, production, consumption to waste management. By closing loops, a CE not only reduces the output of waste and emissions but also lower the overall demand for materials. To stay within planetary boundaries, by 2050 an average European will have to reduce by 80% the amount of natural resources they currently use for nutrition, housing, mobility and lifestyles (IEEP, 2019).
Based on the low self-sufficiency for raw materials in the EU, the physical trade balance is obviously asymmetric. By weight, the Union imports about three times more goods from non-EU countries than it exports. Hence, the Union is very dependent and vulnerable to the import of certain raw materials. According to Eurostat, import dependency climbed from 18.6% (2000) to 23.1% in 2018. To address the issue, in 2008 the Commission launched the Raw Material Initiative, a strategy to secure access to raw materials in the EU. In 2018, the Commission published a report highlighting the potential for a more circular usage of critical raw materials in the economy.22
A circular economy aims to maintain the value of products, materials and resources for as long as possible by returning them into the product cycle at the end of life while minimizing the production of waste. However, modern economies are heavily dependent on a steady and secure supply of raw materials and the demand for materials is increasing with economic development. A recent OECD (2019) report projects the demand for raw materials to more than double by 2060.23 The concept of CE stresses environmental and economic benefits gained by a more circular economic system, mainly by lowering the overall material demand and raw material consumption and increasing resource efficiency as well as developing a secondary raw material market.
The domestic material consumption (DMC) measures the annual amount of materials directly used by an economy.24 In 2000, the DMC of the Union was roughly 7,500 million tonnes (see Figure 2). After a decline due to the financial crisis in 2008, the EU-28 climbed to some 6,900 million tonnes in 2017. Across the EU, material consumption patterns vary largely depending on each Member States´ extraction of domestic resources, population density, living standards, economic development, living standards, the size of agriculture, manufacturing and construction industries, but also the size of its imports and exports market. The amount of consumed material per capita in the EU-28 varies largely. While Finland consumed the most material per (34.5), followed by Estonia (30.9) and Ireland (26.2), the UK only consumes 8.9 tonnes. Spain has the lowest consumption rate across the EU-28 with 8.4 tonnes. In 2018, the total DMC of the EU economy stood at about 13.8 tonnes per capita.25 And much of it still ends up as waste. In the EU, the amount of municipal waste generated per person in 2018 amounted to 492 kg (2008: 518 kg), and about 490 kg in 2017.26 Municipal waste production of the UK was with 463 kg below average. In 2004, the UK´s waste production peaked at 602 kg. The top three waste producers are Denmark (766 kg), Malta (640 kg) and Cyprus (637). At the bottom of the list, we find Poland (329 kg) and Romania (272 kg). The recycling rate of municipal waste of the EU-28 (2018) was at 47%. The top recycling countries are Germany (67.3%), Slovenia (58.9%), Austria (57.7%), while the UK shows a rate of 44.1%. Although not the core idea of a circular economy, recycling of materials helps to improve circularity as it can be re-introduced into the economy as new substances or products.

Digitalization provides new space for platform-based enterprises focusing on circular efforts, engaging in new business models based on renting or leasing.36 At the end of life, manufacturers take back the product including all the precious materials for refurbishing, reselling, or recycling. In this scenario, design for easy dismantling, refurbishing and repair is key. Companies are rethinking their business models and adapt the ownership structure of their products (e.g., pay per washes, pay per lux) and provide leased products.37 However, circular business models require a business environment based on sharing, mainly provided by the platform economy. <mark>The UK is leading</mark> efforts in developing platform-based <mark>business models.</mark> With the departure of the UK, experiences and insights are lost for EU policy-making. <mark>The EU lost a major innovative power</mark>, providing difficulties to be compensated.
The Political Realm
During its membership, the UK realized its potentials by actively shaping policy-making through leading the discussions on EU policy instruments. From the 1990s onwards, London turned into a driver for setting the EU’s environmental agenda, in some areas pushing for more action, in some areas blocking it - depending on its own national interests. As part of the Green Growth Group, London was supportive to actions combating climate change, and it became a leader within on climate change mitigation efforts. With Brexit the group weakens significantly and this may result in a decline of influence on the matter.
The UK was a <mark>key</mark> driver <mark>for</mark> installing the EU emissions trading scheme and pushed for more ambitious GHG reduction targets emissions by 2020 and 2030.38 Furthermore, the UK has taken a prominent role in shaping EU external environmental and <mark>climate</mark> policy and <mark>diplomacy at the international level</mark>. As the departure of Britain may lead to a readjustment of the coalitions within the Council, it could lead to a situation where EU environmental policy itself becomes less ambitious.
Britain´s EU membership provided a framework promoting transboundary cooperation, with keeping some room for local policy innovation. The UK actively directed the discussions towards more market-based approaches (e.g., environmental auditing and eco-labels).39 Britain directly benefited from the membership through cleaner air, water, and oceans and the development of green market systems and turned from an environmental laggard into a progressive environmental power pro-actively steering the discussions. However, with about 80% of the environment-related legislation coming from Brussels, London never forgot to point on the regulatory burden of environmental action during its membership. Now, the UK needs to completely rebuild its institutional capacities regarding environmental protection (e.g., DEPA) and Brexit has also intensified internal political turmoil, such as constitutional disputes between the Scottish, Wallisian and UK governments. Given the large disagreements among people about Brexit (urban vs. rural, pro-EU vs. anti-EU), there is an environmental divide emerging between (largely anti-EU) rural areas in favor of lowering environmental standards to attract business and pro-European urban areas in favor of upholding/tightening high environmental standards. In the mid- and long-run, there are concerns that the break-up may weaken (or slow down) environmental protection efforts on the island. Since the UK will be occupied mainly with internal issues, the EU will not be able to get much support from the UK on certain global environmental issues (e.g., circular economy, climate change). Consequently, future coalition-building becomes more difficult, as the UK government has new immediate priorities. This is underlined by London´s lack of interest in participation in the European Environment Agency. Given the importance of the build-up a pan-European monitoring system for circular economy, the UK´s long-term strategy is questioned.
Implications of Brexit for Political Dynamics in the Union
During the enlargement process, the influence of each individual state on decision-making has declined gradually. Thus, for gaining leverage over EU decision-making, building winning coalitions is essential. In the EU, groups of member states with more or less close ties form coalitions to influence the decision-making process. Basically, such coalitions emerge at an early stage, often during the informal approach to formal talks in the European Council and the Council of the European Union. And since proposals can only be rejected by a group, Britain’s departure will have a major impact on relations in the Council of the European as new coalitions need to be formed to advance with CE. According to the Lisbon Treaty, approval of decisions in the Council requires at least 55% of votes from Member States and 65% from share-of-population votes.
Britain clearly utilized its EU membership to steer European policy-making in its favor.40 While the country supported more than 97% of the legislative measures adopted by the Council between 2004 and 2016, London voted against proposals more often and showed relatively higher abstention scores (see Figure 6) than others. Germany is the second-largest veto player but on different issues than the UK. Closest with the UK on passing proposals come Denmark, Sweden, and the Netherlands.
In regards to environment-related decisions between 2010 and 2020, the UK never voted against a Council proposal, with only one abstention.41 Most votes against environment-related proposals come from Poland (6), Bulgaria, Cypress, Denmark, Hungary (4 each), followed by Malta, Lithuania and Slovakia (3 each).42

A Franco-German Coalition for Circular Economy?
As a result of Brexit, the importance of large member states (Germany, France, Italy, Spain, and Poland) increases, as their voting power gains are greater. A blocking minority can be achieved with 13 member states voting against, or at least four member states representing more than 35% of the EU’s population. France and Germany together represent 33.2% of the EU’s population. In a different constellation, France, Italy, and Spain together represent 39.24% of the European population and can therefore easily form a blocking minority (Kleinowski, 2019).
In the past, the Union's political integration was mainly driven by a Franco-German coalition. Paris and Berlin were the primary architects for closer integration in monetary, foreign, security, as well as justice affairs. 43 London often voiced skeptical and critical voices, attempting to steer discussions in its favor. Most of the <mark>British proposals</mark> have <mark>insisted</mark> that <mark>EU legislation should be efficient and market-oriented</mark> with a focus <mark>to reduce bureaucratic burden</mark>. London has relentlessly criticized the burden of the tight regulatory framework of the EU. Furthermore, British environmental NGOs have played a crucial role in the creation of EU-level ENGOs and influencing European environmental policy-making.44 London was an advocate for a reduction of regulations with the aim to reduce the bureaucratic costs for small and medium-sized enterprises in the internal market. Whereas London often called for more market-based instruments, Germany prefers approaches based on binding regulatory instruments. Brexit silences these critical voices, while others have more space. Poland is the largest Central and Eastern European member, and its solid economic performance provides Warsaw with legitimacy to join discussions. However, in the past Poland showed no strong engagement in promoting strict environmental policies (e.g., renewable energies). In the EU-27, the voice of climate laggards such as Poland, Hungary or the Czech Republic could turn louder. These countries blocked a new EU climate change target and refused to support writing the target into the EU’s strategic program for 2019-24. Sharing a communist past and poor environmental records, the Visegrád group could - led by Warsaw - further slowdown EU efforts in stepping up circular economy efforts.
In another option, we could see a coalition between Germany and the eight northern European member states. Finance ministers of these countries already met in 2018 to start talks about alternative proposals for reforming the Eurozone. On various matters, such as the environment, monetary, fiscal, EU budget, their viewpoints are close to Germany's. However, despite this close relationship with its Northern neighbors, Berlin will not risk its uniquely close bilateral relationship with Paris. Thus, the political realm about CE is still in limbo.
In times where the EU seeks to leave behind its linear production system, the departure of Britain triggers massive challenges. Modernization and promoting sustainability, such as decarbonizing production processes gain relevance but there are discussions on how to promote such a major systematic transition. Are large industrial actors the main drivers or is change driven by the integration of a regional ecosystem of resilient actors, enabling diversity of approaches (open source)? While it is not clear yet which approach prevails over the other, some countries prefer certain approaches. Larger investments in a future-oriented European industry are supported by several Member States (i.e., the Netherlands, Benelux Germany, France, Poland, Spain). However, the four large member states Germany, France, Italy and Spain show little interest in promoting the digitalization of their economies, mainly out of fear of a decline in their traditional manufacturing industries. On the other hand, the Netherlands, the Nordic countries, Ireland and Czechia, see digitalization as an opportunity, also promote a sustainable economy. Also, the UK´s strategy stresses the idea that a circular economy can be promoted by open platforms. While Britain's <mark>departure</mark> may lower pressure from competitors in the field of CE in the short term it could <mark>slow down</mark> necessary <mark>transition</mark> processes in the long run.
