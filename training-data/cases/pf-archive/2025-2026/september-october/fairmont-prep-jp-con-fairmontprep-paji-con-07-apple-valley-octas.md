] NL - CSAM is structurally incapable of preventing trafficking.
<mark>Kredit 20</mark> [Kevin Nicholas Kredit (Masters in Computer Science from Grand Valley State University), December, 2020, "Confronting Wicked Crypto: Wicked Problems, Encyption Policy, and Exceptional Access Technology,” Information Security Commons, accessed 10-17-2025, https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1996&context=theses //vh 🍓]
Encryption is not the only challenge that law enforcement faces with digital evidence. A 2018 study found that identifying and cooperating with service providers through existing legal frameworks were <mark>even greater challenges</mark> [120]. The <mark>report</mark>, titled “Low-Hanging Fruit,” also lists <mark>resource limitations and poor training as top issues</mark>. Indeed, many current <mark>initiatives are underfunded</mark>. The New York Times’ 2019 investigation into CSAM uncovered a chronic lack of commitment from government initiatives [4]. Major legislation passed in 2008, but has been funded <mark>at only 50% of authorized levels</mark>; a commissioned Justice Department task force has produced only two of five biennial reports; and <mark>a senior executive position </mark>within the Justice Department to target CSAM <mark>was never created</mark>. Federal entities aiming to improve training and coordination between law enforcement and industry are similarly underfunded [120].

] T - Affirming leads to paralysis within law enforcement. 
Aranha 25 [Diego F., Associate Professor in the Department of Computer Science at Aarhus University, research in cryptographic engineering, and Nikolas Melissaris, CNRS cryptography and the theory of computation postdoctoral researcher at IRIF, Université Paris Cité, 2025, “What is Cryptography Hiding from Itself?,” Cryptology ePrint Archive, Paper 2025/1951, accessed 10-26-2025, https://eprint.iacr.org/2025/1951.pdf, Oliver J]
Now imagine scaling this up across Europe. With ChatControl, not only known CSAM images (like in Google’s system) but also new images, videos, and even text chats would be scanned by fallible algorithms. The <mark>European</mark> Parliament’s <mark>study warned of high error rates</mark> for detecting new material. False positives could include: parents’ innocent photos of their kids in the bath, teens sharing consensual intimate pictures with each other, artists’ drawings or memes that an AI misclassifies, or text discussions about sexuality that are non-exploitative but flag certain keywords. Each false alert could send a person’s private messages to the new EU Center, and likely onward to law enforcement for review. Even if eventually cleared, that person may have unknowingly had very sensitive information read by government agencies. This erodes trust and may violate privacy in cases where there was no crime at all. 
Furthermore, children themselves could be harmed by false positives or overreach. Ironically, a law intended to protect children might end up incriminating them. Consider that a significant proportion of so-called “CSAM” reports involve self-generated images by minors (like teens sexting each other) or exploration that, while concerning, is not a result of adult exploitation. If the system flags a consensual exchange between two minors, it could result in them being caught up in the justice system or facing school discipline, etc. There are instances in some countries where teenagers have been charged with child pornography for possessing images of themselves or classmates. Critics fear ChatControl will multiply such cases unless very carefully handled, essentially criminalizing normal adolescent behavior and violating minors’ privacy rather than protecting them. 
Another consequence is the overloading of law enforcement with trivial or irrelevant reports. <mark>Law enforcement</mark> agencies <mark>have</mark> <mark>limited resources</mark>; <mark>flooding them with false leads</mark> or marginal cases could <mark>divert attention from</mark> the <mark>truly urgent cases</mark> of children in real danger. This is what some <mark>police</mark> representatives <mark>have</mark> also quietly <mark>expressed concern</mark> about. Quality over quantity of reports is crucial. If everything becomes noise, the real signals might be missed which is a scenario actually bad for child protection. 

] T - Affirming makes it harder to track antagonists. 
Cordina 25 [Jacques, Bachelor’s of Science @ Tilburg University, 9-30-2025, “The Death of End-to-end Encryption,” 3CL Foundation, accessed 10-5-2025, https://www.3cl.org/the-death-of-end-to-end-encryption-csam-detection/, Oliver J]
Apple’s Failed Experiment: A Crucial Warning
Apple’s reversal on client-side scanning provides a crucial lesson. In 2021, Apple announced <mark>plan</mark>s to scan iCloud Photos for CSAM using its NeuralHash technology. Security researchers quickly demonstrated fundamental flaws, creating hash collisions—innocent images that would trigger the same hash as CSAM. CSS <mark>creates</mark> many <mark>false positives</mark> and negatives according to its own report. <mark>Bad actors</mark> can <mark>manipulate this</mark> in their favour <mark>by</mark> purposefully <mark>creat</mark>ing these <mark>clash</mark>es (by altering an image’s hash numbers), and they can use this information <mark>to</mark> <mark>bypass CSS</mark>. 
<mark>Apple</mark>, despite controlling both hardware and software in its ecosystem and having unlimited resources, ultimately <mark>admitted</mark> it couldn’t implement <mark>CSS</mark> without <mark>creat</mark>ing “<mark>new threat</mark> vector<mark>s</mark>” that would undermine user security. They indefinitely postponed and then abandoned the project entirely. If Apple couldn’t make CSS work safely in their closed ecosystem, how can the EU implement it across dozens of platforms and billions of devices?
Furthermore, they showed how the system could be weaponised by authoritarian governments and proved that once such infrastructure exists, limiting its scope becomes technically and politically impossible.
Why Criminals Will Circumvent This, Whilst Citizens Can’t
The proposal’s fundamental weakness is how easily it can be circumvented by those it purports to target. <mark>Sophisticated</mark> criminal networks already use operational security that would easily evade CSS: self-hosted encrypted servers, steganography to hide content in innocent-appearing files, physical exchange of encrypted drives, and migration to platforms outside EU control.
<mark>Paedophile networks</mark>, already security-conscious, <mark>will migrate en masse to</mark> these <mark>alternatives</mark>. Meanwhile, billions of law-abiding citizens who use WhatsApp to talk to their families will remain under constant surveillance. They need convenient, mainstream platforms to communicate with family, conduct business, and live their digital lives. They can’t opt out of surveillance without opting out of modern communication entirely.

] T- Backdoors drive criminals further underground 
[Richard Forno, Teaching Professor of Computer Science and Electrical Engineering and Assistant Director of UMBC Cybersecurity Institute and University of Maryland and Baltimore County, “TGovernments continue losing efforts to gain backdoor access to secure communications”, May 16, 2025, https://theconversation.com/governments-continue-losing-efforts-to-gain-backdoor-access-to-secure-communications-253016, ichang ]
Governments usually claim that weakening encryption is necessary to fight crime and protect the nation – and there is a valid concern there. However, when that argument fails to win the day, they often turn to claiming to need backdoors to protect children from exploitation.
From a cybersecurity perspective, it is nearly impossible to create a backdoor to a communications product that is only accessible for certain purposes or under certain conditions. If a passageway exists, it’s only a matter of time before it is exploited for nefarious purposes. In other words, creating what is essentially a software vulnerability to help the good guys will inevitably end up helping the bad guys, too.
Often overlooked in this debate is that <mark>if encryption is weakened </mark>to improve surveillance for governmental purposes,<mark> it will drive criminals and terrorists further underground.</mark> <mark>Using different</mark> or homegrown <mark>technologies</mark>, they will still be able <mark>to exchange information in ways that governments can’t readily access. But everyone else’s digital security will be needlessly diminished.</mark>
This lack of online privacy and security is especially dangerous for journalists, activists, domestic violence survivors and other at-risk communities around the world.
Encryption obeys the laws of math and physics, not politics. Once invented, it can’t be un-invented, even if it frustrates governments. Along those lines, if governments are struggling with strong encryption now, how will they contend with a world when everyone is using significantly more complex techniques like quantum cryptography?
Governments remain in an unenviable position regarding strong encryption. Ironically, one of the countermeasures the government recommended in response to China’s hacking of global telephone systems in the Salt Typhoon attacks was to use strong encryption in messaging apps such as Signal or iMessage.
Reconciling that with their ongoing quest to weaken or restrict strong encryption for their own surveillance interests will be a difficult challenge to overcome.
