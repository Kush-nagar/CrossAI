Sole Contention - Cohesion

Starmer is gaining the advantages of EU membership without reigniting the Brexit debate – it’s a political tightrope

Michèle <mark>Auga</mark>, 05-20-20<mark>25</mark>, Michèle Auga heads the Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung's office in the UK and Ireland. Previously, she headed the Sub-Saharan Africa and Western Europe/North America sections of the FES in Berlin as well as the FES offices in Mali, the Palestinian Territories and at the United Nations in New York. "The future is not rejoin — it’s rebuild," IPS Journal, https://www.ips-journal.eu/topics/democracy-and-society/the-future-is-not-rejoin-its-rebuild-8293/ // MH

When Michel Barnier first addressed the British public after the signing of the EU-UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement in 2021, he was clear-eyed: ‘This is a divorce, and nobody should be happy after a divorce... The EU is not a prison. You can leave, but there are consequences.’ Four years on, those consequences are all too visible.

‘Bregret’ has entered the Westminster lexicon, with a mounting body of evidence pointing to Brexit’s economic toll. The UK’s Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) estimates a 4 per cent long-term GDP loss due to reduced productivity and trade friction. Trade volumes are expected to be 15 per cent lower than had the UK remained in the EU. For many Britons, the change became personal when they found themselves queuing at EU borders with travellers they once considered ‘foreign.’

In the lead-up to the 2019 general election, 63 per cent of voters listed Brexit as a top national issue. By 2024, that number had fallen to just 7 per cent. <mark>A mere 21 per cent </mark>of the public now<mark> believes Labour’s victory grants </mark>it<mark> a mandate to rejoin the EU, while 51 per cent </mark>explicitly<mark> reject that </mark>idea. However, <mark>45 per cent support closer ties without rejoining</mark> the Single Market or Customs Union — an opportunity Labour has cautiously embraced.

It is on this fragile consensus that Keir Starmer and Ursula von der Leyen have begun crafting a more functional relationship. The EU-UK Strategic Partnership Summit on 19 May marked a significant departure from years of acrimony. The mood in Lancaster House was noticeably different. ‘Look forward, not back,’ said European Council President António Costa, calling for solidarity in the face of global challenges. Von der Leyen was even more emphatic: ‘We are turning the page. We are opening a new chapter.’

This renewed<mark> cooperation is driven by necessity</mark>. Russia’s war on Ukraine, tensions with an unpredictable US administration, and the urgency of energy security have realigned European priorities. The<mark> UK and EU now</mark> find themselves <mark>on the same side of several </mark>existential<mark> issues</mark> — security, climate, migration, and energy.

Storytelling and institutional commitment
<mark>Progress has already been made</mark>. <mark>The Windsor Framework resolved </mark>contentious <mark>issues around</mark> the <mark>Northern Ireland</mark> Protocol. The<mark> UK </mark>has<mark> rejoined Horizon Europe</mark>. At the May summit, <mark>a roadmap was laid out for deepening cooperation without reopening the wound of EU membership</mark>.

But this process is not without challenges. Both sides must explain the long-term benefits of this new relationship to their publics. Trust remains fragile. And while grand economic prizes like full market access for services remain out of reach, the summit delivered real gains. The<mark> SAFE</mark> Defence Pact <mark>allows the UK </mark>closer<mark> cooperation on Ukraine</mark>, shared planning, and access to the EU defence fund —effectively <mark>reintegrating </mark>it<mark> into Europe’s security architecture</mark>. Energy cooperation <mark>will </mark>see the UK <mark>participate in the EU electricity market</mark>, potentially lowering bills and securing infrastructure against external threats. <mark>Emission trading </mark>systems<mark> will be linked</mark>, creating a shared path to Net Zero and levelling the environmental playing field. <mark>A new SPS deal simplifies trade in agri-foods</mark>, reducing costs for farmers, exporters, and consumers, especially in Northern Ireland. <mark>Reinstatement of ERASMUS+</mark>offers opportunities for young people, particularly those from disadvantaged backgrounds hit hard by Brexit. <mark>Tariff-free trade in steel</mark> – unexpected but vital – was shaped in part by US trade pressures and may prove critical for the British steel sector and Europe’s defence supply chains. Enhanced <mark>cooperation on migration and justice</mark> will bolster <mark>cross-border policing, data sharing, and</mark> joint efforts against <mark>smuggling</mark> networks.

Each step reflects a pragmatic understanding: the UK and EU are better off working together than apart. Still, these advances must be communicated effectively. If the public doesn’t feel tangible improvements, the risk of backlash remains. Agreements alone won’t build trust — implementation, transparency, and visible benefits will.

This is where progressive leadership must step up. In 2016, the Brexit campaign mastered the digital battlefield. Now, progressives must reclaim it. That means telling real stories — of farmers navigating new trade deals, of students regaining study opportunities, of families seeing lower energy bills. Narratives should focus less on abstract growth and more on everyday wins: cheaper groceries, cheaper energy, more jobs.

Targeted messaging is key. Under-45s, urban professionals, students, and skilled workers are open to nuance. TikTok and other platforms can elevate relatable voices. University events, town halls, and local business forums can personalise the EU-UK partnership.

Coordination matters too. Progressive parties across the UK and EU should share strategy and messaging via platforms like the Progressive Alliance. Materials need to be localised, showing regional impacts of lost cooperation and potential gains from renewed ties.

And let’s be honest about the risks. Signing deals raises expectations — sometimes unrealistically so. Delays or missteps could spark renewed disillusionment. Hardliners are poised to exploit any gap between promise and delivery. That’s why storytelling must be paired with institutional commitment: joint committees, monitoring systems, and dispute resolution frameworks must be set up quickly and functionally.

There are deeper structural questions as well. <mark>Can progressives avoid reigniting a ‘Brexit identity’ debate?</mark> Will the EU remain unified in resisting the far right? Can the UK find a sustainable position between the US and the EU?

So far, <mark>Starmer has managed to tread that tightrope</mark>. But EU leaders also remain wary. Both sides are guarding against ‘cherry picking’ — seeking advantages without obligations. The way forward will require realism, persistence, and a clear-eyed understanding that negotiation and compromise is the foundation of democracy.

In the end, Europe is under pressure — from within and without. Cooperation isn’t a luxury; it’s a necessity. That message should be central to any progressive strategy. Integration today isn’t about idealism; it’s about resilience. Shared energy grids, secure supply chains, and coordinated defence are not political luxuries — they’re economic and geopolitical imperatives.

Labour must make the case not for going back to the EU, but for going forward with it. That means abandoning the old rhetoric of betrayal or triumph and building something new — a durable, functional relationship based on mutual respect and shared values.

The divorce is final. What we need now is a cooperative future for the next generation. Let’s not leave the lawyers of separation in charge. Let’s give the storytellers, the strategists, and the bridge-builders a chance to shape what comes next.

UK could become an ‘EU lite’ member of bloc, suggests Franco-German report

Blue-sky exercise envisages four levels of membership to allow for countries having looser ties to the union

<mark>A vision of</mark> a future EU with four <mark>types of membership – including an “EU lite” for</mark> countries such as <mark>the UK</mark> – <mark>has been tabled by</mark> a group of academics commissioned by<mark> France and Germany</mark> to consider future reforms.

The proposal comes as the UK’s opposition leader, Keir Starmer, told France’s president, Emmanuel Macron, that he wanted to build an “even stronger” relationship between the two countries if he wins power at a national election pencilled in for next year.

With the Ukraine war accelerating the appetite for enlargement, the EU leadership has acknowledged the urgent need to start thinking about the shape of the bloc, which could increase from 27 members to as many as 36 countries.

A report presented by France and Germany on Tuesday called on EU institutions to pass a raft of reforms to be ready by 2030 to accept new members such as Ukraine.

Experts from the two European heavyweights drew up the proposals aimed at streamlining the way the bloc works, as Brussels considers its biggest wave of expansion in decades.

The report does not represent the official German or French positions, but will feed into debates at an upcoming summits of 46 European countries and an informal gathering of the 27 EU leaders on successive days in October in Granada in Spain.

“It is clear the enlargement of the EU is in all our interests,” said Germany’s Europe minister, Anna Lührmann, who presented the report in Brussels with her French counterpart.

“We must therefore start now to do everything possible to ensure that the EU is ready for this enlargement.”

The working group of 12 academics and lawyers were commissioned to conduct a blue-sky thinking exercise on a future Europe.

One suggestion is to offer different grades of membership, including an inner circle of countries with “deep integration in areas like the Eurozone and Schengen area”.

The next concentric circle would consist of the current EU, with a third circle of “associate members” who participate in the single market, which could include the likes of Norway, which is in the European Economic Area with access to the trading bloc.

The fourth circle could be a space for countries such as <mark>the UK</mark> and <mark>would involve “political cooperation without having to be bound to EU law”</mark>.

The 46 European leaders meeting in Spain next month are already part of the fledgling European Political Community, which includes the UK and other non-EU members such as Iceland, Serbia, Kosovo, Georgia and Moldova.

Other reforms mooted include a change from unanimous to qualified majority voting on key areas such as taxation, finances and foreign affairs, they said.

This would require changes to European treaties and referendums in some countries.

“Governments have acknowledged that further enlargement without proper institutional reforms would make it even harder – if not impossible – for the EU to take decisions,” the report said.

The experts recommend potential opt-outs that would allow countries’ wary of using their veto powers not to go along with decisions they oppose.

The report also envisions trimming Brussels’ notorious bureaucracy by cutting the number of commissioners put forward by member states in the EU’s executive.

If some member states do not want to go along with changing the EU’s treaties, then the experts said <mark>a “coalition of the willing” could push </mark>on with<mark> reforms</mark> in a new “inner circle” of countries.

Lührmann said the report had received a “very positive” welcome at a meeting of EU European affairs ministers held on Tuesday in Brussels.

But Spanish minister Pascual Navarro, who was at the meeting, said the talks had shown how far apart member states were on key issues.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit impact calculus or pre-written weighing mechanisms (magnitude, probability, timeframe, reversibility, scope). Cards like Auga 2025 and Carrell 2024 assert that "now is the wrong time" and rejoining takes "at least a decade," but no card instructs the judge *how* to weigh delayed benefits against immediate cooperation gains or how to prioritize political feasibility over economic optimality. A weighing card would tagline "Timeframe weighing: delayed rejoin benefits are outweighed by immediate reset gains — probability of success > magnitude of theoretical full membership" and sit after the Auga card to frame the cohesion narrative.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards proactively attack the Pro's core offensive positions (e.g., single market access GDP gains, regulatory alignment benefits, political influence in EU institutions). The case plays pure defense — arguing rejoin is hard/unpopular — but never asserts that *pursuing* rejoin actively harms UK interests (e.g., "rejoin negotiations destabilize the Windsor Framework" or "rejoin rhetoric empowers Reform UK"). An offensive spike would tagline "Rejoin campaign triggers Brexit identity backlash — empowers far-right, fractures Labour coalition" (citing a 2024-25 study on referendum polarization) and sit as a standalone subpoint under Cohesion.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case lacks embedded frontlines to specific Pro responses. Pro will inevitably argue: (1) "Reset is insufficient — only full membership solves services trade friction," (2) "Opt-outs are negotiable — UK got them before," (3) "Public opinion shifts during campaigns." No card preempts these. A defensive spike would tagline "Services market access requires regulatory autonomy — UK cannot cherry-pick; EU demands dynamic alignment" (citing a 2024 EU Commission negotiation mandate) and sit after the Smith 2025 card to frontline the opt-outs argument.

### Evidence quality
**ABSENT:** Most evidence fails elite evidence-comparison standards. Auga 2025 (FES think-tank director) and Carrell 2024 (Guardian editor) are opinion pieces without original data. Harris 2025 (former Labour MP, Telegraph columnist) is partisan commentary. Smith 2025 (YouGov Head of Data Journalism) provides the only proprietary dataset (EuroTrack survey, n≈2,000 per country), but the Independent 2025 card is merely reader comments. No cards cite peer-reviewed studies, government impact assessments (e.g., OBR 2024 Brexit cost update), or EU negotiation directives. A quality card would tagline "OBR 2024: Full EU rejoin yields +2.3% GDP over 15 years vs. +0.8% for reset — but transition costs negate gains until 2035" (citing OBR Fiscal Risks Report 2024) and replace the Auga narrative evidence.

### Collapse flexibility
**PARTIAL — Auga 2025 (tags: "SAFE Defence Pact allows UK closer cooperation on Ukraine... reintegrating it into Europe's security architecture"; "Energy cooperation will see the UK participate in the EU electricity market"; "Emission trading systems will be linked"; "New SPS deal simplifies trade in agri-foods"; "Reinstatement of ERASMUS+"; "Tariff-free trade in steel"; "Enhanced cooperation on migration and justice").** This single card bundles seven distinct cooperation wins (security, energy, climate, agri-food, youth mobility, steel, migration) that function as independent impact scenarios. If Pro beats the economic link, Con can collapse to security cooperation; if Pro beats security, collapse to energy/climate. However, all scenarios derive from one source (Auga) and one summit (May 2025), creating a single point of failure. A second independent card (e.g., "UK-EU Defence Ministerial 2024: Joint drone procurement program operational") would secure flexibility.

### Warrant depth
**PARTIAL — Smith 2025 (tags: "While 54% of Britons support rejoining the EU when asked in isolation, this figure falls to just 36% in the event that rejoining would require the UK to forego its previous opt-outs"; "33-36% would allow [rejoin with opt-outs] — they remain outnumbered by those opposed (41-52%)").** The YouGov EuroTrack data provides the actual survey questions, sample splits (Remain/Leave, party ID), and cross-tabulation logic — the warrant for the "public opinion impasse" claim is fully in the text. Contrast with Auga 2025's claim "45 per cent support closer ties without rejoining" — no pollster, question wording, or sample size is given; it is an unsourced assertion. Harris 2025 offers no data at all, only rhetorical questions. At least one card per contention should meet the Smith standard.

### Internal consistency
**EXECUTED — The case maintains a uniform "reset > rejoin" framing across all cards.** Auga 2025: "The divorce is final. What we need now is a cooperative future... Labour must make the case not for going back to the EU, but for going forward with it." Carrell 2024: Mandelson says "essential... to reduce the damaging impact of the Brexit deal... as best we possibly can" while rejoin talks start "in 10 years' time." O'Carroll 2023: "EU lite" associate membership for UK — "political cooperation without having to be bound to EU law." Harris 2025: "current government is more likely to continue to pursue a 'reset'... in the knowledge that progress... is best made without the interference of pesky voters." Smith 2025: UK voters want opt-outs; EU voters reject opt-outs — "public opinion impasse" makes rejoin impossible. Independent 2025: "Rejoining would take at least a decade... step one has to be to get the issue fully back into the arena of political debate." No card contradicts the "reset now, rejoin later (maybe)" thesis.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 4/10** — The case has a coherent strategic narrative and one high-warrant evidence set (Smith 2025), but fails to embed weighing, offensive/defensive spikes, or elite evidence standards; collapse flexibility relies on a single source; and most cards are opinion commentary rather than warranted analysis.
