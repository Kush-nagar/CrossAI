De Luca 24 [Marino De Luca, Department of Culture, Education, and Society, University of Calabria, Italy; 09/26/2024; “From opponents to enemies? How the British press framed the EU–UK relationship in Brexit news”; Taylor & Francis; https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/22041451.2024.2410668?af=R#abstract; DOA: 10/04/2025] doobz
ABSTRACT
In contemporary democracies, the media plays a crucial role. How the press addresses issues concerning the European Union and its relationships with member states profoundly influences political discourse and public opinion. As an ‘awkward partner’, the United Kingdom has consistently had a complex relationship with the EU, predominantly regarding questions of identity and sovereignty. Within this political context, British newspapers are widely regarded as highly sensitive to EU-UK conflict, employing media rhetoric that underscores a form of destructive dissent associated with threats to sovereignty and identity in the face of a German-dominated Europe. Furthermore, the 2016 Brexit referendum notably exacerbated the EU and the UK divide. This article investigates the prominence of the EU-UK relationship and its portrayal in British media coverage of Brexit. To achieve this, the study analyses news articles published by two British broadsheet newspapers and two tabloids between 1 February 2020 and 31 December 2020.
Introduction
The relationship between the European Union (EU) and the United Kingdom (UK) is pivotal in media discourse. The UK has consistently exhibited a complex interplay with the EU, often centred around issues of identity and sovereignty, leading to its description as an ‘awkward partner’ (George, Citation1990). The UK’s ambivalent entry into the European Economic Community (ECC) in 1973, followed by a referendum on its continued membership two years later, set the stage for fluctuating portrayals of the EU-UK relationship in the media.
These portrayals have been significantly influenced by the British press, which has not merely reported on but actively shaped public discourse regarding the EU-UK relationship since long before the term ‘Brexit’ entered common parlance. Notably, the British press played an instrumental role in priming public opinion through ‘Euromyths’ and crusades against the EU, effectively pre-legitimising Brexit in the public discourse well before the referendum was called (Henkel, Citation2021; Zappettini, Citation2021a, Citation2021b).
British newspapers are widely regarded as reactive to EU-UK conflict, employing mediatic rhetoric that accentuates a form of destructive disagreement linked to sovereignty and identity under threat from a German-dominated Europe (Daddow, Citation2012). Furthermore, <mark>referendums</mark> inherently <mark>foster </mark>significant <mark>divisions by presenting a binary</mark> choice of ‘yes’ or ‘no’, which tends to encourage polarising media coverage. The 2016 Brexit referendum, in particular, intensified this rift between the EU and the UK.
<mark>Historical crusades by major tabloids</mark> against the EU and the dissemination of ‘Euromyths’ <mark>played a pivotal role in shaping</mark> public <mark>perceptions</mark>, effectively <mark>priming</mark> the <mark>readership towards Euroscepticism</mark> well before the referendum was even proposed. During the Brexit referendum campaign, most national tabloids and some broadsheets advocated for a ‘Brexit’ vote (Levy, Aslan, & Bironzo, Citation2016; Newman, Fletcher, Levy, & Nielsen, Citation2016), with the press playing a crucial role in delegitimising the EU.
This alignment with Eurosceptic stances can be traced back to the 1980s, when the rise of Euroscepticism in British politics was paralleled by the press’s increasing focus on the ‘European issue’.
This study investigates the prominence and framing of the EU-UK relationship in British news coverage concerning Brexit. Individual characteristics of news outlets can shape the media coverage of specific issues. As previously noted, the UK press is heavily biased in its reporting on the EU (Scammell & Semetko, Citation2008). This bias is due to a longstanding Eurosceptic or Europhile orientation in different newspapers, rather than a generic negativity bias. It is noteworthy in the current case, because it underscores the persistent polarised views that continue to shape public discourse on Brexit (Levy, Aslan, & Bironzo, Citation2016; Newman, Fletcher, Levy, & Nielsen, Citation2016). Media outlets that supported the Leave campaign are more likely to have covered the EU negatively during the post-Brexit period, framing news in a manner consistent with the media’s referendum endorsement (Brandenburg, Citation2006), particularly in tabloids known for their Eurosceptic tendencies (de Vreese, Banducci, Semetko, & Boomgaarden, Citation2006).
This essay seeks to address the following research questions:
‘Is there a connection in Brexit news between the EU-UK relationship and significant political leaders and parties in the UK?
How do such connections influence the press’s framing of this relationship?’
In order to answer these questions, the study analyses 1,906 news articles published by two broadsheets and two tabloids between 1 February 2020 and 31 December 2020.
The turbulent EU-UK relationship in the context of Brexit
<mark>Negativity bias</mark> in media coverage <mark>is</mark> a <mark>prominent</mark> area of investigation in communication studies. The pervasiveness of media negativity can influence the perception and legitimacy of media coverage of the EU. Existing research demonstrates that coverage of European politics adheres less stringently to press objectivity criteria than coverage of domestic politics (Örnebring, Citation2013).
During the 1980s, as Euroscepticism began to gain a foothold in British politics, the press played a crucial role in aligning public opinion with increasingly sceptical views of the EU. This period saw the British press reporting on and actively participating in political discourse, often echoing and amplifying the Eurosceptic sentiments of prominent political figures. On the one hand, despite more balanced positions during domestic electoral campaigns, the media appears more nationalistic when discussing foreign policy or issues related to national interests (Hannerz, Citation2004). On the other hand, while journalism’s objectivity criteria generally encompass political balance, opposition to the EU seems to hold greater appeal in terms of media coverage and is employed more frequently by journalists (de Wilde, Michailidou, & Trenz, Citation2013). This media approach impacts readers’ understanding of the EU-UK relationship, since British newspapers, for example, have demonstrated a critical deficiency in reporting EU affairs (Leveson, Citation2012) or covering European rules and regulations (Daddow, Citation2012).
This alignment of media narrativesFootnote1 with political Euroscepticism set the stage for the intense media dynamics observed during the Brexit referendum campaign. Studies such as Zappettini’s (Citation2021a, Citation2021b) underline the extent to which pre-referendum media coverage laid the groundwork for Brexit.
Although a rational approach would encourage the dismantling of such frameworks, the <mark>negativity</mark> of the conflict <mark>exerts a more significant impact</mark>, mainly <mark>due to the echo chamber effect</mark> of social media (Tandoc, Citation2014). From this media standpoint, conflict-centric negativity enhances the EU’s visibility (Galpin & Trenz, Citation2017). Consequently, while European actors become more prominent through their mediatic and personal stories, increased negativity amplifies the political dimension, <mark>characterised by attacks,</mark> <mark>cynical news and</mark>, most importantly, a general sense of <mark>fear</mark>, which encourages Eurosceptic positions (Soroka, Young, & Balmas, Citation2015).
Hence, media negativism misrepresents EU policies (Galpin & Trenz, Citation2017) and perpetuates stereotypes in the British press of EU institutions as dysfunctional and corrupt (Anderson & Weymouth, Citation1999; Gavin, Citation2001).
British media outlets have historically shaped public opinion on the EU-UK relationship (Bingham & Conboy, Citation2015; Carey & Burton, Citation2004). Since the inception of the European integration project, most British newspapers have supported varying political views on European affairs. Though there have been fluctuations – such as changes in government and political party power or shifts in editorial stances on Europe – most broadsheets (except The Times and The Daily Telegraph) have portrayed the EU-UK relationship on a spectrum from Europositive to soft-Eurosceptic. In contrast, the majority of tabloids have adopted a hard Eurosceptic stance (Zappettini, Citation2021a), using tactics of extreme simplification and EU blaming, particularly in ‘red top’ media coverage such as the Daily Mail, The Sun, and the Daily Express (Anderson & Weymouth, Citation2014; Daddow, Citation2012; Hameleers, Bos, & de Vreese, Citation2016, 2017).
Within this context, the Brexit referendum campaign was characterised as a fear-based debate designed to advance a ‘project’ associated with Leave/Remain positions (Daddow, Citation2016; Galpin & Trenz, Citation2017; Ruparel, Citation2016), reflecting a deeply divided nation (Hobolt, Citation2016). This debate exposed the prevalence of Eurosceptic sentiment (Goodwin & Heath, Citation2016) and populist fears related to social insecurity (Browning, 2018) or identity ideology (Kenny, Citation2017). Both sides employed fear-driven narratives centred on the invasion of EU migrants or economic catastrophe following EU departure. Beyond influencing voting behaviour, these framings reinforced public opinion surrounding the notion of a stark boundary between the UK and the EU (Coutto, Citation2020). British news outlets, in particular, highlighted the threat posed by EU policy to the concept of the nation (Anderson & Weymouth, Citation1999; Díez Medrano, Citation2003; Rowinski, Citation2014). <mark>Tabloid</mark> outlet<mark>s</mark> were instrumental in linking the issue of European integration with migration, <mark>employ</mark>ing <mark>an ‘emotional’ strategy based on</mark> identity and <mark>sovereignty</mark> (Startin, Citation2015). This approach fostered a framing of ‘us and them’ between individual state members and the EU organisation (Triandafyllidou, Citation1998).
Furthermore, certain British tabloids have been accused of trivialising EU policy <mark>and</mark> portraying the UK as an unfortunate victim of a Brussels conspiracy (Berry, Citation2016; Levy, Aslan, & Bironzo, Citation2016). In the post-referendum period, the approach to effectively exiting the EU polarised media debate around the negotiations between the UK and the EU. The implementation of Brexit intensified the media’s emotionalisation strategy, which crafted a narrative of the UK’s purported <mark>humiliation</mark> at the Salzburg meeting (2018) <mark>during</mark> the Brexit <mark>negotiations</mark>. Tabloid coverage emphasised fear, frustration, pride and freedom as frameworks for Brexit, perpetuating a longstanding narrative of EU control and British heroism against the ‘incompetent’ gangsterism of the EU (Zappettini, Citation2021b).
Data and sample
This study concentrates on the examination of daily newspapers for several reasons. First, their coverage shows more partisanship than that of the more impartial broadcasting outlets (Scammell & Semetko, Citation2008; Schoenbach, De Waal, & Lauf, Citation2005). Secondly, newspapers endorsed a Leave or Remain stance during the Brexit referendum (Levy et al., Citation2016). Thirdly, they employ a framing strategy to present various news items. Fourthly, they serve as a crucial source of information for public opinion (Newman, Fletcher, Levy, & Nielsen, Citation2016).
From this standpoint, selecting the news outlets is a vital methodological step in analysing media coverage of the EU-UK relationship. This content analysis is based on a predetermined set of newspapers-two broadsheets, The Guardian/The Observer, and The Daily Telegraph/The Sunday Telegraph, and two tabloids, the Daily Mail/Mail on Sunday and The Mirror/Sunday Mirror – and considers their editorial inclination, political framing, and their known influence on public opinion regarding Brexit. Broadsheets primarily focus on ‘hard’ news, encompassing politics and economics (Esser, Citation1999). In contrast, tabloids tend to concentrate more on ‘soft’ news involving the private lives of politicians, celebrities and ordinary individuals (Sparks, Citation2000). The choice of these particular newspapers was deliberate in order to ensure a representative sample of the British press, capturing a range of editorial perspectives and political alignments. The Guardian/The Observer are known for their pro-Remain stance and left-leaning editorial policies. On the other hand, The Daily Telegraph/The Sunday Telegraph are characterised by their conservative and pro-Leave positions. The Daily Mail/Mail on Sunday are prominent examples of right-leaning tabloids that heavily supported Brexit, while The Mirror/Sunday Mirror, known for their left-leaning tendencies, advocated for Remain (De Luca, Citation2023; Simpson & Startin, Citation2023). For instance, the Daily Mail’s pro-Leave stance contrasts sharply with The Guardian’s pro-Remain position, providing a comprehensive spectrum of political discourse surrounding EU-UK relations. This selection allows for a thorough analysis of how different segments of the British press framed the EU-UK relationship during the Brexit transition period (Berry, Citation2016; Levy, Aslan, & Bironzo, Citation2016).
Furthermore, by means of a ‘manual’ content analysis, it is feasible to come to a specific comprehension of the UK media coverage of Brexit, emphasising the particular facets of each newspaper. The content analysis was conducted by examining articles published by the four newspapers under scrutiny in the LexisNexis database, published between 1 February 2020, and 31 December 2020 (i.e. encompassing the entire transition period and five weeks of analysis), which mentioned ‘Brexit’ in their corpus text.
Specifically, this involved analysing articles published by the newspapers, while ensuring that our sample constituted a balanced view of the media landscape during the Brexit transition period.
The entire content of 856 articles from The Guardian/The Observer (278), The Daily Telegraph/The Sunday Telegraph (278), The Mirror/Sunday Mirror (158), and the Daily Mail/Mail on Sunday (142) were selected. Then, 1,906 units of analysis of The Guardian/The Observer (655), The Daily Telegraph/The Sunday Telegraph (634), The Mirror/Sunday Mirror (300), and the Daily Mail/Mail on Sunday (317) were identified and collected using NVivo. In NVivo, a series of nodes and themes was developed to map out the various dimensions of the EU-UK relationship. The units of analysis comprised individual sentences containing the term ‘Brexit’. A thematic coding approach was employed, creating nodes representing vital themes such as ‘sovereignty’, ‘identity’, ‘economic impact’ and ‘political leadership’. These themes were identified based on the content of the articles and the context in which ‘Brexit’ was mentioned. The analysis paid particular attention to figurative language such as metaphors, which are often used to convey complex political sentiments in a more relatable manner. Metaphors were coded as a separate node, to capture the nuanced ways in which Brexit was framed. For example, metaphors portraying Brexit as a ‘divorce’, the EU as a ‘dictator’, or the UK as a ‘victim’ were identified and analysed in order to understand the underlying sentiments and rhetorical strategies used by the newspapers. The study included news reports and editorial/opinion pieces, allowing us to explore the presentation of facts and the opinion-based commentary that shapes public perceptions.

So, why, given this awakening to (an entirely predictable) reality, should the effort not be made to rejoin? There are three decisive reasons: first, it would create a host of new and damaging uncertainties; second, it would tear British politics apart just as they were calming down; third, the deal the UK would get would be quite different from the one it had, not least because, as Michel Barnier, the erstwhile EU negotiator, has told the Financial Times, “The EU today is no longer the EU that the UK left. We have begun to draw the lessons of Brexit.”
<mark>The</mark> uncertainty an <mark>application</mark> to join would create is quite clear. The battle to reapply alone <mark>would</mark> <mark>consume</mark> much of <mark>a parliament</mark>. There would need to be a new referendum — in my view, two, one to launch negotiations and another to assess its terms. Between these, there would be yet another negotiation, with unpredictable outcomes. <mark>The UK would look deranged</mark> in doing this so soon. <mark>For business, it would be a nightmare.</mark>
Moreover, reopening <mark>the</mark> question would be <mark>bitterly divisive</mark>. Yes, the result of a referendum might go the other way this time. But that is far from certain. What it would certainly do is redivide the country, with Leavers viewing it as a betrayal and Remainers as a chance for revenge. Labour, if it were indeed in government, would be mad to undertake so divisive a <mark>project</mark>. Worse, it <mark>would divert attention</mark> and energy away <mark>from</mark> tackling many <mark>other economic</mark> and social <mark>challenges</mark>. Sir Keir Starmer knows this.
Most important, the outcome would not be the UK’s to decide. The EU would want to be quite confident that the new member would be more co-operative and committed than the old one. Given the many challenges the EU confronts, it cannot afford to take on a big and potentially hostile member. Who can forget the offensive scene in which Nigel Farage and his Brexit party colleagues stood with their backs to the podium while the anthem played at the opening session of the EU parliament in 2019?

Much attention has focused on how much “headroom” the Chancellor, Rachel Reeves, has against her fiscal rules and which taxes she might raise in the Budget on 26th November. (See here and here.) But this leaves us in danger of losing sight of what really matters, namely the condition of the UK’s public finances and the key fiscal risks. In this Update, we take a bigger picture view by assessing whether the UK is on the precipice of a fiscal crisis, what could trigger such a crisis and how policymakers would respond.
Is a fiscal crisis in the UK imminent? 
There are genuine reasons to be concerned about the UK’s fiscal position. At 5.1% of GDP in 2024/25, public borrowing was above the 2.9% average in the five years prior to the pandemic. Public sector net debt rose from 80% of GDP in early 2019 to 97% in 2020 and has barely budged since. This raises the risk of sudden stops as foreign investors pull out of the market at times of high risk. <mark>It’s</mark> easy to see why investors’ nerves are frayed.
This does <mark>not</mark> mean that a fiscal crisis is imminent or <mark>inevitable</mark>. <mark>History shows governments</mark> <mark>can muddle</mark> <mark>through</mark> with high and rising debt levels for some time without a crisis erupting. <mark>What</mark> <mark>are</mark> the possible <mark>triggers</mark> for a UK fiscal crisis<mark>?</mark> 
There are seven key escalation risks. (See Table 1.) <mark>First,</mark> the politics, such as <mark>a further slump</mark> of the government <mark>in</mark> the <mark>polls</mark>, <mark>could force</mark> the <mark>bending</mark> or breaking of the <mark>fiscal rules to fund</mark> new <mark>initiatives</mark>. In a favourable fiscal environment, changing the fiscal rules would have little effect. In the current febrile fiscal environment even a small change to the rules could be perceived as a waning commitment to fiscal discipline, adding to the sense of nervousness about the UK’s perilous fiscal position and leading to sharp increases in borrowing costs. 
<mark>Second,</mark> investors may conclude that the government can’t or won’t restrain spending or raise taxes enough to reduce the deficit. Even if the government sticks to its fiscal rules and replenishes its “headroom” against them, investors need to be convinced the government is willing and able to push through tax rises/spending cuts. If those fiscal plans are not credible, the bond market could wobble. Once fiscal credibility is lost, any plans to reduce borrowing may also not be believed and a crisis could ensue. 
Third, Reeves could be sidelined or replaced by someone less committed to fiscal discipline. The rise in borrowing costs in July following the rumours that Reeves might be removed from her post shows just how sensitive investors are to the possibility of fiscally disciplined figures losing their influence. 
Fourth, the UK’s <mark>debt dynamics could deteriorate</mark> further <mark>if</mark> UK inflation and interest rates don’t fall and GDP <mark>growth doesn’t accelerate as we expect.</mark> This could lead to the effective interest rate on UK government debt exceeding the UK’s long-term nominal GDP growth rate of 3.5-4.0%, putting the debt to GDP ratio on an upward trajectory. At 3.6% in 2024/25, it was already close to exceeding this growth rate. 
Fifth, a vicious cycle could develop where fears about long-term sustainability of UK public debt leads to a further sell-off in the UK’s government bond market, raising borrowing costs, adding to the budget deficit and making the debt path even more unsustainable. This in itself might require a trigger, or just a combination of smaller unfavourable developments.

But the bond markets would probably be rattled by the unfunded reality of some of Reform’s current tax cuts/spending increases and require a higher return for taking on the greater risk.

<mark>If a</mark> fiscal <mark>crisis does happen</mark>, how would it pan out? 
In the first phase, if there were signs of dislocation in the financial markets, the response would probably involve the Bank of England stepping in to buy gilts, as it did after the mini-budget in September 2022. The Bank’s “Ways and Means” facility (the government’s overdraft at the Bank) would provide a backstop for the government to borrow short-term funds. And <mark>the government would need to</mark> perform a <mark>u-turn on any recent initiatives</mark> that loosened policy and/or replace its government personnel with someone more fiscally prudent or able to earn the confidence of the market. 
The second phase would involve the government putting together a credible long-term plan <mark>to</mark> address investors’ concerns and <mark>reduce the debt ratio</mark>. There are four ways to reduce the debt ratio: growth-boosting reforms, higher inflation, financial repression or fiscal austerity. Our sense is that <mark>the onus would fall on austerity</mark>. Growth-boosting reforms can’t be done overnight and there is no guarantee that they would work, so this would not be enough to end a crisis. The UK’s reliance on inflation-linked debt suggests resorting to higher inflation could push up borrowing costs and be counter-productive or at the least less effective. Financial repression, in which interest rates are kept artificially low, may help to tackle the debt problem, but at the expense of higher inflation. 
Does the UK need a fiscal crisis? 
Some have suggested that if the UK is going to address its fiscal issues, a crisis is exactly what the UK needs. This would jolt the government into having a proper conversation with the public about what the state does, and the level of taxation required to pay for it. Tax rises/spending cuts would happen sooner than if the Chancellor muddles through each fiscal event gradually tightening fiscal policy. Austerity could ultimately reduce gilt yields and restore confidence in the bond markets. And some even suggest austerity could be stimulative i.e. so-called “expansionary austerity”.
We’re not convinced. Even if tax rises/spending cuts happen sooner, austerity is very unpopular and tends to weaken the economy. Indeed, we very much doubt the boost to GDP from lower gilt yields would be big enough to offset the drag on GDP from the austerity needed to bring gilt yields down. 
There are also scenarios in which <mark>a bigger crisis</mark> occurs, and the hit to the wider economy is greater. <mark>If the move in markets is severe</mark>, it <mark>could expose hidden vulnerabilities in the financial system</mark>, as it did in late 2022 when the rise in bond yields led to the liability driven investment crisis. And the UK’s large current account deficit means it is dependent on external financing. A loss of confidence by international investors raises the risk of a sharp fall in the exchange rate generating higher import prices and higher inflation. In the current situation, these risks are not worth taking to force the government to change tack.
Overall, the potential trigger for a UK fiscal crisis is just as likely to be changes in perception or personnel as policy. Whatever the Chancellor does in the Budget on 26th November, the government must continue to fly the flag of fiscal rectitude to keep the bond market onside. It needs to persuade bond markets of its commitment to fiscal discipline, while at the same time reducing the budget deficit in a measured and gradual way.

He is also a Senior Fellow with the Transatlantic Defense and Security program at the Center for European Policy Analysis (CEPA), having formerly served in the German defense ministry and as Director of Carnegie Europe from 2011 to 2016; 08/19/2025; ‘The Future of European Integration: A Threshold Moment of Hope”; THE Center for International Relations and Sustainable Development (CIRSD); https://www.cirsd.org/en/horizons/horizons-summer-2025--issue-no-31/the-future-of-european-integration; DOA: 09/12/2025] doobz
The EU has, in many ways, become synonymous with Europe because it is uniquely relevant to the fate of millions of Europeans. Indeed, most European countries are now member states of the EU; the union is a global mercantile power, and it permeates European politics from the lofty heights of supranational decision-making to local politics in communities and cities in every corner of the continent. This is why discussing the future of the EU largely, although not entirely, means discussing the future of Europe.
The Union’s future, in turn, will depend primarily on two things: first, how it can create input legitimacy by letting citizens participate in EU decision-making; and second, how the EU can continue to deliver value to its 27 member states and its 450 million citizens (output legitimacy). This article will focus on output legitimacy: essentially, the EU’s ability to produce prosperity, security, and stability in Europe.
The Big Three
<mark>For the most part, the E</mark>uropean <mark>U</mark>nion <mark>is humming along</mark>, producing added value for Europeans every day: via the single market, a unified trade policy, competition policies, consumer protection, the Common Agricultural Policy (CAP), and a myriad of other policies designed to make life easier, richer, safer, and more comfortable for EU citizens.
But the EU has been notably weak in producing similarly meaningful results in the types of crises that have dominated news headlines in recent years. These fields are foreign policy, security and defense, migration (including asylum policy and border protection), and, finally, fiscal policy.
Now, more than any of the past EU successes, these areas shape EU citizens’ perception of Brussels and its machinery. While the benefits of integration are now taken for granted, the shortcomings and failures in these fields determine the EU’s image.
These sectors happen to be the three decisive ones for the future of the EU. It is here that the greatest need for deeper cooperation—and integration—between member states lies. These are also the areas where no significant leaps toward meaningful integration have been made in at least a decade.
The EU managed the global financial crisis relatively well: the European Central Bank (ECB) guaranteed the solvency of ill-faring EU member states (“whatever it takes”), and fiscal instruments were created to alleviate the worst economic meltdown in decades. Similarly, the EU handled the COVID-19 pandemic by coordinating aid work, procuring and disseminating crucial material and equipment, and creating a fiscal aid mechanism aimed at reconstructing Europe’s hard-hit economies. Finally, after the 2022 Russian invasion of Ukraine, the EU not only helped organize military aid at unprecedented levels, but also created programs and budget lines to help its member states spend more on defense, accelerate their military build-ups, and expand defense industrial capacity in Europe.
However, even this successful crisis management did not lead to deeper integration within the EU. We have not made significant steps toward a fiscal union (to accompany the monetary one), and the EU is no closer to being a relevant foreign policy player or a provider of military security. Meaningful action under duress, yes. Meaningful integration, no.
The latter entails genuine sovereignty bargains by member states—i.e., the delegation of significant chunks of national sovereignty to the EU level in return for a gain in collective problem-solving capacity. Contrary to widespread misperceptions, it is not the implementation of temporary measures of crisis management, nor the creation of yet another ad hoc special instrument, project, or budget line meant to address a specific problem. Rather, it is the EU’s systematic involvement in a particular policy field, based on a clear legal mandate from the member states, that produces a net result of enhancing the EU’s ability to act.
The Dirty Little Secret
The dirty little secret of European integration is the monetization of shared problems. In many fields, integration is possible because member states can “buy compromise” (i.e., create unity by compensating EU countries for their participation in an unwanted policy, or by granting member states exemptions from particular policies in return for compensatory payments).
In the crucial fields mentioned above, monetization is not (or not as easily) possible as in other policy areas. They all touch upon the core of national sovereignty, not just its secondary or tertiary elements. Giving up sovereignty in these fields is considerably harder for member states, which are eager to guard the principal aspects of their statehood. Genuine sovereignty bargains in these areas would mean trading away constituent elements of sovereign statehood: the power of the purse, unitary command over the armed forces, the decision over whom to let enter the country, whom to show to the door, and whom to keep. These policy fields are also closely tied to citizens’ national identities and therefore cannot be easily re-labeled as “European.”
This is also the reason why the Monnet method of integration in these fields does not work—at least not “properly.” According to the Monnet method, deep economic integration will have automatic spillover effects into the political realm, bringing about sovereignty bargains in both commercial and political fields. The idea is that the momentum toward integration steadily grows as more areas are sucked into the integration logic more or less automatically. The prosperity effects of deeper economic integration lead to more—and even deeper—harmonization and convergence, making political integration unavoidable. According to this method, the single market and the common currency are the two main drivers. No term describes this logic better than “ever closer union.”
As integration deepens, fewer fields are subject to national vetoes and opt-outs, and authority and power increasingly rest with the institutions in Brussels. Member states do not lose their influence altogether; they remain powerful during decision-making in Council meetings. However, they relinquish considerable (and sometimes all) steering power once they have agreed to “communalize” a policy field.
In areas where the Monnet method does not work, standard institutional reform (treaty change, qualified majority voting (QMV), size of the European Parliament and Commission, rebates, etc.) will not bring about more integration. First, institutional reform is generally harder to accomplish in these fields because member states are more reluctant—for the reasons stated above. Second, even if institutional reform happens in these areas, it does not create the same binding power as in other sectors.
In other words, member states are less compliant with decisions in these fields, even if the formal rules compel them to follow the (new) directives. EU countries will take the risk of being non-compliant, even if that creates political costs. These sacrifices are often considered more acceptable and less costly than losing core elements of national sovereignty.
This is also why the introduction of QMV on issues of foreign and security policy is unlikely to bring about great leaps forward in integration—let alone a “single voice” situation in which the EU finds it easier and quicker to develop a unified position and the means to pursue its goals.
Crisis Management is Not Integration
As the COVID-19 pandemic and the global financial crisis have shown, a lack of integration in specific policy fields does not necessarily render the EU unable to act under pressure. However, these crises have also demonstrated that contingencies and conflagrations need to be seen as overwhelming and existential in order to lead to a (temporary) transfer of power from member states to EU institutions. Typically, in such a crisis, the EU is seen as the actor of last resort—even by reluctant member states. Traditional notions of sovereignty lose their validity, and established cost-benefit calculations no longer apply.
Remarkable things can be achieved under such circumstances, but these emergency mergers should not be mistaken for “integration” as defined above. Such measures are mostly temporary or come in the shape of narrowly defined special instruments, often with limited running time.
In none of the aforementioned fields has this level of existential fear been reached yet—not even in the field of migration, which is arguably the most pressing, most emotional, and most politically costly issue in the EU today. It is clearly an issue in which only a pan-European solution could bring the relief member states seek.
The migration issue illustrates that as long as the pain from the crisis is considered smaller than the pain from a migration-related sovereignty bargain, that bargain will not happen. Only additional pressure—or “learning through pain”—is likely to bring about a change of mind (even though many reject this notion as an overly cynical view of politics). The same logic applies to security and defense, where, for most EU member states, NATO still offers the best solution to the existential problem of European security. It remains to be seen whether this changes if, during the second Trump administration, the United States decides to no longer be the backbone of NATO deterrence and the guarantor of security on the European continent. In these policy fields, great leaps forward are not expected in the near future, thus defying the integration logic.
Matters of Life and Death
Defense is the least likely field to integrate in the foreseeable future. It is the oldest and most primal of all competencies of the modern state. Keeping its citizens safe from external threats is the state’s first and final obligation—long before and long after other key functions such as domestic security, welfare, health, or education. This is why states are least likely to integrate this policy field with other nations and rely on supranational constructs. Apart from the EU’s smallest members, individual countries believe they can avoid this scenario by opting instead for reliance on their own (sometimes residual) strength and capabilities. In other words, for defense to be integrated, EU-style, a level of trust between European states would be required that is unlikely to emerge in ultimate questions of life and death.
The field of defense is also most closely tied to questions of identity. Nation-states are defined as communities of fate that, in the end, need to stand together in questions of physical survival. This requires a strong sense of inclusion and shared identity—a powerful force not easily replaceable by other, more abstract aspects of belonging.
Finally, a look at NATO, the existing military alliance devoted to European security, also does not encourage integrationist defense fantasies. Even in NATO—a well-oiled military alliance—political integration at a deeper, supranational level has never occurred. Despite NATO’s integrated military command structure, there is no such thing as a NATO army. The alliance has always remained strictly intergovernmental; sovereignty bargains that characterize the EU’s integrated policies are unknown to NATO. According to the Washington Treaty of 1949, each ally decides independently whether to participate in alliance operations, according to its national constitutional requirements. Decision-making is not delegated to a supranational body such as the European Commission, nor is it based on an elaborate body of supranational law. Political cohesion in NATO does not come from Monnet-style “integration,” but from external pressure and a shared threat assessment.
Furthermore, the Europeans’ dependence on America—by far the strongest ally—has also created a certain amount of cohesion. Paradoxically, each member’s national veto on NATO decisions works as an integrative force, not as a divider. Member states see no reason to leave or damage NATO, knowing that their veto will always enable them to prevent a massively undesired development from happening. This might hold a lesson to be learned for the EU. NATO has also kept the probability of institutional blockade small by focusing exclusively on security and defense-related issues.
The Most Divisive Issue
Flawed migration and asylum policies, and a deeply dysfunctional “Dublin” system in the EU—whose primary role is to determine the country responsible for dealing with individual asylum applications—have made migration and asylum policy the most divisive issue in Europe over the last decade. The inability to resolve this problem remains the number one reason for the populist surge across the European continent.
This is all the more ironic given that this is the policy field in which integration, at least in theory, should be most likely. Member states have a broadly shared outlook on the problem, possess the means to deal with the issue, and feel the pressure both domestically and externally. However, it is also the field in which the strong necessity to devise an overarching pan-European solution collides sharply with issues of identity and sovereignty. Nations are reluctant to relinquish control over a key element of what makes a sovereign nation: control over its borders and the decision of whom to admit or deny.
The much-needed Europe-wide solution would demand painful compromise from EU member states—either in the form of accepting precisely defined numbers of refugees into their societies or by adapting strict asylum and migration policies. The latter, however, would violate existing European law and humanitarian convictions, which are preferred by many national political constituencies over more archaic notions of control based on identity and belonging.
Recent unilateral steps taken by some member states have somewhat eased migratory pressures, but have also come at the expense of further integration. A constructive squaring of this circle—based on watertight migration management at Europe’s external borders and openness within its internal borders—has not yet been devised. Attempts to monetize the issue have failed; the Monnet method does not seem to work. Whether genuine integration will be possible in these policy fields therefore remains unanswered in mid-2025.
Monetary, Not Fiscal
When the EU’s common currency, the euro, was devised in the late 1980s and early 1990s, the integrationist ideal behind it was pure Monnet: integrate the easy bits (read: monetary policy) first, then the more difficult bits (read: fiscal policy, meaning common borrowing and budgeting) would follow sooner or later. However, a quarter-century after adopting the euro as a means of payment, this idea has not yet become a European reality.
To be sure, new fiscal tools invented during the euro crisis, the sovereign debt crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic have created the impression of significant leaps in integration in budgetary policy. But do these measures meet the standard for meaningful integration, as defined above? The answer is no. Ad hoc means of collaboration in crisis—even new fiscal instruments designed to tackle specific challenges—do not constitute an integrationist breakthrough. The fundamental principles of EU decision-making on budgets remain unchanged. Nations are in charge of the primary fiscal decisions in Europe, with the EU playing an increasingly important role in the secondary ones. Member states will likely keep their ultimate veto power on fiscal policy in the EU for the foreseeable future.
The reasons for this are manifold, but the core logic resembles the ones at play in the defense and migration fields: a core tenet of what makes a sovereign nation-state defies all forces that pull in a more integrationist direction. National parliaments show no inclination to delegate their most constitutive right—the power of the purse—to a supranational entity such as the European Parliament. As long as the principal political process in Europe is playing out at the national level, this is unlikely to change. The principal mechanisms for organizing the allocation of power, legitimacy, and responsibility in Europe are national elections, not European ones. Governments stand and fall based on the approval or disapproval of national constituencies.
Going Beyond the Nation?
This is where we stand today: the future of the EU will depend on integrationist progress in fields where such progress is least likely to emerge. This is no academic matter. The ramifications of this state of affairs are practical and play out politically in Europe daily. The risk for the EU is obvious: if it cannot offer solutions to existential problems, its output legitimacy will dwindle, and political cohesion will diminish. Ultimately, its existence is at risk.
<mark>The big question</mark> <mark>remains</mark> <mark>whether</mark> the EU’s principal overlords—the EU <mark>member states</mark>—are <mark>will</mark>ing to <mark>risk the union’s demise. So far, the answer is no. However,</mark> the majority response to requests for genuine integration in the three aforementioned key policy fields is also a no. This is not a state of affairs that can go on indefinitely. Political realities develop at breakneck pace and demand policy responses. One such response to the dilemma, given mainly through anti-integrationist nationalists, is this: reduce the EU to a free trade zone and, maybe at best, a single market—and forget about the political stuff, the sovereignty bargains, and the pooling of decision-making at the supranational level.
This is what strict sovereigntists have always wanted; it’s the mindset behind Brexit and anti-EU populism. It is the nationalist snake oil that promises to heal Europe from the complex (and sometimes unpalatable) rules-based compromise-making within the EU. It is not the solution to problem-solving in a highly globalized, data-driven, and knowledge-based global environment—facing problems that far exceed the coping capacity of even the biggest nation-states. But it will always retain its lure as long as notions of belonging, identity, solidarity, and fateful connection do not meaningfully exceed the concept of the nation. Nationalists will tell you that this will never happen. But just as the powerful idea of a nation once replaced the family, the clan, and the tribe as the principal frameworks of solidarity and accountability, the nation can be exceeded without losing its value or even disappearing. This is the hope at the core of all thoughts about the future of Europe. Our <mark>current times are the threshold moment</mark> for this hope.
