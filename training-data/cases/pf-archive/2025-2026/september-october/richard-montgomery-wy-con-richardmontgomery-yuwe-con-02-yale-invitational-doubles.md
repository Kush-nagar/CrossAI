 The British public’s decision to leave the European Union in the referendum of 2016 was far from the end of the discussion around Britain’s future as a member state. The debate has continued to trundle on, albeit more quietly compared to the period of parliamentary chaos which occurred between 2017 and 2019, with support for the prospect of re-joining the EU continuing to rise. Currently, support for re-joining the EU (49%) is higher than support for staying out (35%) which has been the case since July 2022. Labour has ruled out any attempt to re-join the EU, at least in the near future, as well as the prospect of single market or customs union membership. But even if they did want to re-join, the way back to full <mark>EU membership</mark> is difficult – something acknowledged by both Labour and the Conservatives as part of the Better Together Campaign in 2014, in which they highlighted that the route to membership would be difficult for an independent Scotland. To become an EU member, prospective member states need all current member states to accept their application. This <mark>is</mark> particularly <mark>difficult given</mark> the <mark>diverging interests of the 27 member</mark> countrie<mark>s</mark>, not least because the <mark>UK’s withdrawal</mark> from the EU has <mark>meant a gain in representation</mark> in European Parliament and greater influence in decision making in Council <mark>for some</mark> member states. As time moves on, the UK is also diverging from EU standards, suggesting that the way back to membership would be difficult. The public, despite their seeming desire to re-join the EU, acknowledge that this is a demanding process. A poll conducted by Redfield and Wilton for UK in a Changing Europe, shows that just 27% of voters think it is likely that EU member states would accept the UK’s application to re-join the Union. But what actually is the state of play? Do EU member states want the UK back? The answer isn’t a straightforward ‘yes’ or ‘no’. There is some support for the prospect: the European Free Alliance (EFA), a European Parliamentary group of regionalist political parties and part of the fourth largest parliamentary group in the EP, had stated that the ‘EU must also be sure to never close the door on the peoples of the UK’. Indeed, most European Parliamentary groups have included something on closer UK-EU ties in their manifestos for upcoming European Parliament elections. While a particular area envisaged for closer cooperation is defence (EPP), as a result of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the European Free Alliance, the Alliance of Liberals and Democrats for Europe and the Party of European Socialists all want to develop a closer relationship with the UK. In September last year, a research group also floated the prospect of Britain becoming an ‘associate member’ of the EU, allowing the UK to participate in the single market whilst requiring the UK to contribute to the EU’s budget and be governed by the European Court of Justice. Yet in many ways, <mark>Brexit</mark> has strengthened the EU. Firstly, Britain leaving the EU <mark>removed a country that was</mark> frequently <mark>an obstacle to progress</mark>, or at least made cooperation more difficult. For example, as Prime Minister, David Cameron vetoed a new EU-wide treaty in 2011, designed to salvage the single currency. Without the UK, the EU has continued to deepen the single market, and the Eurozone has more power to drive economic and financial policy. Secondly, the EU’s far right no longer makes explicit demands to the leave the EU. Shortly after the Brexit referendum, Marine Le Pen said she would call a referendum on leaving the EU if she won the French 2017 Presidential election. Since then, Le Pen is no longer advocating for France to leave the EU, but instead to reform the EU from within, having seen the difficulties which accompany the withdrawal process. And, in <mark>any</mark> future <mark>negotiations over British membership</mark>, individual member states <mark>would</mark> likely <mark>bring</mark> their own <mark>gripes</mark> to the negotiating table – <mark>from Spain</mark> on Gibraltar, <mark>Cyprus</mark> on sovereign bases, <mark>Ireland</mark> on Northern Ireland, <mark>and Greece</mark> on those Marbles. And, while EU member states might accept the UK back into the fold, it would not be on the terms that the UK once had. As a member state, the UK had several unique ‘opt-outs’, most notably from the Euro and Eurozone, from being part of the border-free Schengen area, and numerous exemptions from justice and home affairs legislation and the Fundamental Charter of Human Rights. However, as a new applicant the UK would be obliged to being part of those bits of the EU that it used to avoid – including use of the Euro.

## Construction-criteria audit

### 1. Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanisms — no magnitude, probability, timeframe, or reversibility comparisons pre-written into the cards. For instance, the Markley '16 card asserts EU cohesion solves "climate change, infectious disease, nuclear proliferation, financial crisis, cyber insecurity" but never quantifies the risk of cohesion loss or compares it against the Aff's economic benefits. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like **"EU cohesion collapse triggers immediate great-power conflict — magnitude outweighs any bilateral trade gain"** (citing a 2023–24 IR security study) and would be placed at the bottom of Contention 1 as a mini-weighing module.

### 2. Offensive spikes
**Schelkle 24** — *"the UK’s eventual expulsion was accompanied with greater closure and cohesion of the remaining EU members"* / *"the EU – despite the potential for internal division – managed to present a united front"* / *"inclusion and prioritisation of member states’ concerns and hence their disciplined adherence to collective decisions"*  
**How it executes:** This card preempts the Aff’s likely "EU is fragile / Brexit weakened the EU" narrative by empirically documenting post-Brexit cohesion gains and the mechanism (elite-driven consensus, low salience) that sustains them. It turns the Aff’s breach argument into a Con link: Brexit *proved* the EU can cohere under stress, so rejoining reintroduces a known disruptor.

**Luke 24** — *"Brexit has strengthened the EU… removed a country that was frequently an obstacle to progress… the EU’s far right no longer makes explicit demands to leave the EU"* / *"in any future negotiations over British membership, individual member states would likely bring their own gripes to the negotiating table – from Spain on Gibraltar, Cyprus on sovereign bases, Ireland on Northern Ireland, and Greece on those Marbles"*  
**How it executes:** Preempts the Aff’s "EU wants the UK back / re-entry is smooth" claim by listing concrete veto players and historical grievances (Gibraltar, sovereign bases, Northern Ireland, Elgin Marbles) that turn re-accession into a fragmentation vector. The card also spikes the "far-right contagion" Aff argument by showing Le Pen abandoned Frexit after seeing Brexit’s difficulty.

### 3. Defensive spikes
**Luke 24** — *"Labour has ruled out any attempt to re-join the EU, at least in the near future… the way back to full EU membership is difficult… just 27% of voters think it is likely that EU member states would accept the UK’s application"*  
**How it executes:** Frontlines the Aff’s solvency claim by establishing (a) UK domestic political constraint (Labour’s refusal), (b) procedural hurdle (unanimous 27-state consent), and (c) public skepticism — making the plan’s passage and implementation improbable.

**TFEU 2012** — *"the adoption of a common customs tariff in their relations with third countries"* / *"prohibition between Member States of customs duties on imports and exports and of all charges having equivalent effect"*  
**How it executes:** Preempts the Aff’s "we keep independent trade deals" perm by legally entrenching the EU’s exclusive competence over external tariffs — rejoining *requires* surrendering the DCTS, India FTA, and US tariff negotiations cited in C2.

### 5. Collapse flexibility
**Present.** The case offers three independent impact pathways:  
- **C1 (Political/Security):** EU cohesion → prevents great-power war, climate inaction, pandemic unpreparedness (Markley 16).  
- **C2 (Economic/Development):** Independent trade policy → India FTA (Singh 25), DCTS for 65 developing nations/3.3B people (FCDO 24, GB 23), US) → poverty reduction via growth (Maizland 08).  
- **C3 (Innovation/Health):** Biotech boom post-Brexit (Tomlinson 8/25, UK Gov 25) enabled by regulatory autonomy (Hoffer 22).  
Each contention has distinct links (cohesion, trade sovereignty, regulatory freedom) and distinct terminal impacts (security, poverty, health), allowing the 2NR to collapse to any one without conceding the others.

### 6. Warrant depth
**Uneven.**  
- **Deep:** Schelkle 24 (mechanism: elite-driven negotiations, low salience → disciplined adherence); Hoffer 22 (mechanism: MHRA independence → innovation initiatives → quicker authorization); TFEU 2012 (legal text of exclusive competence).  
- **Shallow:** Hutton 25 (raw GDP % with no causal link to Brexit); BCC 25 (monthly trade volumes, no counterfactual); Singh 25 (FTA provisions listed, no evidence they *require* Brexit — India could sign with EU); Tomlinson 8/25 (investment totals, no comparison to EU-member biotech sectors); UK Gov 25 (investment announcement, no proof Brexit caused it).  
- **Missing warrant card:** **"Brexit-specific regulatory divergence (e.g., MHRA rolling review, clinical-trial simplification) caused X% faster drug approvals vs. EMA 2021–24"** (cite: MHRA-EMA comparative audit 2024) — belongs in C3 after Hoffer 22.

### 7. Internal consistency
**Consistent.** All three contentions reinforce a single framing: **Brexit = strategic autonomy → cohesion, trade sovereignty, regulatory agility → net benefits**. No contention undercuts another: C1 argues the EU is stronger *without* the UK; C2 argues the UK is stronger *outside* the EU’s common commercial policy; C3 argues the UK’s life-sciences sector is stronger *outside* the EU’s regulatory orbit. The Luke 24 and TFEU cards explicitly bind the three by showing rejoining forces simultaneous loss of all three advantages (veto players block re-entry, customs union kills FTAs/DCTS, regulatory realignment slows biotech).
