as a new legal battleground Reuters

ets-emerge-new-legal-battleground--pracin-2025-11-06/
1-28-2026 // [mrLB]
November 06, 2025 - Last fall, political prediction markets
garnered significant attention as market participants could buy and
sell contracts on outcomes of the presidential race and other
congressional races. But a new vehicle for prediction markets has
begun to emerge: sports event contracts.
Event contracts are regulated by the federal Commodity Futures
Trading Commission (CFTC) and are generally defined as derivative
contracts where the payoff is based on a specific event,
occurrence or value, like an economic indicator, a level of rainfall
over a period or value of damages to crops. The most basic
structure of event contracts is the presentation of a binary
question where a buyer of a contract may predict that the answer
will be "yes" or "no" and a seller will take the opposite position.
At the time the contract is set to expire (i.e., when the "event" is
determined to occur), the contract's value will go to whichever
party predicted the correct answer. These contracts are generally
traded between buyers and sellers on an exchange where prices will
fluctuate according to market forces until the event occurs.
This model translates easily into sports game prediction, where
buyers and sellers can exchange event contracts related to the
outcome of sports games. For example, a buyer of a sports event
contract may decide to purchase one of these derivatives that
provides that a certain team will win the Superbowl; if the buyer's
chosen team wins, they are paid out on the event contract, but if

the buyer's chosen team does not win, the buyer must pay the
seller of the contract.
In the past year or so, multiple companies have begun entering the
sports event contract space in different ways. Kalshi is a
CFTC-designated contract market that has offered futures like
odds on championships or outright winners since January 2025, but
more recently has allowed trading on more specific events related
to football, such as the number of touchdowns a certain player may
score during a certain game, point spreads and the overall total
score of a game.
A new filing with the CFTC has also indicated that Kalshi may soon
begin allowing contracts to trade that allow predictions on multiple
outcomes, assuming there is sufficient liquidity in the underlying
components' markets. Robinhood has recently announced a new
partnership with Kalshi to allow users of the Robinhood platform to
trade on the outcomes of professional and college football games.
Polymarket's CEO Shayne Coplan said on X that the platform has
the "green light" to begin offering event contracts in the U.S.
again following a no-action letter from the CFTC. And Crypto.com
newly announced a partnership with Underdog, a fantasy sports and
gaming company, where they will begin offering sports event
contracts in 16 states, as reported by CNBC (Sept. 2, 2025).
With this rapid expansion, companies offering sports event
contracts are already facing significant legal challenges from
gaming regulatory authorities, and may continue to face similar
opposition. After facing adverse regulatory action, Kalshi sued
state gaming authorities in several states to block the regulators'
enjoinment of its sports event contracts offerings. See KalshiEX,
LLC v. Hendrick, Case No. 2:25-CV-00575-APG-BNW (D. Nev.);
KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty, Case No. 25-CV-02152-ESK-MJS
(D.N.J.); KalshiEX LLC v. Martin, Case No. 25-cv-1283-ABA (D.
Md.); KalshiEXLLC v. Schuler, Case No. 2:25-cv-1165-SDM-CMV

(S.D. Ohio); KalshiexLLC v. Williams, Case No. 25-cv-9946
(S.D.N.Y.).
The primary argument made by Kalshi in its suits is that the
federal Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) preempts any state-law
challenges to event contracts traded on exchanges regulated by the
federal CFTC. The CEA grants the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over
these event contracts, see7 U.S.C. § 2, and the CEA is so
expansive and comprehensive that it "reflects congressional intent
to occupy the field of regulating CFTC-designated exchanges and
the transactions conducted on those exchanges." KalshiEX, LLC v.
Hendrick, 2025 WL 1073495, at *6 (D. Nev. Apr. 9, 2025).
Thus far, Kalshi has had success in New Jersey and Nevada where
federal district courts granted its requests for preliminary
injunctions against state gaming authorities' actions involving the
company's event contracts. Seeid. at *8; KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty,
2025 WL 1218313, at *1 (D.N.J. Apr. 28, 2025). The New
Jersey state gaming authority has since appealed the decision to
the 3rd U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. See KalshiEX LLC v.
Flaherty, Case No. 25-1922 (3d Cir.).
The Court heard oral argument on the appeal on Sept. 10, 2025,
but a decision has not yet been issued. Robinhood has also filed
suit against gaming authorities in Nevada and New Jersey to enjoin
them from enforcing state gaming laws against Robinhood involving
the same sports event contracts using the same preemption
arguments. See Robinhood Derivatives, LLC v. Dreitzer, Case No.
25-cv-1541 (D. Nev.); Robinhood Derivatives, LLC v. Flaherty,
Case No. 25-cv-14723 (D.N.J.).
Breaking with the New Jersey and Nevada courts' rulings, the
District of Maryland denied Kalshi's same request for a preliminary
injunction because it said that Kalshi failed to show a likelihood of
success on the merits. KalshiEX LLC v. Martin, 2025 WL 2194908,
at *1 (D. Md. Aug. 1, 2025). The court opined that Congress had
not intended to occupy the entire field of relevant law, id. at *11,

and Kalshi could not show that compliance with both Maryland law
and the CEA was impossible. Id. at *13. Kalshi has appealed to the
4th U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals. KalshiEX LLC v. Martin, Case
No. 25-1892 (4th Cir.).
The state of Massachusetts has itself sued Kalshi alleging that the
company has promoted and accepted online sports wagers in the
form of sports event contracts without complying with state gaming
laws. Massachusetts v. KalshiEX LLC, Case No. 25-cv-12595 (D.
Mass.). The state requests a declaration that Kalshi is violating
the state gambling laws, monetary relief and an injunction from
Kalshi engaging in sports wagering without a license.
No decision has yet been issued, and Kalshi has not yet responded
to the state's claims. Citing the Massachusetts AG's case against
Kalshi, Robinhood preemptively filed an injunction in federal court
to prevent that state from enforcing its gambling laws against
Robinhood's sports event contracts. See Robinhood Derivatives, LLC
v. Campbell, Case No. 25-cv-12578 (D. Mass.).
Companies offering sports event contracts may find additional
challenges from state authorities in the future. In the Kalshi
appeal to the 3rd Circuit, 34 states, the District of Columbia, and
the Northern Mariana Islands filed an amicus brief in support of
the New Jersey gaming authorities. Brief of Amici Curiae, KalshiEX
LLC v. Flaherty, Case No. 25-1922 (3d Cir. June 17, 2025).
Ohio authorities have alerted licensees that they consider sports
event contracts to be sports gaming which must be licensed in
Ohio. Arizona has warned licensees in a similar manner, as reported
in InGame. ("Arizona Warns Regulated Operators, Via Letter,
Against Offering Prediction Markets," Sept. 17, 2025.)
Ultimately, the argument that federal law preempts state law
attempting to regulate the sports event contracts is likely to
prevail based on the Supremacy Clause, but in spite of this, other
states may ramp up enforcement against sports event contracts,
particularly in light of Kalshi's preliminary loss in Maryland.

One of my major assignments with IBJ was to research and write a
paper on the connection between democracy and the rule of law.
The purpose was to demonstrate that democracy could not exist
without the rule of law, and that only caring about the rule of law
during elections is simply not enough to sustain democracy. I am
hopeful that some of my language will be useful for IBJ grant
applications in the future. The Rule of Law is a millennia old
principal referring to the way by which states are governed. As
compared with Rule by Law, where the government uses the law to
govern and is considered to be above the law, Rule of Law means
that all entities, including the government, must adhere to the
supremacy of the law. The Rule of Law is a nearly a universal
value, and the United Nations General Assembly regularly identifies
“human rights, the rule of law and democracy” as universal and
indivisible values of the United Nations. The United Nations has
also prioritized the Rule of Law in Sustainable Development Goal 16
(SDG16): Peace, Justice and Strong Institutions. Specifically,

SDG16 emphasizes that the Rule of Law plays a key role in
promoting “peaceful, just, and inclusive societies and . . . ensuring
sustainable development.” One of the SDG16 targets is to promote
the rule of law at both national and international levels to ensure
equal access to justice for all. The Rule of Law is closely linked
with the ideals of democracy. A democratic state under the Rule
of Law is a state where citizens elect their own leaders, and the
government itself is bound by the law, while also helping to ensure
that the law is respected among the citizens of the state.
Democracy cannot exist without the Rule of Law, especially the
rule that dictates who should occupy public office given the results
of elections. However, only supporting the Rule of Law during an
election season is not enough. Democratic stability depends on a
self-enforcing equilibrium. In other words, political officials must
respect democracy’s limits on their actions, particularly regarding
the rights of citizens. Institutions that are self-perpetuating and
do not operate based on individuality of single actors are powerful
actors stabilizing that equilibrium. In a stable, self-perpetuating
institution all conflicts are solved according to the institutional
rules, and therefore, the Rule of Law stabilizes the democratic
society. Rule of Law in a democratic institution allows governments
to work their will through general legislation, and then to be
subject to that legislation themselves. Democratic stability depends
on the self-enforcing equilibrium of the Rule of Law, which is often
inherently vulnerable. The viability of the Rule of Law ultimately
depends on the citizens: if they elect leaders who will violate the
Rule of Law, the Rule of Law will decline rapidly. In fragile,
conflict-affected societies, the Rule of Law is particularly fragile.
Legislation and regulations for maintaining order do not have an
immediate effect on behavior or security, and thus on democracy.
Implementing sound Rule of Law principles in conflict-affected
societies creates distinct challenges, because in addition to
promoting the Rule of Law in judicial and legislative institutions, the

security sector—including the military, police, and prisons—must
also have a firm foundation in the Rule of Law. International
Bridges to Justice promotes the Rule of Law around the globe by
encouraging early access to the accused, cooperating with
governments to create best practice legislation, creating awareness
about basic rights and protections within a system, and training
defenders to provide effective representation. Additionally,
International Bridges to Justice is at the forefront of implementing
SDG 16, particularly the access to justice component. IBJ’s work
strengthening the Rule of Law around the world has inherently
strengthened democracy as well. Previously, I had only given
thought to democracy and the rule of law as separate but
intersecting concepts. This assignment allowed me to think much
deeper about the how the two concepts are entirely codependent.
IBJ’s work to promote the rule of law is essential in promoting
democracy around the globe. I am hopeful that the research I did
can be of some help in furthering this mission.
