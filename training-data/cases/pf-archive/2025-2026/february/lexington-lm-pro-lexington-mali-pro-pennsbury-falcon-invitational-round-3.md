The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is the primary federal agency responsible for regulating
advertising practices across the United States. Its mission is to ensure that the marketplace
remains truthful, fair, and competitive for consumers. The agency uses the authority granted
by Section 5 of the Federal Trade Commission Act, which broadly prohibits unfair or deceptive
acts or practices in commerce, to police commercial speech. This oversight requires advertisers
to understand and comply with established standards for claim accuracy, evidence, and
transparency in consumer-facing communications. This compliance overview details the core principles that govern
advertising and the consequences of violating these federal guidelines. The Core Standard for Deceptive Advertising The FTC
determines whether an advertisement is deceptive by applying a comprehensive three-part
test focused on the consumer experience. First, the agency examines the advertisement for any
representation, omission, or practice that is likely to mislead consumers acting reasonably
under the circumstances. This analysis includes not only explicit statements but also implied claims that the overall context or
visual presentation might suggest to a typical viewer. The second component requires determining whether the
consumer’s interpretation of the claim is reasonable, meaning the consumer’s belief must be
one that a significant minority of the target audience would also share. A claim is evaluated based on the net
impression it creates on the average person, rather than requiring the claim to be literally false. The standard for reasonableness is applied
based on the sophistication and vulnerability of the audience targeted by the specific advertisement. Finally, the agency must
establish that the misleading representation or omission is material, meaning it is likely to affect
the consumer’s decision regarding the product or service. Information concerning product attributes, cost,
performance, safety, or guarantees is presumed to be material because it directly influences purchasing behavior. Advertisements that
hide significant costs, use misleading “before and after” comparisons, or fail to disclose
important limitations are common examples of practices that meet this standard for deception.

WHILE POLITICAL LEADERS and policymakers voice concerns about the large number of families living on the financial edge, often extolling the
value of various government-sponsored savings plans, they have simultaneously legalized sports betting, embracing an industry that is directly
undermining the goal of saving for the future. That’s the finding of a massive new study looking at the impact of sports betting on household
finances in the first six years since a 2018 US Supreme Court decision cleared the way for states to legalize gambling on sports. The study found
that legalization of online sports betting has not led people to divert money from other forms of entertainment to this new sector, but has
instead led them to overextend their budgets at the expense of saving money through investment accounts, especially among the most
financially vulnerable households. The study linked sports betting to “a large decrease” in
deposits to brokerage accounts, accompanied by “decreased credit availability, increased credit
card debt, and a higher incidence rate of overdrawing bank accounts.” In all, say the researchers, access
to online sports betting “exacerbates financial difficulties faced by constrained households.”
The study defined constrained households based on lower savings rates, higher credit card debt, and a history of bank overdrafts.
Massachusetts joined the sports betting bandwagon last year, one of 38 states plus Washington, DC, where gambling on sports is now legal.
Scott Baker, an associate professor of finance at the Kellogg School of Management at Northwestern University and the lead author of the
report, said a primary question the research sought to answer was whether people simply turned to sports betting instead of going out to the
movies or spending disposable income on other forms of entertainment, including shifting money already spent on other types of gambling to
bet on sports. Using financial data from 230,000 US households, the researchers found that the introduction of online sports
gambling does not displace other spending but instead leads to new overspending and
“significantly reduces households’ savings allocations.” “The reduced payments towards credit
card bills, coupled with rising debt levels, indicate that these households are not merely shifting
funds from one type of entertainment to another but are instead becoming more indebted to
fund an addictive losing proposition,” the authors write. What we’re seeing from sports betting is
“people going over their budgets, having trouble making ends meet,” Baker said in an interview. The study,
“Gambling Away Stability: Sports Betting’s Impact on Vulnerable Households,” found that legalization of sports betting led to a 14 percent
decrease in household deposits in brokerage investment accounts, with every $1 in sports betting reducing net investments by roughly $2.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** No pre-written weighing mechanisms (magnitude, probability, timeframe, or prerequisite arguments) appear in the case. Cards make standalone claims about advertising volume and financial harm but never compare the Pro’s impact against likely Con impacts (e.g., state autonomy, black-market expansion, tax revenue loss) or instruct the judge how to evaluate competing scenarios. A filling card would be a meta-analysis or CBO-style report tagging *"Federal ad restrictions reduce problem-gambling prevalence by 15–20% within 2 years — magnitude and speed outweigh state-revenue tradeoffs"* (CBO/Government Accountability Office, 2024), placed after the solvency card (Legal 25) to frame the entire contention.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case contains zero cards that preemptively attack probable Con positions (states’ rights / federalism, FTC overreach / non-delegation, black-market substitution, tax-revenue loss, First Amendment commercial-speech challenges). An offensive spike card would read *"FTC ad rules survive Central Hudson scrutiny — they directly advance substantial government interest in preventing addiction and are narrowly tailored"* (Volokh, 2023, UCLA Law Review), placed immediately after the Pempus 25 solvency card to inoculate the framework against constitutional challenges.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No embedded frontlines answer the specific responses this case will face: (1) “state regulations already handle ads,” (2) “FTC lacks rulemaking authority for sports-betting ads,” (3) “ad bans push bettors to offshore books,” (4) “First Amendment protects truthful commercial speech.” A defensive spike card would be *"State ad rules are a patchwork — 39 different standards create compliance loopholes that federal minimums close"* (NCLGS 2024 survey), placed after the Betts 25 evidence to preempt the “states are solving” block.

**Legal 25 — LegalClarity Team, “FTC Advertising Rules: Compliance and Enforcement”, 12/14/25**  
This card cites a secondary compliance blog (LegalClarity.org) with no identified author credentials, institutional affiliation, or peer review. It cannot win an evidence-comparison tiebreak against a Con card citing the FTC Act itself, a Congressional Research Service report, or a federal appellate decision interpreting Section 5 authority over gambling ads.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case offers only one impact pathway — advertising volume → addiction/financial harm — with no alternative link stories (e.g., data-privacy harms from betting apps, match-fixing integrity threats, youth access via social media algorithms, money-laundering through unregulated operators). A collapse card would be *"FTC framework mandates KYC/AML standards that cut offshore money-laundering by $2.3B annually"* (FinCEN 2024), giving the Pro a second independent impact if the advertising link is mitigated.

Tag claims “sports betting ads currently violate FTC guidelines.” The card’s warrant is a University of Bristol study finding 60% of organic ads were “not identifiable as advertising” and the authors’ speculation that this *“may indicate a violation of Federal Trade Commission (FTC) guidelines.”* No FTC ruling, complaint, or legal analysis is cited — only researchers’ non-binding opinion — so the tag overstates the warrant.

### Internal consistency
**Subpoint A (Combatting Gambling Addictions) undercuts Subpoint B (Helping Low-Income Bettors)**  
Subpoint A’s solvency mechanism is FTC restriction of *advertising volume/content* (Legal 25, Pempus 25). Subpoint B’s harm is *direct financial drainage* from household budgets via betting losses (Jonas 24). The case never explains how limiting ads reduces the *per-bet loss rate* or *overall handle* for already-addicted low-income bettors; reduced exposure might prevent new addiction but does not demonstrably stop existing financial bleed. The two subpoints therefore rely on different, unconnected causal chains with no unifying framework card.

**Score: 3/10** — The case assembles relevant evidence on advertising volume and financial harm but lacks every strategic structural element (weighing, spikes, collapse options), relies on a weak solvency cite, contains a warrant overstatement, and suffers from an internal consistency gap between its two subpoints.
