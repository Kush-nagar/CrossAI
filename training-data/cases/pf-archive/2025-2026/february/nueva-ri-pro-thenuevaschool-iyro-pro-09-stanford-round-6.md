<mark>Advertising for sportsbooks</mark> and DFS commonly occurs on the Internet, particularly social media,169 and some of this advertising likely <mark>misleads consumers</mark>. Online advertising requires oversight from <mark>the FTC</mark>, as the Federal Trade Commission Act “<mark>prohibits</mark> unfair or <mark>deceptive advertising in any medium</mark>.”170 Although the Internet did not exist when President Wilson signed the Act into law in 1914, the FTC’s jurisdiction over Internet advertising has generally not been questioned. This authority to enact and enforce regulations on online advertising might appear broad, but the Commission simply applies the same rulemaking and enforcement mechanisms to the Internet that already apply to traditional forms of advertising.171 In other words, the FTC treats Internet advertising as having the same fundamentals as all other forms of marketing.173 From a practical standpoint, the key difference between traditional and influencer advertising is that influencers, through their presence as “content creators” on social media, can disguise advertisements as entertainment.174 Furthermore, social media blurs the line between friend and public figure, and followers develop parasocial relationships175 with online personalities trying to sell them products.176 In simplest terms, this resembles the relationship a bar patron develops with a bartender. Legalizing sports betting will generate new tax revenues only if spending on other types of gambling remain unchanged, or increase following legalization.¶ This paper exploits the legalization of sports betting in West Virginia, different opening dates for sports books in the state, and an unexpected suspension of sports betting at two casinos to investigate the impact of newly legalized sports betting on revenues from table games and VLTs in casinos using an IV and difference-in-differences approach. The evidence paper develops evidence consistent with important substitution between consumer spending on sports betting and spending on VLTs in casinos, called “cannibalization” in the gambling literature. The results imply substantial fiscal implications: <mark>legalized sports betting generated</mark> about <mark>$2.6 million in new tax revenues</mark> from September 2018 to March 2020, <mark>but consumer substitution</mark> into sports betting and <mark>away from VLT wagering caused</mark> a <mark>reduction</mark> in tax revenues generated by VLTs <mark>by</mark> about <mark>$45 million</mark> over the same period. Legalization of sports betting carried a substantial opportunity cost in the form of forgone VLT tax revenues in West Virginia.¶ These results should give state policy makers considering legalization of sports betting pause. While new revenue streams from legalized sports betting appear attractive on the surface, states already generate substantial tax revenues from gambling, and the introduction of sports betting to this mix does not leave spending on other forms of gambling untouched. States may implicitly assume that the introduction of sports betting will induce people who formerly placed bets on sporting events in Nevada, where sports betting has been legal for decades, or illegally with clandestine local bookmakers or online with offshore bookmakers to instead patronize local casinos with sports books. This likely occurs, but the legalization of sports betting also induces existing casino customers to change their spending patterns.¶ These results should also lead state policy makers to re-examine tax policy on all types of gambling. West Virginia taxes net VLT revenues at 53.5% and new sports book revenues at 10%. This differential magnified the overall fiscal effect of the change in gambler’s spending patterns generated by legalization. Changing the mix of tax rates applied to different types of gambling could mitigate the fiscal consequences of legalization.

Cards like Tonko 23 and Wardle 22 provide impact evidence but do not contain comparative weighing language that would allow debaters to extend "this outweighs on magnitude because X" without generating new analysis. A filling card would be a 2023–2025 source explicitly comparing the scale of gambling harms (e.g., 20M affected, 15× suicide risk) to typical counter-impacts like state tax revenue or black-market migration, tagged: **"Weighing — Gambling harms outweigh state revenue/black market: magnitude (20M people), probability (empirically observed), irreversibility (suicide/debt), and scope (families/communities) — FTC framework uniquely solves."** Placed after the Wardle 22 card in the Advertising contention.

Card text includes *full regression controls* (demographics, SES, marketing exposure), *odds ratios with 95% CIs*, and *mechanism* (direct marketing receipt → unplanned spend). Warrant is complete: correlation + controls + dose-response (1 form vs. 2+ forms of direct marketing).

**Spencer 2024** — Tag: "DraftKings sued for deceptive 'risk-free' promos." Card text is a *news summary of a single lawsuit*; no warrant that this is systemic, no data on prevalence across operators, no link to FTC framework solving it. Anecdote passed as warrant.

### Internal consistency
**INCONSISTENT:** Overview (Tonko 23, Sargeant 2022, Whitfield 24) frames the *entire* sports betting regime as predatory and calls for federal *intervention* (Tonko's bill *bans* ads; Sargeant argues "only response to a rigged game is to quit"). Advertising contention (Navin, Spencer, Eisenshtadt) argues for *federal regulation of deceptive ads* under existing FTC authority — a *targeted consumer-protection* model, not a ban. The case never reconciles: does the FTC framework *ban* sports betting ads (Tonko/Sargeant) or *regulate* their truthfulness (Eisenshtadt/Navin)? This tension lets Neg concede "regulation good" on Advertising while extending "Pro's own authors say ban necessary" on Overview, creating a double-bind. A fixing card would be a 2024–2025 FTC-commissioner statement or congressional testimony tagged: **"Framing — FTC framework = comprehensive consumer-protection floor (ad restrictions, affordability checks, self-exclusion), not prohibition; reconciles Tonko's ad-ban with Navin's regulatory floor — [Source 2024/25]."** Placed as new Overview closer.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 5/10** — The case has two elite pieces of evidence (Wardle 22, Eisenshtadt 22) and three distinct link chains enabling collapse flexibility, but it lacks any embedded weighing, offensive/defensive spikes, or internal framing consistency, and half the cards are claim-only or low-quality; it reads as a strong evidence set waiting for strategic architecture.
