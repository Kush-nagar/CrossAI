Scholar argues thatrestrictions on sports-betting advertising can align with commercial free speech

protections. Anyone who ventures online these days will see sports-betting advertisements everywhere. The omnipresence of these ads
stems from a 2018 U.S. Supreme Court decision that struck down a decades-old ban on sports betting. Since then, 38 states have legalized
sports betting. Advertising has followed, raising concerns about harmful effects, including problem gambling and underage involvement.
Regulators face an important question: Can anything be done to limit the prevalence of advertising for sports gambling in an age of heightened
commercial speech protection? Mark Conrad, a professor at Fordham University confronts this question in a recent article, proposing that the
Federal Communications Commission (FCC) use its power over broadcasting to regulate sports-betting advertisements. Conrad explains that
rules governing sports-betting advertising, which vary by state, are often narrowly focused on prohibiting “false and deceptive” ads and
requiring disclosures about responsible gambling. Although these rules help to deter underage gambling and to reduce
the risk of gambling addictions, Conrad contends that they are inadequate to address the full range of harms that sports-betting
ads pose. What about ads that are not false and deceptive? Conrad argues that the sheer volume of sports-betting
advertisements contributes to rising rates of problem gambling and warrant greater regulation, even
when the ads themselves are not misleading. He contends that a broader approach on the national level, centered on
broadcast media, would complement the current state-by-state patchwork and better address gambling harms. Such broad
restrictions on advertising would, however, face constitutional barriers. Conrad observes that courts in recent decades have shown increasing
sympathy for commercial speech protections. Although commercial speech has no formal definition, the U.S. Supreme Court and lower courts
have adhered to a “‘common-sense’ distinction between speech proposing a commercial transaction and other varieties of speech.” Before
commercial speech had First Amendment protections, courts upheld restrictions on so-called sin product advertising as part of a state’s power
to protect health and safety. As Conrad explains, the idea of commercial speech protections gained traction in the late 1960s and early 1970s,
with the Court expressly extending First Amendment protection to commercial speech for the first time in 1975. Since 1980, the standard used
by courts to evaluate restrictions on commercial speech has been the Central Hudson test. Conrad describes the elements required to justify
restricting commercial speech under Central Hudson: “the government must show that the restriction had a substantial government interest;
the restriction directly advances the state’s interest; and the restriction is not more restrictive than necessary to advance that interest.” If done
the right way, broader restrictions on sports-betting advertising can satisfy the Central Hudson standard, Conrad contends. He argues that
regulating sports-gambling ads made over public broadcasting channels is the best way to reconcile constitutional protections for free speech
and limits on harmful advertising. He identifies the FCC as the agency best positioned to carry out this plan because it exercises broader
authority over broadcast content than any other part of government does over other forms of speech. A critical aspect of the FCC’s authority is
its power to restrict “indecent” broadcasts to late-night hours, when children are unlikely to be tuning in. Courts have upheld this practice
because of the FCC’s enhanced broadcasting oversight authority and because children can easily access broadcast media. Conrad proposes
extending the indecency framework to sports-betting ads, arguing that the FCC could invoke its powers to impose time-based limits on gambling
promotions, given the risks they pose to young audiences. Conrad suggests prohibiting sports-betting ads during hours
when children are most likely to watch television—such as between 6:00 a.m. and 10:00 p.m.—and
limiting ads and the display of betting odds during sporting events. This “channeling” would directly
advance the government’s interest in preventing minors from being enticed to gamble, while avoiding
the constitutional concerns of an outright ban. Conrad argues that such restrictions would likely satisfy the Central Hudson
test because they serve a substantial governmental interest, advance that interest in a direct way, and are not more restrictive than necessary.
Conrad classifies time-based restrictions as a reasonable and legally justifiable step toward limiting children’s exposure to sports betting and
preventing further problem gambling. The FCC’s indecency framework, he concludes, provides a workable guide for regulators seeking to
balance commercial speech protections with the government’s interest in shielding vulnerable audiences.

Casinos and online gambling platforms collect vast amounts of sensitive consumer data, from financial
transactions to behavioral tracking.252 While states have taken steps to regulate data privacy,the fragmented nature of
U.S. privacy laws leaves significant gaps in consumer protections.253 Without a comprehensive federal
standard, consumers remain vulnerable to data breaches, and businesses must navigate inconsistent compliance requirements across
jurisdictions.254 The solution to the challenges of online gambling and data breaches does not lie in blanket federal regulations of the gaming
industry itself but instead in adopting comprehensive, bipartisan federal privacy legislation that establishes a baseline
standard while allowing states to implement stronger protections. The success of federal-state regulatory models in
areas such as environmental law and financial consumer protections demonstrates that a federal baseline need not preempt stronger state laws
– instead, it can provide a foundation for uniformity while preserving state innovation.
Since 2019, there has been bipartisan support for more regulation to protect personal information.256 Advances in artificial intelligence and
data analytics have increased Americans’ concerns regarding protecting personal data.257 These rapidly evolving technologies create new
privacy challenges that require a flexible federal framework capable of adapting to future innovations.Only about a quarter of
Americans trust companies to handle their data responsibly when using artificial intelligence; at the same time, the

majority— 70%—have little to no confidence in companies making responsible decisions with their data. 258
This trust deficit could be addressed through comprehensive federal legislation, potentially boosting consumer confidence in digital services.
The gaming industry is particularly vulnerable due to the lack of uniform privacy legislation. While gaming is
regulated by state agencies and subject only to the regulations each state deems necessary, privacy laws are also determined at
the state level.259 This has resulted in some states regulating gaming with little to no comprehensive privacy
protections.
Moreover, some have argued that states are better equipped to respond to technological changes and data collection practices and that the
federal government cannot keep pace.272 At the same time, others argue that establishing federal standards could limit states’ power of
innovation.273 On the other hand, many believe that the APRA should set the standard, not the ceiling, for data protection.274 Opponents of
the preemption provision point to Congress’s prior adoption of federal baselines that allow states to provide additional protections.275
Examples include the Clean Air Act, which allows states like California to adopt stricter environmental
protections, or HIPAA, which establishes a federal baseline for health data privacy, grants the State Attorney
General concurrent enforcement authority, and only preempts contrary state laws—allowing states to adopt stronger protections.276 Such an
approach to the APRA would establish baseline privacy protections for all Americans while preserving states’
flexibility to enhance safeguards as needed.277 A federal framework is essential, especially in high-risk industries

operators, leaving the patron vulnerable. The gaming industry—already a prime target for
cybercriminals—exemplifies the urgent need for stronger, more consistent privacy protection. Without federal
legislation, the mosaic of state laws will continue to create loopholes, making it easier for bad actors to
exploit regulatory inconsistencies. A federal standard would protect consumers and benefit businesses by simplifying compliance.
A unified framework would reduce legal uncertainty and promote greater trust in digital platforms.
However, federal legislation must not override stronger state protections to be genuinely effective. Instead, it should work with
existing law to create a robust, adaptable system.

Specifically, a recent lawsuit filed by the City of Baltimore should serve as an example for a broad
theory of liability against sportsbooks, which can, in turn, direct them to regulate their own practices

out of fear of having to make large payouts. In the suit, Baltimore lays out allegations that DraftKings
and FanDuel, two popular sportsbooks, knew the danger of their products and methodically targeted
customers who they knew were susceptible to gambling addiction based on analytical trends. Using
these facts, Baltimore argued that the sportsbooks violated a local consumer protection law by using
intentionally deceptive and abusive business practices to maximize profit. While the suit in Baltimore
relies on a specific local code, the basic factual allegations can be used for a number of other causes of
action. First, every state has its own set of consumer protection laws, some of which may support a viable claim. The difficulty for plaintiffs in
any such case would likely be showing actually deceptive practices on the part of sportsbooks. After all, it is common knowledge that the
average bettor will lose money, and gambling advertisements already carry warnings about the dangers of gambling. Next, using the
Baltimore fact pattern, plaintiffs can also test certain tort claims. In particular, product liability seems to
be a logical fit. According to the complaint filed by Baltimore, DraftKings and FanDuel each operate
differently in the United States than they do in the United Kingdom. As a result of foreign regulation,
these sportsbooks took affirmative steps to protect vulnerable British users by limiting promotions and
implementing age-based restrictions. Crucially, this suggests that there is an alternative method of
product delivery available—but not implemented—which can maintain the basic utility of sports
betting while providing at least some protections for users. Finally, potential plaintiffs should consider claims of
intentional infliction of emotional distress. While such claims often fail based on the incredibly high burden of showing conduct is “outrageous,”
the behavior of sportsbooks is shocking enough to create a legitimate argument. The Baltimore lawsuit reveals a pattern of
intentional exploitation on the part of sportsbooks: tailored notifications and bonuses are given to
frequent (losing) bettors—in other words, the consumers most likely to be struggling with addiction. These
tactics are incredibly sophisticated and have been a major force behind the increased revenue generated from gambling. To be sure, any of these
claims would face an uphill battle given that it is ultimately the bettor’s own choice to continue betting, which leads to massive losses and raises
significant causation questions. However, these claims are nonetheless compelling because they speak to what
products liability and tort law intend to do: promote efficiency by pushing losses onto the actors with
greater control over the loss-generating action. In this case, it is overwhelmingly clear that sportsbooks
bear the ultimate responsibility for enabling problematic gambling behavior. It is inherently difficult for
addicts to control their addictions. Yet it is remarkably easy for sportsbooks to limit the accessibility of
their products to gambling addicts. They have the tools to identify problematic behavior, and they have
the power to cut off users exhibiting that behavior. As such, by imposing liability on sportsbooks, courts
can more accurately assign the losses that flow from gambling and subsequently force sportsbooks to
take measures to limit these losses. To conclude, the legal solution to the sports betting problem largely
depends on litigation based on addiction. However, the benefits from limiting the pervasiveness of betting would also be positive
for sports themselves. Sports are an escape from the challenges of daily life. In their ideal form, they offer both action and community. While
there has always been a historical connection between gambling and sports, this connection has grown out of control in recent years. To reclaim
a positive sports culture and to make sports safer for both players and fans, the time to challenge the role of gambling is right now.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case lacks explicit embedded weighing mechanisms such as magnitude, probability, or timeframe comparisons between impacts. No card contains pre-written weighing language (e.g., "outweighs on magnitude," "first on probability," "short-term vs. long-term") that would allow debaters to extend comparative impact calculus in the back half. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Weighing — Magnitude: Underage gambling affects 17% of HS students daily (Gerber 24) vs. state revenue discrepancies (Faulconer 19) — youth addiction crisis outweighs economic inefficiency on scope and irreversibility" and belong at the bottom of C1 or as a standalone "Weighing" contention.

### Offensive spikes
Schuette 25 (Regulatory Review, 2025) — "Regulation on advertising is constitutional – simple daytime bans ensure protection of underage gambling" — preempts the likely Con First Amendment challenge by citing the Central Hudson test and Conrad's FCC indecency framework, arguing time-based restrictions (6 a.m.–10 p.m. bans) satisfy intermediate scrutiny. This functions as an offensive spike because it turns Con's likely constitutional objection into a Pro solvency mechanism.

### Defensive spikes
Eisenshtadt 22 (Admin. Law Review, 2022) — "Luckily, the FTC already has empirical frameworks to regulate" — preempts Con's "FTC lacks authority/expertise" argument by detailing the FTC's existing Section 5 jurisdiction over internet advertising, its loot box workshop precedent, and its historical cigarette labeling enforcement role. This embedded frontline answers the predictable agency-capability attack before Con makes it.

### Evidence quality
**ABSENT:** Evidence quality is inconsistent and often insufficient for an evidence-comparison tiebreak. Gerber 24 is a high school newspaper article (Forest Scout) with a 99-student survey — low author quals, small dataset, and no peer review. Immunize Nevada 24 is a nonprofit blog post citing an uncited "[3]" study of 1,147 youth without DOI or journal. Remington 25 cuts off mid-cite with no year, publication, or URL. A tiebreak-winning card would be a peer-reviewed study (e.g., "JAMA Pediatrics 2024, n=15,000, longitudinal: daily gambling ad exposure → 3.2x odds of problem gambling by age 21") with clear author credentials (PhD, affiliation), recent data, and transparent methodology.

### Collapse flexibility
The case offers three distinct impact scenarios: (1) C1 Ads → underage gambling/mental health/debt crisis (Gerber 24, Immunize Nevada 24); (2) C2 Implementation → race-to-the-bottom tax/regulatory fragmentation + $150B illegal market (Faulconer 19, Armato 25); (3) C2 Implementation → data privacy gaps + cyber vulnerability in gaming (Gordon 25). These are independent links — Pro can collapse to any one or two without conceding the third.

### Warrant depth
**ABSENT:** Multiple taglines receive only claim + cite without internal warrants. Gerber 24's tag "Betting is becoming more accessible – leading to an insurgence in underage gambling" is supported solely by anecdotes (Mrs. Hektor, Oliver Cooper) and a 99-student opt-in survey — no causal warrant explaining *why* accessibility drives underage participation beyond correlation. Immunize Nevada 24's tag "Otherwise gambling advertisements prove devastating for minors– continuing to exacerbate the mental health and debt crisis" quotes a study ("[3]") with no methodology, sample details, or causal mechanism. A warranted card would include: "Longitudinal fMRI study (Nature Human Behaviour 2023, n=2,400) shows gambling ads during sports broadcasts activate adolescent reward circuitry (ventral striatum) 40% more than adults → causal pathway to impulse-control deficits."

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** C1 (Ads) and C2 (Implementation) operate on different solvency mechanisms with no unifying framing. C1 argues for FTC advertising regulations (daytime bans, disclosure rules) grounded in First Amendment commercial speech doctrine (Schuette 25, Eisenshtadt 22). C2 argues for federal legislative/regulatory uniformity on taxation, integrity monitoring, and data privacy (Faulconer 19, Armato 25, Gordon 25) — none of which the FTC has clear authority to enact. The resolution asks what the *FTC* should establish, but C2's solvency (federal guardrails, APRA-style privacy law, Silver's integrity protocols) requires Congressional action, not FTC rulemaking. This creates a topicality tension: the case does not uniformly defend "FTC establishes framework" across contentions.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 4/10** — The case has collapse flexibility and two strategic spikes (offensive + defensive), but evidence quality is critically weak for a top-50 team, warrant depth is missing on key claims, internal consistency fails on the agent of action (FTC vs. Congress), and there is zero embedded weighing — making back-half extensions vulnerable to even basic Con weighing.
