Mobile sports betting is a new and growing phenomenon in the US (Reyes Reference Reyes2024), and <mark>everything</mark> <mark>that</mark> could <mark>make</mark> <mark>someone</mark> more likely to <mark>gamble</mark> accurately <mark>describes a soldier</mark> (<mark>high-stress</mark> levels, <mark>isolation</mark> and <mark>boredom</mark>, <mark>financial incentives, psychological behaviors</mark>, etc). In dayrooms, barracks, bars, and homes around the world, soldiers are watching, and recently in greater numbers, streaming sporting events. During these hours of enjoyment, <mark>advertisers</mark> attempt to <mark>influence soldier</mark>s’ <mark>behavior</mark> on what to consume. The list includes beverage companies (Coca Cola, Pepsi, Gatorade, and Powerade), apparel lines (Adidas and Nike), car manufacturers (Toyota and Ford), and, of course, the alcoholic beverage and spirits industry (Bud Light, Michelob, and Budweiser) (Beren, Reference Beren2024). Recently, however, a new entrant to the sports advertising space has emerged: gambling, which encourages consumption of something that could possibly have significant negative effects on soldiers.
Gambling-related <mark>ads</mark> have come to <mark>dominate</mark> live <mark>sports</mark> <mark>broadcasts</mark>, with sports fans encountering gambling messages as often as three times a minute (McCarthy, Reference McCarthy2025). This exposure has helped fuel the rise of mobile sports betting, transforming the landscape of gambling, making sports betting more accessible than ever. And while this presents opportunities for entertainment and potential profit, it also <mark>pose</mark>s <mark>significant threats</mark> <mark>to</mark> the <mark>financial well-being of military personnel and</mark> ultimately unit <mark>readiness.</mark> Former Secretary of Defense Austin asserted that service member and family economic security is a priority when he stated that “[o]ur <mark>people and</mark> our <mark>readiness remain</mark> <mark>inextricably linked</mark>” (2021). This report explores new findings and early <mark>research</mark> <mark>about</mark> the rise of mobile <mark>sport betting</mark> and the <mark>risks</mark> it poses for <mark>soldiers</mark>, including the potential to <mark>exacerbate</mark> <mark>financial instability</mark>, <mark>addiction</mark>, and broader <mark>mental health challenges</mark>.
This is a relevant topic for academic research, but also for personal finance education and for policy; it is critically important that those who serve the country stay financially secure and mentally healthy. In addition to overviewing the evidence on mobile sport betting, we will provide suggestions on how financial education, policy, and programs could help mitigate and prevent future problems.
The rise of mobile sports betting
In 2018, the Supreme Court repealed the ban on sports betting, leading to 38 states and Washington D.C. to legalize some form of sports gambling. Of these 38 states, 30 allow mobile sports betting. Many of these states are home to some of the Army’s largest posts, including Fort Bragg (NC), Fort Campbell (KY), Fort Carson (CO), Fort Riley (KS), and Fort Drum (NY).
With the legalization and rapid proliferation of mobile sports betting applications, millions of Americans, including military personnel, have access to online wagering platforms at any time of day. From DraftKings, FanDuel, BetMGM, Fanatics, ESPN Bet, and others, these companies inundate soldiers with easy ways to place bets. This accessibility can lead to problematic gambling behaviors, especially among vulnerable populations like soldiers who may experience stress, deployment-related challenges, and financial pressures.
This proliferation has resulted in the average number of users growing dramatically in a short period of time. For example, in 2023, DraftKings reported 3.5 million average monthly users, up from 1.5 million at the end of 2022, while FanDuel reported 2.5 million average monthly users during the same period (Gambling Addiction Hotlines, 2024). While it is difficult to determine the exact number of soldiers in the reported users, a recent St. Bonaventure/Siena Research survey estimates that almost 1 in 5 Americans have an online sports betting account (2024). Assuming the force is the representative of the American public, this could mean nearly <mark>100</mark>,000 <mark>of the 461,000</mark> active-duty <mark>soldiers</mark> could <mark>have accounts</mark>, not to mention a similar number for the Army National Guard and Army Reserves, whose numbers total 500,000 in service.
The large Army posts mentioned above are also heavily populated with young, enlisted soldiers, with 71% of enlisted soldiers between the ages of 18-30. According to the website www.mobilebettingsite.com, which helps users find the best mobile betting app, about 14% of their respondents’ ages 18 to 29 state that they bet on sports. Of the same respondents, 20% of males state that they participate in sports betting, compared to just 7% of females. This is an important finding given that the Army enlisted population is 83% male (“Best Betting Apps For Your Phone 2024 | Mobile Betting Site” 2021).
Unfortunately, this is even a lower bound to the number of soldiers participating in this activity. A 2021 CivicScience survey found that 53% of Americans aged 21 to 29 bet on sports online, greatly increasing the numbers estimated above (Brode Reference Brode2021). There is little reason to think that the forces in this category are radically different from their civilian peers. Their participation in this activity is just another threat to the well-being of those who serve.
Vulnerable population
A reason to focus on soldiers is that they are a risk-seeking population, engaging in high-risk activities, and seeking challenges to benefit the Army mission (Arif et al. Reference Arif, Hoopsick, Lynn Homish and Homish2024). Research on the value of statistical life (VSL), the willingness to trade-off wealth and mortality risk, shows soldiers require less compensation to take risk than their U.S. civilian counterparts (Greenberg et al., n.d., Reference Greenberg2021). This translates to soldiers willing to pay less to reduce their chance of dying in a given year, effectively quantifying a higher risk threshold (Robinson, Reference Robinsonn.d., 2021)Footnote 1 . In other words, <mark>soldiers are more tolerant of risk</mark>, a feature that may make them more susceptible to gambling online (van der Maas and Nower 2021).
Moreover, soldiers, especially the young ones and those in lower ranks or with <mark>limited financial education</mark>, may face financial instability. This instability can <mark>make them more susceptible</mark> to gambling as a means of quick financial gain. In addition, soldiers may be drawn to sports betting during their off-duty hours or when deployed, as a way to relieve stress or pass the time. The combination of financial stress and the availability of easy betting options can make them particularly vulnerable. In addition, unlike a casino, <mark>mobile apps allow people to wager money</mark> directly <mark>from</mark> their bank accounts, and even more riskily, from personal <mark>credit cards</mark>. Finally, research has shown that soldiers can be prone to adverse peer effects, given the close-quarters lifestyle both on- and off-duty (Murphy, Reference Murphy2019), and so, a unit could be at risk from having just one influential prolific sports bettor within its ranks.
Although the legal gambling age is 18, addictive gambling disorder can be developed in younger, pre-enlisting persons. There has been a growing trend of teens gambling on websites using video games. For example, in “skin gambling,” teens exchanged virtual items on a game called Counter-Strike for on-site currency and placed bets (“‘It Ruined a Lot of Years of My Life.’ Teens and Young Adults Are Hooked on a Different Kind of Gambling.,” n.d.). Thus, over time, the Army is more likely to enlist young soldiers who may be already addicted to gambling, or at least, experienced gambling activities.
Gaming has become so prevalent that even affect work hours. Aguiar, Bils, Charles and Hurst (Reference Aguiar, Bils, Charles and Hurst.2017) documented that younger men, ages 21 to 30, exhibited a larger decline in work hours than older men or women. According to their estimates, innovations to gaming/recreational computing since 2004 explain on the order of half the increase in leisure for younger men. We cannot afford not to address this risk to soldiers’ personal economic security.
Mobile sports betting threat
Although there has not been a lot of research yet on the numbers of soldiers that gamble using mobile sports betting apps, with 81% of men considering themselves sports fans and 31% considering themselves avid fans (“New Siena/St. Bonaventure Survey Reveals: 70% of Americans Say They Are Sports Fans, Nearly Half Engage Daily or Several Times Every Week – Siena College Research Institute,” n.d.), the numbers are likely to be sizeable.Footnote 2 <mark>Young</mark> <mark>soldiers</mark> <mark>are</mark> being <mark>deluged</mark> <mark>with</mark> mobile sports betting <mark>ad</mark>vertisement<mark>s</mark>, with over 20% of broadcast time during live events including references to sports gambling and 90% of gambling logos or references appearing on playing surfaces or rink-side area (McCarthy Reference McCarthy2025). With exposure so great, problems are likely to follow.
The National Council on Problem Gambling conservatively estimates that 4% of military personnel meet the criteria for moderate to severe gambling problems, which is twice the national average (Means Reference Means2022). A different study found that 4.2% of veterans indicated at-risk or probable pathological gambling post-deployment (Whiting et al. Reference Whiting, Potenza, Park, McKee, Mazure and Hoff2016). To further compound this threat, experts on problem gambling point out that particular aspects of online gambling can be more addictive than traditional gambling due to its easy accessibility and convenience (Health Reference Health2024). This may be due to gamblers viewing sports betting as less risky, given the availability of player statistics and team performance data. In contrast, the stock market is often seen as more complex and harder to analyze for the average person, making sports betting appear more accessible and less intimidating.
Before the advent of mobile sports betting, soldiers would largely be limited to gambling unless they were off post and off duty. Now, a soldier can place a bet in the motor pool, on the rifle range, or even before morning physical training with just a few taps on the phone. And despite having a Department of Defense (DoD) Memorandum governing the use of non-government owned mobile devices, it is hard to regulate the use, unlike a physical location in which a commander can place as off-limits. To use an analogy with health, we have a de facto new drug, but without any controls to mitigate its effects, and also without a study of its potential side effects.
Mobile apps allow for real-time in-game betting and continuous engagement, which can lead to impulsive decisions. Recent research on frequent sports bettors aimed to measure the impact of two biases, overoptimism about financial returns and self-control problems, on the demand for sports betting, supporting the threat of impulsive decision making (Brown et al. 2025). The authors assert that bettors may have self-control problems in that more is bet in the moment than would have likely been bet if they had chosen in advance. This is compounded by the fact that most sports bets are placed on mobile devices rather than in brick-and-mortar sportsbooks.
In addition, with a large number of betting options, soldiers do not have to bet only on the outcome of an event but can make bets “inside” the game. Gamblers can bet on the next home run, the next touchdown, the next goal, or the next out. These possibilities and <mark>the speed and frequency</mark> with which they occur can <mark>compound the threat of gambling</mark> and the possibility that it becomes an addiction. Moreover, the high payoff odds on multi-leg parlays can attract the losing gambler who makes a last desperate bet to “get back to even.” Not surprisingly, individuals in high-stress environments may be more susceptible to making rash financial choices that jeopardize their financial stability (Wright Reference Wright2024).
Costs of unchecked behavior
Recent work finds that the increase in sports betting does not displace other gambling activity or entertainment-related consumption, but it does significantly reduce households’ savings allocations (Baker et al., Reference Baker, Balthrop, Johnson, Kotter and Pisciotta2024). Thus, not only are soldiers already at inherent risk of not saving and establishing personal economic security, but mobile sports betting may represent a looming threat to their financial well-being.
Findings suggest that for every dollar spent on sports betting, there is a reduction of two dollars in “investment behavior” (Finance and Herron Reference Finance and Herron2024). These additional dollars are routinely spent on supportive expenses, like purchasing streaming services and/or a night out at bars. The cumulative effect of sport betting can be devastating to a soldier’s financial health, leading, for example, to reducing contributions to the individual thrift savings plans (TSP).
The reason for such negative effects is that the TSP provides an employer match, which is a significant benefit to a soldier’s retirement plan. It is also the strategy the Army has advocated for soldiers, who also receive mandatory financial education about their pension. However, as Baker et al. note (2024), the endorphin rush from mobile sports betting in the short term could outweigh the benefits from the match or growth of personal TSP contributions, which will be experienced in the future.
One additional reason to be concerned about sports betting is the intersection of gambling addiction and mental health issues. Soldiers experiencing PTSD, anxiety, or depression may turn to mobile sports betting as a coping mechanism (Etuk et al. Reference Etuk, Shirk, Grubbs and Kraus2020). This can create a vicious cycle where financial troubles further exacerbate mental health issues, leading to a decline in overall well-being. Research has shown that many comorbidities are associated with gambling disorders among veterans, such as substance, alcohol, and nicotine use (abuse) (Etuk, 2020). In addition, military personnel have shown higher rates of gambling disorders due to factors such as being young, male, prone to risk taking, suffering from depression and PTSD, and experiencing deployment stress (Paterson et al. Reference Paterson, Whitty and Leslie2021).
The structure of mobile sports betting can foster addictive behaviors, especially among vulnerable populations. The instant gratification of winning and the thrill of betting can lead to a cycle of chasing losses, ultimately resulting in significant financial losses. Since military members and veterans have an elevated risk of gambling disorder (Van Der Maas, 2020), mobile sports betting can be even a greater threat since it can be accessed by soldiers any time of the day, anywhere they are.
For years, the Army has implemented numerous screening mechanisms and education programs to care for soldiers and family members afflicted by PTSD, substance abuse, addiction, and depression. However, only recently, through the National Defense Authorization Act of 2019 (NDAA) have provisions been required to screen for gambling addiction (NDAA 2019). It is unclear the degree to which programs have been implemented to detect gambling among soldiers. The intent of screening is to mitigate the detriments to readiness that behavioral issues pose, like problem gambling. There is no question that problem <mark>gambling</mark> can also <mark>affect readiness</mark>, and therefore efforts should be made to prevent, detect, and or mitigate. Fortunately, there are screening tools in the civilian sector that the Army could adopt, to include: The Gambling Disorder Identification Test, the Lie-Bet Tool, the Brief Biosocial Gambling Screen, and the Problem Gambling Severity Index (The Maryland Center of Excellence on Problem Gambling, n.d.). In addition, prevention and mitigation can be important, and we turn, next, to these important topics.

Readiness stabilizes great powers by imposing credible costs on aggression. A collapse triggers existential escalation and miscalculation.
Dowd 15 — Alan Dowd, Senior Fellow at the Sagamore Institute for Policy Research and Contributing Editor for the American Legion magazine, 2015 (“Shield & Sword: The Case for Military Deterrence,” Providence, December 31st, Available Online at https://providencemag.com/2015/12/shield-sword-the-case-for-military-deterrence/, Accessed 04-11-2024)
Surely, the same principle applies in the realm of nations. Our world teems with violent regimes and vicious [humans]. And something precious—our notion of peace, sovereignty, liberty, civilization itself—sits exposed to all that danger. In a world where might makes right, the <mark>only thing that keeps</mark> the <mark>peace</mark>, defends our sovereignty and liberty, and upholds civilization <mark>is</mark> the <mark>willingness to use our resources</mark> to keep the dangers at bay. Yet too many policymakers disregard the wisdom of military deterrence, and too many people of faith forget that the aim of deterrence is, by definition, to prevent wars, not start them.
Some people of faith oppose the threat of military force, let alone the use of military force, because of Christ’s message of peace. This is understandable in the abstract, but we must keep in mind two truths.
First, governments are held to a different standard than individuals, and hence are expected to do certain things individuals aren’t expected to do—and arguably shouldn’t do certain things individuals should do. For example, a government that turned the other cheek when attacked would be conquered by its foes, leaving countless innocents defenseless. A government that put away the sword—that neglected its defenses—would invite aggression, thus jeopardizing its people.
Second, all uses of force are not the same. The sheriff who uses force to apprehend a murderer is decidedly different from the criminal who uses force to commit a murder. The policemen posted outside a sporting event to deter violence are decidedly different from those who plot violence. Moral relativism is anything but a virtue.
Some lament the fact that <mark>we live in</mark> such <mark>a violent world</mark>, but that’s precisely the point. Because we live in a violent world, <mark>governments must </mark>take steps to <mark>deter </mark>those <mark>who can be deterred</mark>—<mark>and neutralize </mark>those <mark>who cannot</mark>. In this regard, it pays to recall that Jesus had sterner words for scholars and scribes than He did for soldiers. In fact, when a centurion asked Jesus for help, He didn’t admonish the military commander to put down his sword. Instead, He commended him for his faith.[i] “Even in the Gospels,” soldier-scholar Ralph Peters reminds us, “it is assumed that soldiers are, however regrettably, necessary.”[ii] They are necessary not only for waging war but, preferably, for maintaining peace.
It’s a paradoxical truth that military readiness can keep the peace. The Romans had a phrase for it: Si vis pacem, para bellum. “If you wish for peace, prepare for war.” President George Washington put it more genteelly: “There is nothing so likely to produce peace as to be well prepared to meet an enemy.” Or, in the same way, “We infinitely desire peace,” President Theodore Roosevelt declared. “And the surest way of obtaining it is to show that we are not afraid of war.” After the West gambled civilization’s very existence in the 1920s and 1930s on hopes that war could somehow be outlawed, the men who crafted the blueprint for waging the Cold War returned to peace through strength. Winston Churchill proposed “defense through deterrents.” President Harry Truman called NATO “an integrated international force whose object is to maintain peace through strength…we devoutly pray that our present course of action will succeed and maintain peace without war.”[iii] President Dwight Eisenhower explained, “Our <mark>arms must be</mark> mighty, <mark>ready for</mark> instant <mark>action</mark>, so that no potential aggressor may be tempted to risk its own destruction.” President John Kennedy vowed to “strengthen our military power to the point where no aggressor will dare attack.” And President Ronald Reagan steered the Cold War to a peaceful end by noting, “None of the four wars in my lifetime came about because we were too strong.” Reagan also argued, “Our military strength is a prerequisite for peace.”[iv]
Even so, arms alone aren’t enough to deter war. After all, the great powers were armed to the teeth in 1914. But since they weren’t clear about their intentions and treaty commitments, a small crisis on the fringes of Europe mushroomed into a global war. Neither is clarity alone enough to deter war. After all, President Woodrow Wilson’s admonitions to the Kaiser were clear, but America lacked the military strength at the onset of war to make those words matter and thus deter German aggression. In other words, America was unable to deter. “The purpose of a deterrence force is to create a set of conditions that would cause an adversary to conclude that the cost of any particular act against the United States of America or her allies is far higher than the potential benefit of that act,” explains Gen. Kevin Chilton, former commander of U.S. Strategic Command. It is a “cost-benefit calculus.”[v] So, given the anemic state of America’s military before 1917, the Kaiser calculated that the benefits of attacking U.S. ships and trying to lure Mexico into an alliance outweighed the costs. That proved to be a grave miscalculation.
<mark>In order</mark> for the adversary <mark>not to miscalculate</mark>, a few factors must hold.
First, <mark>consequences must be clear</mark>, which was not the case on the eve of World War I. Critics of deterrence often cite World War I to argue that arms races trigger wars. But if it were that simple, then a) there wouldn’t have been a World War II, since the Allies allowed their arsenals to atrophy after 1918, and b) there would have been a World War III, since Washington and Moscow engaged in an unprecedented arms race. The reality is that miscalculation lit the fuse of World War I. <mark>The antidote</mark>, as alluded to above, <mark>is strength plus clarity</mark>.
A second important factor to avoid miscalculation: The adversary must be rational, which means it can grasp and fear consequences. Fear is an essential ingredient of deterrence. It pays to recall that deterrence comes from the Latin dēterreō: “to frighten off.”[vi] Of course, as Churchill conceded, “The deterrent does not cover the case of lunatics.”[vii] Mass-murderers masquerading as holy men and death-wish dictators may be immune from deterrence. (The secondary benefit of the peace-through-strength model is that it equips those who embrace it with the capacity to defeat these sorts of enemies rapidly and return to the status quo ante.)
Third, the <mark>consequences</mark> of military confrontation <mark>must be credible and tangible</mark>, which was the case during most of the Cold War. Not only did Washington and Moscow construct vast military arsenals to deter one another; they were clear about their treaty commitments and about the consequences of any threat to those commitments. Recall how Eisenhower answered Soviet Premier Nikita Khrushchev’s boast about the Red Army’s overwhelming conventional advantage in Germany: “If you attack us in Germany,” the steely American commander-in-chief fired back, “there will be nothing conventional about our response.”[viii] Eisenhower’s words were unambiguously clear, and unlike Wilson, he wielded the military strength to give them credibility.
Discussing military deterrence in the context of Christianity may seem incongruent to some readers. But for a pair of reasons it is not.
First, deterrence is not just a matter of GDPs and geopolitics. In fact, scripture often uses the language of deterrence and preparedness. For example, in the first chapter of Numbers the Lord directs Moses and Aaron to count “all the men in Israel who are twenty years old or more and able to serve in the army.” This ancient selective-service system is a form of military readiness. Similarly, I Chronicles 27 provides detail about the Israelites’ massive standing army: twelve divisions of 24,000 men each. II Chronicles 17 explains the military preparations made by King Jehoshaphat of Judah, a king highly revered for his piety, who built forts, maintained armories in strategically located cities “with large supplies” and fielded an army of more than a million men “armed for battle.” Not surprisingly, “the fear of the Lord fell on all the kingdoms of the lands surrounding Judah, so that they did not go to war against Jehoshaphat.” In the New Testament, Paul writes in Romans 13 that “Rulers hold no terror for those who do right, but for those who do wrong…Rulers do not bear the sword for no reason.” Again, this is the language of deterrence. Those who follow the law within a country and who respect codes of conduct between countries have nothing to fear. Those who don’t have much to fear. Likewise, to explain the importance of calculating the costs of following Him, Jesus asks in Luke 14, “What king would go to war against another king without first sitting down to consider whether his 10,000 soldiers could go up against the 20,000 coming against him? And if he didn’t think he could win, he would send a representative to discuss terms of peace while his enemy was still a long way off.” In a sense, both kings are wise—one because he recognizes that he’s outnumbered; the other because he makes sure that he’s not. Put another way, both kings subscribe to peace through strength. Again, as with the Centurion earlier, Jesus could have rebuked the martial character of these kings, but he did not. This is not just description but commendation. We ignore their example at our peril.
Secondly, it is not incongruent if we understand military <mark>deterrence</mark> as a means to <mark>prevent great-power war</mark>—the kind that kills by the millions, the kind humanity has not endured for seven decades. We know we will not experience the biblical notion of peace—of shalom, peace with harmony and justice—until Christ returns to make all things new. In the interim, in a broken world, the alternatives to peace through strength leave much to be desired: peace through hope, peace through violence, or peace through submission. But these options are inadequate.
The sheer destructiveness and totality of great-power war testify that crossing our fingers and hoping for peace is not a Christian option. Wishful thinking**, romanticizing reality, is the surest way to invite what Churchill called “temptations to a trial of strength.”
Moreover, the likelihood that the next great-power war would involve multiple nuclear-weapons states means that it could end civilization. Therefore, a posture that leaves peer adversaries doubting the West’s capabilities and resolve—thus inviting miscalculation—is not only unsound, but immoral and inhumane—unchristian. “Deterrence of war is more humanitarian than anything,” Gen. Park Yong Ok, a longtime South Korean military official, argues. “If we fail to deter war, a tremendous number of civilians will be killed.”[ix]
Peace through violence has been tried throughout history. Pharaoh, Caesar and Genghis Khan, Lenin, Hitler, Stalin and Mao, all attained a kind of peace by employing brutal forms of violence. However, this is not the kind of “peace” under which God’s crowning creation can flourish; neither would the world long tolerate such a scorched-earth “peace.” This option, too, the Christian rejects.
Finally, the civilized world could bring about peace simply by not resisting the enemies of civilization—by not blunting the Islamic State’s blitzkrieg of Iraq; by not defending the 38th Parallel; by not standing up to Beijing’s land-grab in the South China Sea or Moscow’s bullying of the Baltics or al-Qaeda’s death creed; by not having armies or, for that matter, police. As Reagan said, “There’s only one guaranteed way you can have peace—and you can have it in the next second—surrender.”[x]
The <mark>world has tried</mark> these <mark>alternatives </mark>to peace through strength, <mark>and</mark> the <mark>outcomes have been disastrous</mark>.
After World War I, Western powers disarmed and convinced themselves they had waged the war to end all wars. By 1938, as Churchill concluded after Munich, the Allies had been “reduced…from a position of security so overwhelming and so unchallengeable that we never cared to think about it.”[xi] Like predators in the wilderness, the Axis powers sensed weakness and attacked.
In October 1945—not three months after the Missouri steamed into Tokyo Bay—Gen. George Marshall decried the “disintegration not only of the Armed Forces, but apparently…all conception of world responsibility,” warily asking, “Are we already, at this early date, inviting that same international disrespect that prevailed before this war?”[xii] Stalin answered Marshall’s question by gobbling up half of Europe, blockading Berlin, and arming Kim Il-Sung in patient preparation for the invasion of South Korea.[xiii] The U.S. military had taken up positions in Korea in 1945, but withdrew all combat forces in 1949.[xiv] Then, in 1950, Secretary of State Dean Acheson announced that Japan, Alaska and the Philippines fell within America’s “defensive perimeter.”[xv] Korea didn’t. Stalin noticed. Without a U.S. deterrent in place, Stalin gave Kim a green light to invade. Washington then reversed course and rushed American forces back into Korea, and the Korean peninsula plunged into one of the most ferocious wars in history. The cost of miscalculation in Washington and Moscow: 38,000 Americans, 103,250 South Korean troops, 316,000 North Korean troops, 422,000 Chinese troops and 2 million civilian casualties.[xvi] The North Korean tyranny— now under command of Kim’s grandson—still dreams of conquering South Korea. Introduction
The rise of social media has significantly changed the way children and young people (CYP) engage with content online. The term CYP refers to individuals aged 0–25 years, encompassing a range of developmental stages from early childhood to late adolescence and emerging adulthood [1]. This term includes both minors and young adults, who are often more vulnerable to external influences, particularly from social media and digital platforms. Recent data from the Pew Research Centre [2] reveals that in 2022–23, nearly half (46%) of U.S. teens reported being online ‘almost constantly,’ averaging approximately 5–6 h per day on weekdays, with usage rising on weekends. Additionally, social media use is nearly universal among teens aged 13–17, with 97% reporting active engagement on social media platforms [3]. In the UK, the prevalence of mobile phone ownership is similarly high, with 98% of 12- to 15-year-olds owning mobile phones and spending an average of 3–4 h per day on social media [4].
While social media offers opportunities for learning, it also presents risks. Academic evidence [5] has found that CYP’s use of social media is linked to risky behaviours that affect their health. The trend of earlier and more frequent use of social media by younger children has serious implications for their mental health. Increased screen time can disrupt sleep, increase anxiety, and reduce attention spans. Early exposure to unrealistic body images can contribute to low self-esteem, and prolonged use limits face-to-face interactions, affecting social development. Constant exposure to content can also overstimulate their developing brains, leading to impulsive behaviour and heightened vulnerability to cyberbullying. More research is needed to confirm causality and identify specific harmful aspects of social media [5].
Gambling content on social media represents a significant threat to CYP’s development. Studies show that up to 66% of UK CYP have been exposed to gambling advertisements on social media [6]. Contemporary innovations like play-toearn games and crypto-trading integrate gambling-like elements that mimic gambling behaviour and target younger individuals [7]. Early exposure to gambling is linked to higher risks of problem gambling in adulthood [8], highlighting the need for more research on this issue.
Social media advertising strategies have proven effective at promoting favourable attitudes towards gambling. Research shows that increased <mark>exposure to gambling ads leads to a higher intention to</mark> <mark>gamble</mark> [9], <mark>a strong predictor of future gambling</mark> behaviour [10]. However, studies on how social media content influences CYP’s gambling behaviour remain limited.
One of the most concerning trends is the rise of <mark>influencer-driven gambling</mark> promotions. Gambling operators use influencers to market their products, targeting CYP, who are especially vulnerable to such ads. The <mark>use</mark> of <mark>music, humour</mark>, and <mark>celebrity endorsements</mark> makes gambling more appealing to CYP [11]. <mark>Sports themes, financial</mark> <mark>incentives</mark>, <mark>and</mark> the <mark>glamourisation</mark> of gambling further <mark>heighten the appeal</mark>, while CYP often struggle to understand the complexities of gambling odds [12]. These factors make children and adolescents more susceptible to gambling promotions compared to adults [13]. Two key reasons for this vulnerability are CYP’s diminished capacity to critically evaluate digital content and their high trust in influencers [14–15]. In the gaming and lifestyle sectors, influencers—especially streamers—have large followings and regularly promote gambling content [16]. Alternatively, the expanded range of betting options may significantly increase the propensity to engage among existing gamblers. Moreover, the magnitude and direction of broader spillover effects are empirically un certain and theoretically ambiguous. For instance, alcohol consumption is influenced by numerous macroeconomic factors– seasonal patterns, income fluctuations, cultural trends, and local economic conditions– making it unclear whether sports betting legalization could generate a detectable signal amid this substantial baseline variation. The theoretical re lationship is further complicated by competing mechanisms: gambling and alcohol could act as complements (both being “vice” products consumed during social sports viewing) or substitutes (competing for limited discretionary spending), with the net effect depending on consumer preferences and income constraints. Similarly, state tax revenues fluctuate sub stantially due to broader economic cycles, federal policy changes, and sector-specific shocks that typically dwarf the potential contribution of a newly introduced gambling tax stream. As such, given the empirical uncertainties of these relationships, it is important to quantify their effects to inform policy evaluations more comprehensively. Understanding these behaviors is important for legalizing states to evaluate existing poli cies and to help inform policy design in states that are contemplating legalization. As of May 2025, 11 states (representing roughly 32% of the U.S. population) have not legalized either online or offline sports betting, with several actively considering legislation. For exam ple, Georgia lawmakers continue to debate sports betting legislation (Butler, 2025), while Minnesota has repeatedly considered legalization but remains stalled in court (Nightingale, 2025). Another eight states have only legalized offline sports betting, and many of these states are actively debating whether to add online wagering, including Mississippi, South Dakota, Nebraska, and Wisconsin (Goldberg, 2025; Evans, 2025; Unicameral Update, 2025; 5 Wisconsin Policy Forum, 2025). These states face important choices about which consumer protection measures to implement alongside legalization. Our analysis of how legalization affects different consumer segments can help inform these choices by identifying which groups are most vulnerable to potentially harmful gambling behaviors, when these vulner abilities are most likely to manifest, what the broader economic and public health con sequences are, and how these effects vary by implementation approach (i.e., online only, online and offline concurrently, or online after offline). Estimating the causal effects of sports betting legalization on these OSG use and misuse behaviors is important but also challenging. Randomized experimentation is neither feasible nor ethical. It is also difficult to disentangle the effects of sports betting legalization from other confounding factors, such as the pre-existing prevalence of and dynamics in DFS. Sports gambling fluctuates over time with strong seasonality tied to annual contests such as the Super Bowl, the World Series and March Madness. Data limitations also present a significant obstacle: prior to online sports betting, the pre dominantly cash-based nature of sports betting made tracking individual gambling patterns nearly impossible.2 Since over 95% of all sports wagers are now placed via online/mobile platforms that require digital payments, our focus on regulated digital channels, where in dividual spending patterns can be systematically measured through digital payment transac tions, captures the vast majority of regulated sports gambling activity as a whole. However, our analysis– like virtually all empirical research on gambling behavior– does not capture unmeasurable channels such as illegal and informal sports gambling. Despite the limitation of unmeasured channels, analyzing regulated markets provides substantial policy benefits. Regulated sports gambling activity enable states to generate tax revenue, imple ment consumer protections such as age verification and spending limits, and collect data necessary for evidence-based interventions. Unlike informal or illegal gambling, regulated markets allow for real-time monitoring of problematic behaviors, provide accessible path ways for individuals to seek help without stigma, and enable targeted interventions based on observable spending patterns. Because our RIG measures only capture regulated activ ity, they represent conservative lower bounds on total irresponsible gambling behavior– if 6 concerning levels are observed in regulated channels alone, the true extent of problematic gambling may be even greater. From this perspective, our analysis of measurable, regu lated online sports gambling provides actionable insights for policy design even if some of this regulated gambling activity may represent substitution from informal channels. All gambling behaviors and outcomes discussed hereafter, such as OSG and RIG, refer to this observable, regulated activity unless otherwise noted. Toconduct our main analysis, we leverage a large financial panel dataset from Consumer Edge, a leading data analytics firm. The data track individual-level card spending and ac count transfers within a balanced panel of 1,201,571 regular card users (“individuals”) in the 23 states we analyze from January 2019 to September 2023 (the “observation period”). The data source can attribute multiple credit and debit cards, across multiple card networks, to the same individual. Further details about this dataset are provided in Web Appendix A. We observe credit and debit card spending at all 20 major OSG operators over the observation period for these panel members. We also observe pre-tax income and modal state by individ ual and time, allowing us to identify which individuals resided in states that legalized online sports betting and which/how much individuals gambled before and after legalization com pared to individuals in states where online sports betting remained illegal throughout. Our analysis proceeds at the “full panel level” in that we analyze spending across all individuals within the panel residing in treatment and control states, regardless of whether they gam bled or not, providing comprehensive coverage of regulated online sports gambling activity within our sample. There are multiple indicators of reasonable external validity– for example, the distribu tion of annual household incomes observed within the panel is similar to the distribution of household income reported in the U.S. Census Bureau’s Current Population Survey (CPS), and analysis on the same credit card panel dataset from Kim and McCarthy, 2023 shows that correlations between panel-based quarterly revenue measures and corporate quarterly revenue disclosures generally exceed 90%. The panel members comprising our analysis represent approximately 3% of all consumer spending in the U.S. A strength of our panel for studying online sports gambling is that it naturally captures the relevant population: since 7 regulated online sports betting requires digital payment methods, unbanked individuals can not participate in these markets regardless of their gambling preferences. Additionally, our main findings are robust to alternative weighting schemes, including population-weighted averages that give greater weight to states with larger panel representation, suggesting that our conclusions are not artifacts of the particular composition of states in our sample (see Web Appendix D11). For more details regarding the external validity of the data, see Web Appendix A1. Regardless, we acknowledge that, as with other major marketing spending panels, these individuals may not be fully representative of the broader population of U.S. online gamblers, and it is not possible to fully verify representativeness without population level data. WeuseaGeneralized Synthetic Control (GSC) model to estimate causal effects within a principled quasi-experimental framework. The GSC model generates counterfactual predic tions for states that legalized sports betting by comparing them to a weighted combination of control states that did not legalize online sports betting during the sample period. This method is particularly well-suited for settings with a smaller number of treated units, such as ours, because it constructs a tailored counterfactual prediction for each individual treated unit in each treated period, enhancing the accuracy of the comparisons. Our analysis reveals that sports betting legalization caused substantial increases in both gambling activity and potentially harmful gambling behaviors. Specifically, online sports gambling spending per individual increased by 369% following legalization, rising from an estimated $.99 to $4.63 per month. However, the rate of irresponsible gambling– defined as monthly gambling expenditures exceeding 1% of income– increased by 372%, from 0.2% to 0.9% of individual-months.3 Both effects stabilized after an initial surge in the first monthpost-legalization. The spending increase did not show significant differences between terciles of the income distribution, with low, middle, and high-income groups contributing 35%, 34%, and 31% of the overall effect, respectively. However, the rate of irresponsible gambling did differ significantly across income terciles, with RIG1 increasing among low income individuals by 0.91%, by 0.79% among middle-income individuals, and by 0.49% among high-income individuals. This pattern suggests that while the revenue benefits of 8 legalization are attributed nearly evenly across income groups, its effects on the rate of irre sponsible gambling were disproportionately concentrated among lower-income individuals. Importantly, we find limited evidence that these increases come at the expense of offline casino activity. Existing OSG participants drive substantially more of the increase in RIG than new OSGadopters, despite representing a smaller share of the population. Legalization also generated significant spillover effects, including a 20% increase in mass-market alcohol consumption and a 75% increase in calls to gambling helplines. From a fiscal perspective, states benefited from an average increase of $0.78 in monthly tax revenue per capita. These f indings highlight the tradeoffs inherent in sports betting legalization, with fiscal benefits and broad-based spending increases accompanied by larger increases in RIG among vulnerable populations and increased help-seeking through public health channels. Related Literature Our research ties into several research streams. It connects most directly with two recent working papers that examine the effects of online gambling legalization on consumer finan cial outcomes (Baker et al., 2024; Hollenbeck, Larsen, and Proserpio, 2024). Using credit report data and bank account records, these studies document worsening trends in house hold finances after legalization, including declining credit scores, diminished savings, and reduced access to credit. While these papers establish important links between gambling legalization and deteriorating financial health, our work provides insight into the behavioral mechanisms driving these outcomes. By measuring RIG, we demonstrate how legaliza tion affects observable gambling behaviors that are associated with adverse financial out comes documented in prior research. As noted above, a large body of prior research has already established strong correlational links between gambling-to-income ratios and var ious harms, including financial distress, relationship problems, and even mortality. Given these well-documented relationships, our measures of irresponsible gambling can serve as 9 early warning indicators of financial distress, potentially enabling intervention before more severe consequences materialize. This behavioral lens complements the existing focus on f inancial outcomes and helps explain why certain subpopulations may be particularly vul nerable to gambling-related financial harms. Additionally, we examine broader spillovers including consumption effects, public health impacts, and substitution between gambling modalities to provide a more comprehensive view of legalization’s societal implications that can inform both targeted interventions and regulatory design. Our work contributes to the responsible gambling literature, which examines how gam bling availability affects consumer welfare and studies mechanisms to prevent excessive gambling behaviors. Blaszczynski, Ladouceur, and Shaffer (2004) established a frame work emphasizing informed consumer choice and corporate social responsibility, while Ladouceur et al. (2017) identified effective intervention strategies at both individual and op erator levels. Canadian Centre on Substance Use and Addiction (2021) established empirically grounded thresholds for responsible gambling as a percentage of income. We extend this literature by providing causal evidence of how gambling policy changes affect rates of irre sponsible gambling within regulated channels using quasi-experimental methods. Our work is also informed by prior literature on lotteries and land-based gambling. This research has established that traditional gambling services tend to be regressive, attracting disproportionate spending from lower-income consumers. Lockwood et al. (2024) found that households earning under $50,000 spend 29% more than those earning over $100,000 on lotteries. We show that this trend also applies to the regulated online sports betting mar ket, but to a lesser degree, with non-significant differences in spending increases between income terciles after online sports betting legalization. Finally, our work contributes to marketing research examining how policy interventions affect the consumption of potentially harmful products. Recent studies have documented various consumer responses to restrictive policies on products like cigarettes (Goli, Mum malaneni, and Chintagunta, 2023; Anand and Kadiyali, 2024) and sweetened beverages (Seiler, A. Tuchman, and Yao, 2020; Bollinger and Sexton, 2023). Our study extends this literature by examining consumption liberalization rather than restriction, providing novel 10 insights into how regulatory changes interact with individual characteristics to influence po tentially harmful consumption patterns. Model Development Generalized Synthetic Control Weaddress the empirical challenges in estimating causal effects using the Generalized Syn thetic Control (GSC) approach. This method offers three key advantages: it relaxes the parallel trends assumption required by traditional difference-in-differences methods; it in corporates interactive fixed effects that capture both state-specific unobservable factors (such as cultural attitudes toward gambling) and their interactions with time-varying influences (such as macroeconomic conditions or major sporting events); and it provides a framework for leveraging data from multiple treated states with varying implementation timing. This f lexibility is crucial given our setting, where states enacted similar policies at different times. Following Bai (2009), Xu (2017), and Li and Sonnier (2023), we model the outcome Yit for each state i, month t as: Yit = δitDit + λ′ iFt + µi + µt +eit Here, Dit is a binary treatment indicator equal to 1 for treated states in posttreatment periods and 0 otherwise. δit represents the treatment effect, Ft is a vector of time-varying latent factors common to all states, with λi being the state-specific factor loadings. µi and µt are state- and time-specific fixed effects. 15 To account for the potential confounding effects of the COVID-19 pandemic, which occurred during our observation period, we include the following time-varying state-level controls: monthly unemployment rates from the Bureau of Labor Statistics; Google Com munity Mobility data capturing percent changes from baseline across six categories (retail and recreation, grocery and pharmacy, parks, transit stations, workplaces, and residential mobility); monthly COVID-19 case counts per capita from The New York Times COVID 19 database; and monthly COVID-19 death counts per capita from the CDC’s Provisional COVID-19 Deaths dataset. These controls help isolate the effects of sports betting legaliza tion from concurrent pandemic-related disruptions to economic activity, consumer mobility patterns, and public health conditions that could independently affect gambling behaviors. The estimation proceeds in three steps: 1. Estimate common factors and fixed effects, and control states’ factor loadings, using control state data 2. Estimate treated states’ factor loadings using pretreatment data 3. Calculate treatment effects as posttreatment differences between treated states’ ob served outcomes and predicted counterfactuals We estimate overall effects by taking a simple average of state-specific effects, treating each state’s policy change as equally informative. Results are robust to using panel size weighted averages instead (see the Robustness Checks section and Web Appendix D11). Under both weighting procedures, following Li and Sonnier (2023), we construct 95% con f idence intervals from their factor-robust asymptotic-normal estimator, which accounts for both latent-factor estimation error and serially correlated idiosyncratic shocks. Substitution Between Online Sports Gambling and Casino Gambling While our main analysis shows substantial increases in regulated online sports gambling following legalization, an important policy question is whether this growth comes at the expense of existing casino gambling activity. This concern is practically significant for state policymakers, as some states– most notably Nevada, but also Mississippi, Louisiana, and Maryland (Salter, 2025)– have been hesitant to expand online gambling due to fears it could harm their established casino gambling industries (Rutledge and Light, 2024). Understanding this relationship is essential for policymakers weighing legalization de cisions. If sports betting significantly cannibalizes casino activity, states with established casino industries might face tradeoffs between new sports betting tax revenue and existing casino tax revenue, along with potential job losses in the casino sector. Conversely, if the ac tivities are complementary or independent, states could capture additional gambling revenue without harming existing industries. Since 95% of regulated sports betting activity occurs online rather than at physical es tablishments (Fisher, 2025), we focus our substitution analysis on whether online sports gambling affects traditional casino gambling rather than offline sports betting. We can ob serve all credit and debit card spending at major casino operators– encompassing hotel stays, dining, entertainment, and related activities– which serves as a plausible proxy for the evolution of overall casino engagement and activity levels. We estimate treatment effects separately for online sports gambling and casino-related spending across three distinct legalization patterns: states that legalized online sports bet ting only (Tennessee), states that legalized online and offline sports betting nearly concur rently (Illinois, Colorado, Virginia, Wyoming, Arizona, Louisiana, Kansas, and Ohio), and states that legalized online sports betting after previously legalizing offline sports betting 20 (Arkansas and New York). Additionally, we distinguish between in-state casino spending (e.g., spending from individuals residing in Illinois at casinos within Illinois) and out-of state casino spending (e.g., spending from individuals residing in Illinois at casinos outside of Illinois) to capture potential geographic redistribution effects, and because state policy makers are primarily concerned about the impact of legalization on casino gambling that happens in-state, as in-state casino spending is what drives state tax revenue and employ ment. These categorizations allow us to test specific predictions about casino substitution pat terns. For instance, if online sports betting primarily attracts casual gamblers who might otherwise visit casinos, we would expect to see declines in casino-related spending, par ticularly in states where both activities become available simultaneously. Alternatively, if sports bettors and casino gamblers represent largely distinct demographics with different preferences, we might observe little relationship between the two activities. **ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit pre-written weighing mechanisms—no probability, magnitude, timeframe, or scope comparisons embedded in taglines or card text. Cards like Walker & Naratil 25 establish a large soldier population at risk (~100,000 active-duty) and Dowd 15 asserts readiness prevents existential conflict, but no card compares this impact against likely opponent impacts (e.g., state autonomy, black markets, consumer choice) or instructs the back half on *how* to weigh. A filling card would be a 2024-25 defense-analysis piece tagging "Readiness collapse outweighs state-regulatory federalism concerns — probability 1.0 vs. speculative black-market risk; magnitude: great-power war vs. administrative friction" placed after Dowd 15.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No card preempts specific opponent positions (state-based regulation good, FTC overreach, black-market creation, consumer freedom, 10th Amendment). The case builds a pure military-readiness advantage without a single card that says "State patchwork fails to protect soldiers because X" or "FTC framework preempts race-to-bottom advertising — solves better than state AG enforcement." A spike card would be a 2024 GAO/IG report or law-review article tagging "State-by-state regulation creates advertising arbitrage — operators target soldiers at bases in permissive states; only federal FTC rule closes loophole" placed after the Walker & Naratil 25 internal link evidence.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** Zero embedded frontlines. The case does not preempt: (a) "DoD already bans gambling on base / can restrict phones" — no card on enforcement gaps in motor pools, ranges, off-post housing; (b) "Soldiers are adults / personal responsibility" — no card on behavioral-economics showing advertising exploits cognitive biases in high-stress populations; (c) "FTC lacks expertise/jurisdiction" — no card on FTC's existing UDAP authority over deceptive gambling ads or 2023-24 rulemakings. A defensive-spike card would be a 2024 DoD IG report tagging "Current DoD mobile-device policy unenforceable against off-app betting — commanders cannot monitor private financial transactions" placed in the "Mobile sports betting threat" section.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case is pinned to a single link chain: *deregulated ads → soldier betting → financial drain → readiness collapse → great-power deterrence failure*. No alternative impact scenarios (e.g., suicide/mental-health crisis, unit cohesion degradation, recruitment/retention collapse, veterans' long-term VA costs, intelligence compromise from debt). No second link (e.g., FTC framework → mandatory affordability checks → direct reduction in soldier losses). A flexibility card would be a 2024 RAND/VA study tagging "Problem gambling correlates 3.2× higher suicide ideation in veterans — federal framework enables early intervention via credit/bank data sharing" placed after "Costs of unchecked behavior" to open a second collapse route.

### Warrant depth
Walker & Naratil 25 — *Tagline*: "Deregulated sports betting advertisement drives soldier financial drain. That collapses military readiness." *Warrant in card*: **Yes** — the card text provides multi-step warrants: (1) soldiers demographically match every gambling-risk factor (age, male, high-stress, isolation, boredom, financial pressure); (2) advertising saturation data (3×/minute, 20%+ broadcast time, 90% logo placement); (3) behavioral mechanism (mobile access + credit-card linkage + in-game micro-betting + parlay loss-chasing); (4) empirical savings displacement (Baker et al. 2024: $1 bet → $2 less investment; TSP match foregone); (5) comorbidity chain (PTSD/depression → gambling coping → financial ruin → readiness degradation). Each sub-claim is sourced to a named study.

Funderburke 2/3/2026 — *Tagline*: "Adversaries are probing for readiness failures during an ongoing posture transition." *Warrant in card*: **Yes** — cites four concrete Jan-2026 events (Operation Absolute Resolve, Greenland framework, Iran reconstitution, Ukraine talks) as empirical proof of the "strategic restraint invites gray-zone probing" mechanism.

Dowd 15 — *Tagline*: "Readiness stabilizes great powers by imposing credible costs on aggression. A collapse triggers existential escalation and miscalculation." *Warrant in card*: **Partial** — card is a philosophical/historical survey (Roman, Cold War, WWI, Eisenhower, Reagan) with *no* post-2015 evidence linking *current* readiness metrics to *current* adversary calculus. Warrant relies on historical analogy, not 2024-26 deterrence empirics.

### Internal consistency
**Consistent** — Contention 1 (Walker & Naratil 25) establishes *soldier-level* financial/readiness harm from *advertising-driven* mobile betting. Contention 2 (Funderburke 2/3/2026) establishes *strategic-level* adversary probing *exactly when* readiness transitions create gaps. Contention 3 (Dowd 15) provides the *theoretical backbone*: readiness = deterrence = prevention of existential conflict. No contention undercuts another; framing uniformly treats readiness as the *necessary and sufficient* condition for deterrence, with soldier financial health as the *proximal* readiness input. The only tension is temporal: Funderburke describes a *2026-specific* transition window, while Dowd 15 is a timeless deterrence theory — but they align directionally.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 5/10** — One elite-evidence contention (Walker & Naratil 25) with deep warrants and a uniquely current strategic card (Funderburke 2/3/2026) carry the case, but zero embedded weighing, zero offensive/defensive spikes, a single-link collapse structure, and a dated tertiary deterrence card leave it strategically rigid and vulnerable to prepared opponents.
