Conclusion Online businesses face multiple challenges in establishing consumer trust. In addition to satisfying perceptions of confidence in the operator's reliability and integrity, they must also satisfy consumer skepticism over the conduct of other users (Chen et al., 2009). This absence of trust by parties can lead to market failures when asymmetries in information between different sides of the market result in a failure to transact (Akerlof, 1970). In few markets is the potential lack of trust more salient than in online poker, where consumers may have distorted views about the product (Fortune & Goodie, 2012) and there is also a public history of defrauded consumers (McMullan, 2012). Trust in online poker is a multifaceted construct, shaped not only by assurances like secure deposits or verified randomness, but also by more abstract dimensions of perceived legitimacy. Trust may emerge from a consistent alignment between player expectations and experienced reality (Schoorman and Mayer, 2007). This alignment transcends any single protective measure and necessarily relies on a set of interdependent safeguards, some visible and explicit, and others implicit and cultural. For example, on a site with convenient payment processing but low perceived trust, users may elect to reduce their own risk by only depositing funds to their accounts when needed to play, and otherwise not keep any deposits with operators.<mark>In a regulated market, transparent licensing </mark>arrangements, clear <mark>dispute </mark>resolution<mark> protocols, and demonstrable oversight </mark>from third-party auditors<mark> lend weight to the idea that the game operates within a structured framework</mark>. Indirect signals also contribute to user interpretations of a site's commitment to fair play, including factors like responsiveness of customer support, timeliness of problem resolution, and the perceived earnestness of communication.
