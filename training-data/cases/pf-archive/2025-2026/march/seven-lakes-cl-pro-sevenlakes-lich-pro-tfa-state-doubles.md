Overall, Americans are more likely to struggle to make housing payments than they were last spring. <mark>In a</mark> comparable <mark>Redfin survey conducted</mark> in May 2025, 44% of U.S. <mark>residents</mark> said they struggle to afford their mortgage or rent payment, compared with <mark>nearly half today</mark>.
It’s <mark>difficult</mark> for many Americans <mark>to afford housing</mark> because housing costs are high. The median U.S. home-sale price and average mortgage rates were historically high in November, when Redfin’s most recent survey was fielded, though they were not at record highs. Homebuyers need to earn $111,000 per year to afford the typical U.S. home, about $25,000 more than the median household income.
It’s particularly difficult for Gen Zers to afford housing because they haven’t yet reached their peak earning years and haven’t had much time to save for a down payment or monthly rent or mortgage payments.
"Buyers have been laying low for the last year or so because housing costs are high and the economy is uncertain. Young people are particularly concerned about job security and tariffs, and how those things will impact their pocketbooks and ability to make their housing payments," said Desiree Bourgeois, a Redfin Premier agent in Detroit. "I recently met a young couple hunting for a house in the $300,000 range. They’re worried about whether the country is going to enter a recession and whether they may lose their jobs or see their home value go down. There are very few first-time buyers out there; the cost of housing is so high that it’s challenging to enter the market."
Homeowners and Renters Are Borrowing Money, Skipping Meals to Afford Housing Payments
More than one in three <mark>(39%)</mark> Americans who struggle to afford housing are <mark>eating out at restaurants</mark> less often to make their monthly payments, making this the most common sacrifice. It’s followed by taking no or fewer vacations (34%).
Roughly one in six <mark>(17%)</mark> people <mark>work additional hours</mark> at their job to afford housing, and nearly one in six (16%) report selling belongings.
Some Americans are also making more consequential sacrifices: <mark>15% skip meals entirely</mark> to afford housing, <mark>14% have delayed medical treatments</mark>, 4% have delayed having children, and 4% have given up pets.
More than one-third (35%) of Gen Zers who struggle to afford housing report nixing restaurants, and 18% have skipped meals entirely. Among the other sacrifices Gen Zers are making: 20% have sold their belongings, 18% have worked a side hustle, and 15% have moved in with their parents.
Affordability struggles make it difficult for many young Americans to make their existing rental or mortgage payments, and they also make it difficult for young renters to break into the housing market. Just over one-quarter (27.1%) of Gen Zers own their home, compared to more than half of millennials and more than 70% of Gen Xers and baby boomers.
There are a few bright spots. The Gen Z and millennial homeownership rates are slowly ticking up as time goes on, with slightly more young Americans becoming homeowners in 2025 than the year before as affordability improved marginally. Redfin expects affordability to improve more in the coming year as mortgage rates stay closer to 6% than 7%, home-price growth loses steam, and wages increase faster than housing costs.

<mark>Unaffordable Housing</mark>: A Root Cause of Social Inequality
“Social inequality” is the new philanthropic buzz phrase that covers a wide range of hard-to-resolve societal problems. For example: racial bias; right-to-vote issues; inferior schools; low-paying job opportunities; homelessness; and limited access to quality nutrition and health care.
In our view, rundown apartment units located in desperately poor neighborhoods are a <mark>root cause of</mark> many <mark>quality-of-life</mark> issues. And these issues will only become more widespread, intractable, and irreversible as the crisis of <mark>unaffordable housing continues</mark> to spread from poor, underclass households to those earning average median incomes and above.
According to a study by the NYU Furman Center and Capital One, from 2006 to 2013 the percentage of low- and moderate-income renters facing <mark>severe rent burdens</mark> increased substantially in many large metro areas across the country. The number of U.S. households that spend at least half <mark>the</mark>ir income on rent could increase 25% to 14.8 million over the next decade, according to a report by Harvard University’s Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS) and Enterprise Community Partners.
According to the report, significant numbers of households in higher income brackets—80% to 120% of area median income—also struggle to find affordable rental units in all 50 states. This lack of “workforce housing” results in their inability to live in or near the places where they work. In high-cost metro areas, two-thirds of renters earning between $30,000 to $45,000 and half of those earning $45,000 to $75,000 have disproportionately high housing costs. This is at a time when average median incomes in America’s largest metro areas range from around $50,000 to just over $63,000, according to the U.S. Census Bureau.
The need for affordable housing subsidies is already overwhelming the resources of federal, state, and local governments, leaving them unable to provide adequate assistance. And regrettably, the philanthropic community has basically abandoned funding services that improve the lives of the residents—after-school programs, summer camps, health and wellness, and financial literacy, to name a few. Government must increase its commitment to affordable housing. It is clear, <mark>if we are to tackle social</mark> <mark>inequality</mark> in this country, <mark>housing</mark> and related services <mark>must</mark> <mark>be</mark>come a <mark>top</mark> priority of these funders.
Where We Have Been …
Fifty years ago, President Lyndon B. Johnson launched his Great Society, a set of domestic programs to eliminate poverty and racial injustice in America. According to some estimates, we’ve spent upward of $22 trillion of taxpayer dollars in hopes of creating such a society. Yet fundamentally little in the way of poverty and racial injustice has changed. Today, 46.5 million Americans live in poverty, 16.1 million of whom are children. Millions live on the edge, one job loss or one illness away from an impoverished existence. Why has this monumental—and costly—social endeavor failed?
While there are a number of reasons, a key component of social inequality is our housing policies, which for decades have simply created islands of poor and low-income families who, for all intents and purposes, have been ignored and cut off from mainstream society.
Our housing policies have forced entire generations of Americans into neighborhoods—whether labeled public housing, low-income housing, or undercapitalized affordable housing—offering little supportive educational or social services. The result has been unemployment, ever increasing crime rates, drugs, gangs, domestic violence, child abuse, high rates of incarceration, and premature deaths.
Consequences of Ignoring the Crisis
Where and how people live either provides or deprives them of opportunities to engage in healthy and prosperous lives. However, the fact is that it’s not only those who live in unhealthy homes and environments who are affected. All of society suffers when people are denied decent, safe, affordable housing.
When people are forced to spend as much as 50% of their income on housing, the following are often the result:
They live in substandard housing. For many, however, the infrastructure of their neighborhoods is important to them—churches, relatives, and friends nearby, public transportation, social services, etc. Moving new affordable housing to the suburbs is not the panacea some believe. Much needs to be done to preserve deteriorating housing and improve neighborhoods in the urban cores.
Those who seek <mark>nicer housing</mark> at <mark>affordable rent</mark> <mark>farther from urban areas</mark> find themselves pushed greater distances from their work. This not only <mark>places a strain on</mark> them and their <mark>families with</mark> respect to <mark>commuting</mark> times and costs, but also on their employers who often experience higher absenteeism and worker turnover rates, greatly adding to the cost of doing business.
· They are often forced to rely on fast food establishments for their caloric intake. A family can buy a lot more calories for $10 at McDonald’s than at the fresh fruit and vegetable section in a grocery store (if they have access to a grocery store) plus there’s no preparation required after a long workday and commute. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the estimated annual medical cost of obesity in the United States was a staggering $147 billion in 2008 U.S. dollars.
· They <mark>cannot afford to</mark>, or have the opportunity to, <mark>educate themselves</mark> or their children in order to achieve higher-paying jobs and work opportunities that would help them break their cycle of financial struggle and poverty. Substandard urban schools are the norm, and job training is inadequate at best. This <mark>places a greater burden on</mark> our already <mark>costly social</mark> and entitlement <mark>programs.</mark>
· The above lead to higher degrees of stress, frustration, and hopelessness, creating a downward spiral of human suffering and an increase in public costs. Clean, safe affordable housing can stabilize neighborhoods and be a springboard for greater opportunity.
Income and Housing
Anyone who works a 40-hour week in the United States, the world’s richest nation, should be able to afford life’s basic necessities, especially decent, safe housing. Yet, according to the National Low Income Housing Coalition, a family with one full-time worker earning the minimum wage cannot afford the fair-market rent for a two-bedroom apartment anywhere in the United States.
Local community development corporations and other mission-driven nonprofit housing groups must compete with well-capitalized, market-rate private developers in an already tight housing market. Research tells us that without support for affordable housing, affordable housing developers are unable to supply new rental units at rents that are low enough for low-income households. With rental restrictions expiring for roughly 30% of the 1.34 million units of affordable housing created by a Department of Housing and Urban Development program known as Sec. 8 project-based rental assistance (400,000 affordable units), housing advocates fear that these properties will be converted to market-rate properties by developers seeking to cash in on gentrifying neighborhoods, typically displacing the existing tenants.
The low-income housing tax credit program (LIHTC) has been an extremely effective tool to create and preserve affordable housing; it is a prototypical public/private program. According to the National Council of State Housing Agencies, the LIHTC program has provided the financing for the development or preservation of nearly 2.8 million units. But the number of tax credits allocated to the states annually doesn’t come close to meeting the needs of communities nationwide. Typically, there are applications submitted for two to three times the number of housing credits the state agencies have to allocate. The need for quality affordable housing has never been more critical. In 2012, there were 11.5 million extremely low-income renters and only 3.3 million affordable apartments available to them—a gap of 8.2 million units. Without the LIHTC, it is uneconomic to develop apartments for low-income families. According to Harvard’s JCHS, in order to develop new apartments affordable to people working full time earning the minimum wage, without subsidy construction costs would have to be reduced by 72% of the current construction cost average.
Since the 1980s, wages have failed to keep up with the cost of housing, whether one rented or owned. During the late 1990s and early 2000s, housing prices were rising 10% to 20% or more per year, depending on where in the country one lived. Yet, according to the Economic Policy Institute, wages have grown only 6% since 1979, after accounting for inflation. In more recent years, real income for the median household has been level or declining each year since the recession that began in 2008, according to the U.S. Census Bureau. In 2014, that number remained 6.5% lower than it was in 2007. More than 55% of American adults—approximately 138 million people—are struggling financially, reports the Stewards of Affordable Housing for the Future. Unless wages begin to rise or affordable rents become more available, there will be no housing relief in the foreseeable future for millions of families.

The housing affordability crisis has evolved into a structural economic challenge affecting nations across different income levels, reshaping labor markets, financial systems, and patterns of urban development. As home prices and rental costs rise faster than wages, an increasing share of households is forced to allocate a disproportionate percentage of their income toward housing, reducing disposable consumption and weakening long-term financial resilience. Literature Review 6.1 Definition and Economic Scope of the Concept Housing affordability refers to the ability of households to secure adequate housing without compromising their financial stability or reducing consumption of other essential goods and services. Economically, it is typically assessed through ratios comparing housing costs to income, but the concept extends far beyond simple numerical thresholds. The economic scope encompasses structural supply–demand dynamics, labor-market conditions, financing mechanisms, demographic trends, and spatial development patterns that collectively shape housing accessibility. Affordable housing is not merely a social concern; it is a core economic variable that influences long-term productivity, household wealth accumulation, financial stability, and national competitiveness. As cities grow into major economic hubs, affordability pressures intensify due to constrained land availability, regulatory barriers, and speculative investment behavior. These pressures extend into rental markets as well, where wage stagnation and rising demand push rents beyond sustainable levels for many households. Economists therefore frame housing affordability as an outcome shaped by interconnected macroeconomic forces—monetary policy, construction-sector capacity, urbanization, and global capital flows— each contributing to the equilibrium between housing costs and household purchasing power. Understanding this concept requires recognizing housing as both a consumption necessity and a financial asset, a duality that makes affordability highly sensitive to economic cycles, regulatory frameworks, and market expectations. 10 View publication stats 6.2 Historical Development and Major Economic Theories The historical development of housing affordability reflects shifting economic conditions, policy frameworks, and urbanization patterns across the past century. In the earlyto-mid 20th century, industrialization and mass urban migration created widespread housing shortages, prompting governments in many countries to adopt large-scale public housing initiatives to stabilize rents and improve living standards. Post–World War II reconstruction efforts further expanded social housing programs, especially in Europe and parts of Asia, where governments recognized housing as essential to economic recovery and labor-force mobilization. However, beginning in the 1970s and 1980s, neoliberal economic ideologies encouraged privatization, deregulation, and reduced state involvement in housing, increasingly shifting responsibility to markets. This transition coincided with financial-sector liberalization, which expanded mortgage credit and fueled rapid home price inflation. Major economic theories emerged to explain these developments. Urban economics highlighted agglomeration effects, showing how high-productivity cities attract disproportionate demand for limited land, driving up prices. Behavioral economics revealed how investor psychology and expectations fuel speculative cycles in real estate. Macroeconomic theories emphasized the role of monetary policy and credit expansion in shaping affordability. Institutional economics stressed the influence of zoning rules, bureaucratic procedures, and governance quality. Historically, the interplay of these forces has transformed housing from a basic welfare provision into a global financial commodity, generating new challenges for affordability and stability. 11 View publication stats 6.3 Fundamental Principles and Analytical Economic Models Fundamental economic principles and analytical models provide essential frameworks for understanding the mechanisms that drive housing affordability and the consequences of persistent price escalation. Urban spatial models, such as the Alonso–Muth–Mills framework, illustrate how land values and housing density evolve based on transportation costs, income levels, and accessibility to employment hubs, offering insight into why affordability crises intensify in major metropolitan areas. Behavioral models reveal how investor sentiment, herd behavior, and risk perceptions contribute to price bubbles. 6.6 Challenges, Misconceptions, and Limitations in Economic Analysis Analyzing the housing affordability crisis presents significant challenges, misconceptions, and methodological limitations that complicate efforts to design effective policy responses. One major challenge is the wide variation in local housing markets: affordability conditions differ dramatically between dense urban centers, suburban zones, and rural areas, making national-level policies difficult to calibrate. Misconceptions frequently arise from oversimplified narratives—such as the idea that affordability problems stem solely from lack of supply or solely from investor speculation—when in reality the crisis reflects a multilayered convergence of structural and financial dynamics. Analytical limitations stem from incomplete or inconsistent data on housing supply, rents, construction costs, land availability, vacancy rates, and informal housing markets. Econometric models also struggle to fully capture behavioral factors, such as expectations, risk perceptions, and intergenerational financial transfers, which play major roles in shaping housing demand. Political constraints further complicate analysis: policymakers often face resistance from homeowners, developers, and financial institutions that benefit from rising property values, making affordability interventions difficult to implement. These challenges underscore the importance of interdisciplinary, data-rich approaches that integrate insights from economics, urban planning, sociology, and political science to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the housing affordability crisis. 15 View publication stats 7. Theoretical Background 7.1 Core Theoretical Principles in Economics Core theoretical principles from economics provide the foundational lens through which the housing affordability crisis can be understood, highlighting how prices, incentives, and structural constraints interact within complex market systems. Institutional economics adds another dimension by explaining how governance quality, property rights, and regulatory efficiency shape market outcomes. ## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
ABSENT: The case contains extensive impact evidence (RedFin 2026 on housing affordability struggles; Burns 2015 on housing as root cause of inequality) but no pre-written weighing mechanisms — no probability, magnitude, or timeframe comparisons embedded in the cards themselves. There are no "even if" framing cards, no impact calculus tags (e.g., "Corporate acquisition accelerates affordability crisis *faster* than supply-side solutions can address"), and no cards that explicitly weigh the Pro's impacts against likely Con impacts (e.g., reduced investment, decreased supply). A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Corporate SFR ownership locks in rent hikes *before* supply responses materialize — timeframe advantage" (e.g., Garriga et al. 2022 on inelastic short-run supply) and belong in Contention 1 or a dedicated weighing module.

### Offensive spikes
ABSENT: The case establishes corporate market power (Sherwin 2025; Fields & Vergerio 2022; Seymour & Shelton 2023) but includes no cards that preemptively undercut likely Con positions. Missing are spikes against: (1) "Ban reduces investor capital → less new construction" — no card showing corporate buyers *crowd out* owner-occupants rather than add supply; (2) "Small landlords fill the gap" — no card on barriers to small-scale landlord entry (financing, regulation); (3) "Ban violates property rights / takings" — no card on precedent for housing-specific acquisition restrictions (e.g., foreign buyer bans, vacancy taxes). An offensive spike card would tag: "Corporate buyers *displace* first-time buyers — net zero supply effect" (Fields & Vergerio 2022 p. 23 "permanent capital competing with a young couple") and sit in the Market Consolidation contention.

### Defensive spikes
ABSENT: The case has no embedded frontlines anticipating specific Con responses. No cards preempt: "Eviction data is cherry-picked / not causal" (no card on systemic eviction filing strategies as revenue model); "Local concentration is a feature, not a bug — economies of scale lower rents" (no card rebutting efficiency claims with quality-adjusted rent data); "Ban pushes capital to multifamily, worsening SFR shortage" (no card on cross-elasticity or corporate substitution patterns). A defensive spike would tag: "Serial eviction filings are a *rent collection strategy*, not last resort — Fields & Vergerio 2022 p. 42" and appear in the Market Power section to inoculate against "evictions reflect tenant nonpayment" rebuttals.

### Evidence quality
The case uses a mix of high-quality and weaker sources. **Strong**: Fields & Vergerio 2022 (UCLA/USC academic working paper, 100+ pg, original dataset of corporate SFR portfolios, merger histories, eviction filings) — wins evidence comparisons on market structure. Seymour & Shelton 2023 (Federal Reserve Board Finance and Economics Discussion Series, peer-reviewed, census-tract-level data on 300k+ properties) — gold standard for local concentration. Garriga, Gete, Tsouderou 2022 (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis working paper, transaction-level data) — strong on price/rent effects. **Weak**: Sherwin 2025 (undergrad honors thesis, UNH, B.S. Accounting/Finance — no peer review, limited scope); RedFin 2026 (industry survey, n=unclear, self-reported "struggle" metric, commercial incentive to highlight crisis); Burns 2015 (advocacy org CEO op-ed, 10-year-old framing, pre-dates corporate SFR boom). The case leans heavily on the strong academic trio but the framing/impact cards (Sherwin, RedFin, Burns) would lose evidence-comparison tiebreaks against Con's likely Fed/OMB/peer-reviewed cites.

### Collapse flexibility
The case offers **two distinct impact pathways** with independent link chains: (1) **Affordability/Rent Burden** — Corporate concentration → local market power → rent hikes/fees/deferred maintenance → severe cost burden (Duvisac 2020; Garriga 2022; RedFin 2026) → inequality cascade (Burns 2015). (2) **Homeownership Displacement/Wealth Extraction** — Proprietary tech/cash advantages → corporate buyers outcompete families (Fields & Vergerio 2022 p. 23) → suppressed homeownership rates (Duvisac 2020 p. 7) → intergenerational wealth gap. These can be collapsed separately: if Con wins "rents don't rise," Pro still wins on homeownership displacement; if Con wins "supply increases," Pro still wins on wealth extraction from existing stock. However, both paths rely on the *same* market consolidation evidence (Fields & Vergerio; Seymour & Shelton) — a Con link turn on "consolidation is efficient" threatens both simultaneously.

### Warrant depth
**Mixed**. Strong warrants in the academic core: Fields & Vergerio 2022 provides *mechanism warrants* for tech advantage ("in-house data platforms fueled by algorithms that rapidly evaluate and submit offers"), vertical integration ("control the supply chain at every step... design it to deliver returns to investors"), and political capture (Blackstone $7M against rent control). Seymour & Shelton 2023 warrants local concentration with census-tract data (24 tracts >50% corporate SFR, up to 80%). Garriga 2022 warrants price/rent effects with buy-and-hold strategy identification. **Weak warrants**: Sherwin 2025 tag "Corporations enjoy outsized power... due to high concentration, predatory practices, proprietary systems, ineffective regulations" is a claim stack with no specific warrant in the cited text — the thesis excerpt shows geographic hotspots but doesn't substantiate "predatory practices" or "ineffective regulations." RedFin 2026 provides descriptive survey stats but no causal warrant linking corporate acquisition to the affordability struggles. Burns 2015 asserts housing as "root cause of inequality" with correlational data (NYU Furman, Harvard JCHS) but no causal identification.

### Internal consistency
**Largely consistent but with a framing tension**. The case uniformly frames corporate SFR as extractive concentration (Sherwin, Fields & Vergerio, Seymour & Shelton, Duvisac) — no contention undercuts another. The market consolidation evidence (Contention 4) logically feeds both affordability (Contention 1/2) and homeownership displacement impacts. **Tension**: The case cites Duvisac 2020 for both "private equity aims to squeeze rents" (p. 7) *and* "overall homeownership rates fell to historic low of 63%" (p. 7) — but Duvisac attributes the homeownership drop to post-2008 credit tightening and investor conversion of owner-occupied to rental, not solely corporate acquisition. The case blurs this by presenting both as corporate-driven. Also, Burns 2015 (pre-corporate-SFR) frames inequality as driven by *public housing policy failures*, while the case argues *corporate acquisition* is the root cause — inconsistent causal stories if pressed.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 6/10** — The case builds a cohesive, evidence-dense narrative around corporate market power with elite academic sources (Fields & Vergerio, Seymour & Shelton, Garriga) supporting two collapse-ready impact paths, but lacks any embedded strategic architecture (weighing, offensive/defensive spikes) and relies on three weak cites (undergrad thesis, industry survey, decade-old op-ed) for its framing and terminal impacts, leaving it vulnerable to evidence-comparison losses and Con frontlines it hasn't preempted.
