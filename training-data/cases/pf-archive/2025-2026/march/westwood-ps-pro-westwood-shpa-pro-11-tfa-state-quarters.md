DETERRENCE IN THE AI AGE For deterrence to work, an adversary must believe that a defender is both capable of imposing serious costs and resolved to do so if challenged. Some elements of military power are visible, but others—such as certain weapons capabilities, readiness levels, and mobilization capacities—are harder to gauge from the outside. Resolve is even more opaque: only the leaders of a country typically know precisely how willing they are to wage war. Deterrence, therefore, hinges on how effectively a country can credibly signal both its capabilities and its willingness to act. Costly military actions, such as repositioning forces or raising readiness levels, demonstrate credibility because they require time, resources, and political risk. After a Pakistani militant group launched an attack on the Indian Parliament in 2001, for example, India amassed troops along its border with Pakistan, and by credibly signaling both its ability and determination to act, it deterred further strikes on its soil. The domestic political pressures inherent in democracies can also bolster credibility. Leaders of democracies must answer to their citizens, and making threats only to later back down can result in political backlash. In 1982, for instance, after Argentina seized the Falkland Islands, strong public pressure in the United Kingdom reinforced Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher’s determination to act, lending additional credibility to the United Kingdom’s threat of military response. Such accountability generally gives a democratic state’s deterrent threats more weight than those of autocratic states. Speed is also a factor: a deterrent state’s threats are more credible when it is seen to be able to act swiftly and automatically against a threat. On the surface, artificial intelligence appears well suited to strengthen deterrence. By processing vast amounts of data, AI can provide better intelligence, clarify signals, and accelerate leaders’ decisions by producing faster and more comprehensive analyses. In the war in Ukraine, AI tools allow the Ukrainian military to scan satellite and drone images to identify Russian troop and equipment movements, missile sites, and supply routes; pull and aggregate data from radar, sound, and radio signals; and sift rapidly through training manuals, intelligence reports, or other materials to create a more complete picture of Russian force strength. For defense planners, such information allows a clearer assessment of their military capabilities relative to those of an adversary. AI can also reinforce deterrence by ensuring that each side’s actions are clearly communicated to the other. Since states frequently have incentives to bluff, they may struggle to demonstrate that they are truly prepared to follow through on their threats. By contrast, AI-enabled tools can ensure that when a country takes costly actions to signal its resolve, those actions are communicated quickly, clearly, and consistently. The adversary’s own AI systems can then efficiently interpret these signals, lessening the risk of misperception. For instance, by tracking domestic public opinion in real time, AI tools can help a democratic country demonstrate that it is prepared to act by showing that its threatened response is backed by real political support. Adversaries can then use their own AI tools to affirm that this support is genuine. Using AI to spot patterns and anomalies that humans might miss—such as sudden changes in troop movements, financial flows, or cyberactivity—can give leaders a clearer read on an adversary’s intentions. Because an aggressor can exploit even slight delays in a target country’s response—to seize territory or otherwise advance its aims—deterrence works best when the latter is able to persuade the aggressor that it will respond quickly enough to deny it any such time advantage. AI helps reinforce this perception by enabling defenders to detect challenges earlier and respond faster. Improving leaders’ long-term planning can strengthen and maintain credibility in longer crises, too. By running large numbers of “what if” scenarios—using data on force, geography, supply lines, and alliances—AI can provide leaders a clearer picture of how a conflict might unfold and help them maintain consistent strategies as conditions evolve .STRENGTH AND FRAGILITY

The same AI technologies that strengthen deterrence can also make it vulnerable to exploitation. Rather than helping a country credibly convey what it knows about itself, AI systems, if they are manipulated, can instead leave leaders unsure of their own capabilities and resolve. Adversaries could use AI to distort public opinion or poison the very AI systems on which a country’s leaders depend. By deploying these twin tactics—AI-enabled influence operations and AI model poisoning—an adversary could reshape a country’s information environment in ways that directly affect its deterrence. In the worst case, such confusion could cause a country’s deterrence to fail, even when its underlying capabilities and resolve are strong.
An adversary could also use influence operations to target a country’s public as well as influential figures shaping that country’s national debate, including decision-makers in government. Recent advances in data science and generative <mark>AI have made</mark> influence <mark>operations</mark> far <mark>more potent across</mark> three linked areas: target identification, persona creation, and individually tailored content. Previously, adversaries seeking to deploy targeted propaganda could only group people into clusters based on similar attributes. With modern AI, however, they can automate this process using data science to target individuals at a massive level.
With these tools, AI can predict targets’ susceptibility to specific narratives or to fake social media profiles that are designed to attract their attention. Whereas bots were once clumsy and easily spotted, generative AI can today make so-called synthetic personas that appear authentic and escape ready detection. These fake profiles can be developed over time to become indistinguishable from real users—featuring realistic posting habits, interests, and language quirks. Moreover, fake accounts can now be created and operated at an enormous scale, making them harder to detect. Such developments allow these personas to spread synthetic content into targeted communities. Seeded across multiple social media platforms, they can steer debate and inflame divisions. To weaken public resolve in the United States, for instance, such fake personas may spread claims that the U.S. military is overstretched, that allies free-ride on American security, or that particular international causes are not worth fighting for. Amplifying messages across many platforms can make false information feel true, or at least create sufficient confusion to undermine public consensus around an issue.
AI systems could give <mark>information warfare</mark> a potent new role in coercion and conflict. Using thousands of unique fake accounts, AI tools may soon be able to deliver individually tailored content in real time across an entire population. This is cognitive warfare, and the implications for deterrence are clear. Because much of a democracy’s deterrent credibility is tied to domestic political pressures, operations that manipulate public sentiment can weaken that state’s signals of resolve. AI manipulations might make a country’s domestic audience less inclined to support a strong military response to an act of foreign aggression—especially one against an ally—and thus <mark>distort</mark> polling <mark>data and</mark> other supposedly empirical signals from the public to which democratic leaders pay attention. This can leave such leaders unsure of how much support they truly have and how much backlash they might face if they yield. Such uncertainty can cause hesitation, weaken leaders’ resolve, and cloud their <mark>decision-making</mark>—all of which can make a state’s deterrent threats appear less convincing.
State-aligned groups are already exploring ways to undermine information security through AI-enabled influence operations. One example is GoLaxy, a Chinese company that uses generative AI tools and vast open-source data sets to build detailed psychological profiles of surveilled individuals and deploy, on a large scale, synthetic personas that mimic authentic users. The company’s campaigns often entail gathering detailed information on influential figures, using that information to produce messages that are likely to persuade targeted audiences, and then sending those messages from carefully crafted social media personas. By achieving an acute level of precision and amplifying misleading narratives across multiple platforms, such operations can sow confusion, corrode public discourse, and weaken the domestic base that makes deterrent signals credible abroad. GoLaxy’s alignment with <mark>Chinese</mark> state priorities and its ties to state-linked research institutes and superconducting firms make it a sophisticated propaganda engine.

POISONING THE WELL
Another pathway that adversaries can take to create uncertainty for defenders is model poisoning: the strategic <mark>manipulation of</mark> the <mark>AI systems</mark> on which governments rely for intelligence and decision-making support. By corrupting these systems’ training data or compromising their analytical pipelines, adversaries can distort a defender’s understanding of its relative strength and of the urgency of the threat. A system that displays an underestimation of an adversary’s powers can encourage unwarranted confidence in a defender; one that exaggerates the nature of the threat can induce hesitation. Either way, the effective manipulation of such AI systems could do more than simply complicate a defender’s crisis management—it <mark>could</mark> weaken the credibility of its deterrent signals and thus <mark>create dangerous risks</mark>.
Essentially, model poisoning works by manipulating a model’s data pipeline so that it overlooks important information and absorbs false inputs. This, in turn, can push the system toward misleading or degraded assessments. One method is by planting false information in the data sets that an AI system ingests to learn. Appearing harmless to human reviewers, this hidden information can nonetheless weaken or bias a model’s reasoning capabilities—for example, by tricking it into flagging certain types of malware as benign so that an adversary might sneak behind an AI-driven firewall. Although no instances of such an approach have yet been recorded, current AI research has demonstrated that existing data sets are vulnerable to this type of data-poisoning attack. What was once theoretical is now possible in practice.
Models can also be poisoned by creating corrupted webpages. An AI system is constantly performing live searches of the Internet for new information; these sites could inject hidden instructions to it and thus skew the model’s assessment. If the filters that screen incoming data are weak, even a small number of corrupted sites can induce inaccurate responses.
An especially stealthy form of <mark>information warfare</mark>, model poisoning <mark>allows adversaries to distort a defender’s understandings about capabilities</mark> and resolve—its own and those of others—by changing the very workings of the tools they use for clarity. In a crisis, poisoning <mark>could encourage a leader to</mark> hesitate or—worse—<mark>miscalculate, weakening deterrence and opening</mark> the <mark>door to escalation</mark>.

3. Housing Affordability Trends
As <mark>private equity firms</mark> continue to expand their footprint in the single-family rental (SFR) market, the consequences for housing affordability <mark>are</mark> becoming increasingly difficult to ignore. Backed by vast financial reserves and sophisticated data tools, these institutional players are zeroing in on the very properties that first-time buyers typically seek. In today’s competitive housing environment, “a mortgage, a modest down payment and the emotional considerations involved in choosing a family home simply can’t compete with all-cash offers made within hours of the property being listed” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 46).
This laser-focused approach has made it even harder for average families to enter the housing market. In places like Phoenix, Houston, and Las Vegas, institutional buyers have taken control of significant portions of the entry-level housing stock. One study highlighted how, in a Nashville suburb, “four institutional landlords collectively controlled three quarters of local rental houses,” <mark>taking advantage of</mark> their <mark>capital to outbid potential homeowners and charging rental rates</mark> nearly <mark>30% “higher</mark> than monthly ownership costs” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, pp. 26, 22).
But their <mark>impact isn’t limited to</mark> competitive <mark>home buying. These firms are also reshaping the</mark> rental <mark>market through</mark> consistent and <mark>deliberate rent hikes</mark>. Tricon Residential, for example, significantly raised prices across its portfolio. During the second quarter of 2021, “rents on new leases in their single-family ‘same home portfolio’ increased by 17.0% on average. For tenants renewing their leases, rents increased by 4.7%” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 34). And the trend didn’t slow down. In the following quarter, “rents for new leases increased by 19.1%, and by 5.7% for renewals, making for a <mark>blended rent growth of 8.8%</mark>” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 34). Even during the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, “amidst widespread lockdowns, stay-at-home orders, high unemployment rates, and economic uncertainty, Tricon still pushed 8.0% rent increases on new leases” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 34).
These hikes are not just responses to market pressures, they reflect intentional <mark>efforts</mark> to manipulate supply and control pricing. American Homes 4 Rent, for example, “artificially and intentionally constrains supply to keep prices high,” strategically phasing the rollout of its build for-rent homes rather than releasing them all at once (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 34).
Institutional landlords have also adopted tactics that place a greater financial burden on tenants. As Duvisac et al. (2020) report, “their strategies can <mark>involve</mark> squeezing renters by deferring maintenance, shifting the cost of utilities as pass-through charges to tenants, and <mark>pushing existing renters out through harassment and legal loopholes</mark>” (p. 7).
In addition to their market power, these firms have benefited from favorable policy environments. A prime example is the CARES Act, which offered “a $170 billion tax saving subsidy over the next ten years for the richest of real estate investors” (Duvisac et al., 2020, p. 9). This windfall has only strengthened their ability to purchase affordable properties and reposition them as high-priced rentals, further shrinking the path to homeownership for many middle-income families.
Altogether, these trends point to a deeper reality. Institutional investors are not merely reacting to housing conditions, they are actively reshaping them. By concentrating on high-demand segments of the market and leveraging their financial, political, and technological advantages, these firms are making it increasingly difficult for millions of Americans to turn the idea of homeownership into a reality.

3.1. Rental Demand, Corporate Strategy, and Rising Rents The ongoing shortage of rental housing in the U.S., combined with growing demand from would-be homeowners who have been priced out of the market, has created a perfect storm, one that has significantly benefited institutional landlords. Armed with substantial investment capital and operating in an environment of limited competition, these firms have implemented aggressive strategies aimed at extracting more value from their tenants. As Frank Nothaft of Freddie Mac put it, the current landscape is “giving the upper hand to institutional investors who spent more than $25 billion since 2012 buying single-family homes to rent” (Gittelsohn & Perlberg, 2014). Executives within the industry have made little effort to hide their motivations. One executive from American Residential Properties was candid about their pricing strategy, stating, “It’s that supply-demand equation that allows us to get aggressive about raising rents” (Gittelsohn & Perlberg, 2014). In markets where options are limited, tenants have little choice but to accept rising prices, giving landlords more leverage to boost profits and reduce tenant turnover. But these rent hikes are only one part of the equation. Institutional single-family rental (SFR) firms are increasingly turning to a wide range of revenue-generating strategies. These include service fees, pass-through charges, and algorithm-driven rent adjustments. As Fields and Vergerio (2022) explain, “Invitation Homes and American Homes 4 Rent are forthcoming about the use of extraneous fees to increase total revenue,” which can include everything from utility reimbursements and late fees to charges for smart home devices and landscaping (p. 36). In fact, in 2021 alone, “$178 million of” American Homes 4 Rent’s “core revenue came from tenant chargebacks,” which “has increased significantly over the years,” rising from “12.5% of AH4R’s overall core revenue” in 2017 to “15.8% in 2021” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 36). Technology plays a key role in this business model. Firms like Tricon rely on internal platforms and analytics to drive decision-making at scale. According to Fields and Vergerio (2022), Tricon uses “data science to optimize rent pricing, review and track maintenance requests through their platform TriForce, and developed Triad, an underwriting software that automates buying single-family properties” (p. 18). Invitation Homes, another major player, has adopted a similar approach, having “underwritten over a million homes in the last decade through their AcquisitionIQ software” (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 18). Beyond these digital strategies, institutional landlords are now actively shaping how homes are built and maintained to suit their bottom lines. American Homes 4 Rent has focused heavily on build-for-rent (BFR) communities, which are specifically designed for operational efficiency. As Fields and Vergerio (2022) describe, “they can maintain their BFR communities at a fraction (25%) of the normal cost, since every aspect of the home has been optimized to reduce maintenance expenses” (p. 18). This approach allows firms to better control rent prices and minimize operational disruptions, all while boosting margins. While this streamlined model is effective at driving profits, it often comes at the expense of renters. Many of the industry’s practices, fee stacking, standardized service models, and unrelenting rent increases, reflect a business approach that prioritizes investor returns over tenant stability. As Fields and Vergerio (2022) point out, “corporate landlords were quick to resume extractive practices as soon as the market picked back up, weeding out ‘delinquent’ tenants unable to keep up with ever-increasing rents” (p. 37). Altogether, the rise in rental demand has created a lucrative opportunity for institutional landlords. But their data-driven operations, layered revenue models, and profit-focused infrastructure are doing more than just maximizing returns, they are reshaping the rental landscape and altering the power dynamics between landlords and tenants in lasting ways. 3.2. Where It’s Happening: Geographic Hotspots Institutional investment in single-family rental housing isn’t spread evenly across the United States. Instead, it tends to be concentrated in select metropolitan areas, especially across the Sunbelt, where the potential for investor returns is highest. As noted by the U.S. Government Accountability Office (2024), “institutional investors cluster or concentrate their home purchases in certain metropolitan areas to achieve operational efficiency and cost savings through economies of scale” (p. 35). Cities like Atlanta, Charlotte, Jacksonville, Phoenix, Las Vegas, and Tampa have emerged as some of the most prominent hotspots. According to federal data, “as of June 2022, regions with the heaviest concentration of institutional investment were Atlanta, Jacksonville, and Charlotte. In these markets, institutional investor-owned homes constituted about 25 percent, 21 percent, and 18 percent of the single-family rental market, respectively” (U.S. GAO, 2024, p. 37). While institutional investors may only own a small portion of single-family homes nationwide, their presence becomes far more significant when viewed through a local lens. As Seymour and Shelton (2023) explain, “300,000 rental homes are a small fraction of the entire U.S. inventory, but they are highly concentrated in specific metropolitan areas” (p. 57). These concentrations are not accidental. They reflect calculated investment decisions that focus on cities with “strong underlying fundamentals in terms of housing demand and a permissive regulatory environment, including a lack of tenant protections that might constrain their business models” (Seymour & Shelton, 2023, p. 57). Atlanta serves as a clear example of how deeply these investors have embedded themselves in certain markets. A focused analysis of five counties in the Atlanta metro area found that “the top 10 percent of census tracts account for over half of the total properties owned by these companies”, and that landlords “own thousands in predominantly Black and working-class suburbs of south DeKalb, south Fulton and Clayton counties” (Seymour & Shelton, 2023, p. 58). In some neighborhoods, the level of market dominance is even more extreme. Seymour and Shelton (2023) point out that “we can identify twenty-four census tracts where these corporateowned SFR properties account for more than 50 percent of all SFRs, and in some tracts these figures rise to as much as 80 percent” (p. 58).

3.3. A Tipping Point for Affordability
<mark>As institutional investors</mark> continue to <mark>expand their portfolios in the single-family rental</mark> (SFR) <mark>space, the effects on affordability</mark> are becoming increasingly difficult to ignore, and <mark>may</mark> soon <mark>reach a point of no return</mark>. While these firms often portray themselves as simply reacting to market demand, their actions tell a different story. As Duvisac et al. (2020) point out, “the aim of private equity backed corporate landlords of increasing returns to their investors by squeezing rents out of tenants is inherently at odds with ensuring that individuals have access to safe and affordable housing” (p. 7).
This business model, marked by rent hikes, additional fees, and deferred maintenance, has direct and lasting consequences for renters. Research by Garriga, Gete, and Tsouderou (2022) shows that “a significant part of these investors followed a buy-and-hold strategy, which might suggest searching for rental income,” but in practice, this has led to “a significant impact on house prices, rents, and affordability,” particularly in areas where housing supply is already tight (p. 5).
The burden on renters is substantial. Even before the pandemic, close to half of the country’s 43 million renter households were spending a disproportionate share of their income on housing. In fact, “a quarter spent more than 50% of their income on rent every month” (Duvisac et al., 2020, p. 4). In neighborhoods heavily targeted by institutional buyers, rent increases have been particularly steep, leaving working-class tenants with fewer options and little negotiating power. These challenges are even more acute in communities of color, where “private equity-owned properties that are over-leveraged” tend to be “also more concentrated,” <mark>creating conditions that heighten</mark> the <mark>risk of</mark> instability and <mark>eviction</mark> (Duvisac et al., 2020, p. 8).
Meanwhile, the American dream of homeownership is slipping further out of reach. <mark>Although home values recovered between 2008 and 2016, the gains didn’t translate into ownership</mark>. During this same period, “overall homeownership rates fell to a historic low of 63%” (Duvisac et al., 2020, p. 7). The landscape has shifted, ownership is no longer the dominant model. Instead, a growing share of homes is now owned by corporate landlords, signaling a larger transition away from household equity and toward a rental economy driven by investor returns.
If this trend continues unchecked, it could represent a defining transformation in the U.S. housing system. This is no longer just a question of rising costs. What’s unfolding is a deeper structural shift, one in which housing is increasingly treated as a vehicle for profit, rather than a basic human need. As Duvisac et al. (2020) warn, “housing has become a product for quick and high returns for investors, which incentivizes practices that make it unaffordable or of poor quality for the many” (p. 8).

4. Market Consolidation and Competition Over the past decade, the single-family rental (SFR) market has experienced a major shift. What was once a fragmented landscape dominated by small, independent landlords has steadily evolved into one controlled by a few powerful corporate players. This transformation began in the wake of the 2008 foreclosure crisis, which opened the door for institutional investors to acquire large volumes of distressed properties. As one study notes, “the single-family rental industry emerged as a new site of institutional investment in the aftermath of the 2008 foreclosure crisis” and quickly began to consolidate (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 6). Today, some of the biggest names in the industry, such as Invitation Homes, American Homes 4 Rent, and Pretium Partners’ Progress Residential, each manage portfolios exceeding 50,000 homes, with some approaching or even surpassing 80,000 units. These companies didn’t grow solely through one-off acquisitions; instead, they scaled through major mergers and strategic rollups. As Seymour and Shelton (2023) explain, “there has been a period of SFR industry consolidation through mergers and acquisitions, alongside shifts in their corporate structures”, helping these firms cement their dominance (p. 57). Tricon Residential offers a clear example of this trend. The company became the fourth-largest publicly held SFR operator following its “transformative” merger with Silver Bay in 2017, a deal that added over 9,000 homes to its holdings (Fields & Vergerio, 2022, p. 31). Similarly, Pretium Partners, the private equity firm behind Progress Residential, strengthened its grip on the market by acquiring Front Yard Residential in 2021, an acquisition that made it the largest private SFR owner in the country. While these firms often downplay their national footprint, usually citing that they own just 1–3% of all single-family homes, the real story lies at the local level. Their influence is far more pronounced in specific regions, especially in Sunbelt cities and high-growth neighborhoods. As Seymour and Shelton (2023) point out, “national statistics obscure how local markets possess high concentrations” (p. 57).

In fact</mark>, in many US cities, <mark>there are more than enough</mark> vacant <mark>units to provide homes for every</mark> individual and <mark>family</mark> currently living <mark>without</mark> permanent <mark>housing</mark>.
One recent report found that, “With more than 36,000 unhoused residents, Los Angeles simultaneously has over 93,000 units sitting vacant, nearly half of which are withheld from the housing market.” In New York, the quantity of vacant rent-stabilized units alone—estimated at around 70,000—is larger than the total population of individuals that currently reside within the city’s network of shelters.
These apartments remain unoccupied because many landlords have calculated that it is more profitable to keep rent-regulated units off the market than to refurbish or maintain—even to a minimum standard—homes rented out to tenants at below market rates.
At least 100,000 more New York apartments sit empty because their owners hold them for “seasonal, recreational or occasional use,” or simply use them as long-term investment chips that they never intend to occupy. This dynamic also exists in other cities around the country, particularly in the most expensive housing markets.

Nothing lasts forever: Every international order finds its end. Pax Romana stabilized the greater Mediterranean world, until decline set in. The British global order flourished in the 19th century but came apart amid two world wars in the 20th. Today, in an unruly world led by an erratic America, it’s hard not to wonder if the <mark>US</mark>-led <mark>order</mark> is on its way out.
Since 1945, that order has <mark>generate</mark>d tremendous <mark>peace</mark>, prosperity and freedom. It can only be termed a smashing success. But stresses on that order — those imposed by its challengers, and those imposed by its creator — have been mounting. One way of gauging just how severe the risks have become is by considering the various ways an order might end.
The brilliant Cambridge historian Brendan Simms has suggested that international orders typically <mark>end</mark> in one of three ways: <mark>Through</mark> defeat in war or some catastrophic failure of deterrence; through <mark>economic decline</mark> or a divergence between the order’s political and economic arrangements; or through the collapse of respect for its guiding rules and norms.
The US order has proven remarkably resilient, but the <mark>possibility of</mark> a <mark>breakdown is growing</mark> as America piles up risk on each of these dimensions at once. And while recent leaders, including President Donald Trump, have made important moves to shore up the order, America’s present policies are increasingly making those dangers worse.
Orders can die of murder, exhaustion or suicide. Today, it’s difficult to rule any of those grim finales out.
How America Took the Lead
Order is about rules and rule-makers. International orders feature commonly accepted norms or principles meant to govern global behavior. Those rules are made, and sustained, by mighty actors and institutions. A long line of powers has sought to structure the globe to their liking. But since World War II, America’s order has been the biggest, most successful game in town.
The lesson US policymakers took from that conflict was that only a secure, prosperous system could ensure America’s own wellbeing. So the US built an order based on relatively free trade; a preference for human rights and democratic values; the prevention of great-power aggression and war; and institutionalized cooperation to address common problems.
<mark>Washington used</mark> its unmatched military and economic <mark>power to buoy</mark> the fortunes of like-minded <mark>nations</mark>. America, said President Harry Truman, was “assuming the responsibility which God Almighty intended” for “the welfare of the world in generations to come.”
Make no mistake: This endeavor was rooted in US interests. But because America was so powerful, and defined those interests so broadly, this project brought historic gains for much of the world.
Democracy went from endangered to dominant in the postwar decades. <mark>Trade flourished</mark> and living standards soared, first in the free world and then globally after communism fell. A <mark>world that suffered</mark> two all-consuming <mark>great-power wars</mark> in quick succession has <mark>avoided</mark> anything like <mark>that</mark> since 1945.
The US presided over a global golden age. Yet the stresses on America’s order have become impossible to ignore.
Revisionist powers — <mark>China</mark>, <mark>Russia</mark>, <mark>Iran</mark>, <mark>No</mark>rth <mark>Ko</mark>rea — <mark>are challenging</mark> a system they view as dangerous to their illiberal regimes and oppressive to their geopolitical ambitions. The Global South has become disillusioned with Western dominance. The US itself has seemed ambivalent, in recent decades, about world leadership. <mark>Threats to</mark> its <mark>economic and military supremacy</mark> have <mark>grown</mark> more <mark>severe</mark>.
Visit nearly any US ally, and you’ll notice conviction that American power remains necessary — and concern that the post-World War II order is slipping away. So how real is the danger? Let’s consider the three ways an order can come apart.
Losing a War
One path to failure runs through defeat or devastation in war. Nothing ruptures the authority of a hegemonic power like a humiliating beatdown on the battlefield. The Athenian empire collapsed after losing the Great Peloponnesian War. Britain won World War I, but never recovered from its costs.
For decades, America has been the sole superpower. As last month’s attack on Iran’s nuclear program reminded us, the Pentagon still possesses power-projection capabilities without peer. But anyone who thinks the US is militarily invincible hasn’t been paying attention.
The Pentagon faces a vexing military arithmetic problem. Several <mark>challenges</mark> — from Russia in Europe, Iran and its proxies in the Middle East, and China and North Korea in Asia — are <mark>stretching</mark> US <mark>resources</mark>. A superpower with a military designed to fight one war at a time is always at <mark>risk</mark> in a world of multiple, <mark>interlocking threats</mark>. But the danger of crushing defeat is most concentrated in the Western Pacific.
“The intelligence couldn’t be clearer,” said Secretary of the Air Force Frank Kendall in 2023. “<mark>China is preparing for</mark> a <mark>war</mark> and specifically for a war with the United States.” The Chinese threat is real, “and it could be imminent,” Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth observed this year. Those are only two of many alarming statements from US officials.
Beijing is building the forces and rehearsing the plans needed to attack Taiwan or otherwise reorder the Western Pacific. It is racing to construct a nuclear arsenal that will match, and maybe exceed, America’s. Meanwhile, Xi Jinping’s government is hoarding food, fuel and other resources. Xi would surely prefer to eject America from the Western Pacific peacefully. But he’s getting ready for a fight.
A US-China war <mark>would cause</mark> cascading economic carnage and bring serious risks of <mark>nuclear escalation</mark>. And if America lost — which is a real possibility — the damage to the US order would be profound. America’s <mark>alliances</mark> in the Indo-Pacific <mark>might</mark> <mark>fracture</mark>. A broken US military might struggle to police other parts of the world. “The trajectory must change,” the head of US Indo-Pacific Command, Admiral Samuel Paparo, has warned: America isn’t responding with the urgency the threat demands.
In fairness, there have been encouraging developments. Israel, with American help, has ravaged Iran and its proxies since late 2023. The US and its North Atlantic Treaty Organization allies have used the war in Ukraine to grind down Russian power.
Trump can take some credit for getting the allies to agree to spend 3.5% of GDP on defense, and another 1.5% on related investments. Over time, that spending will strengthen the military position of the democratic world. But global overstretch remains real, the trends in Asia are daunting, and the US still isn’t acting like it could lose World War III.
American military spending is below 3.5% of GDP, among the lowest levels since World War II, and could dip next year. Stockpiles of munitions and missile defenses are, reportedly, low, and have been depleted by recent scrapes in the Middle East.
With a moribund shipbuilding industry and a sluggish, fragile industrial base, America would struggle to replace assets lost in the opening phase of a fight. “You can’t AI your way out of material deficiency,” Paparo has argued: A country that can’t replace battlefield losses won’t win a grinding, great-power war.
No one, not even Xi Jinping, knows exactly how capable China’s untested military is. But as the Pacific military balance changes, the dangers of an order-breaking catastrophe mount.
An Economic Collapse
Orders don’t have to explode violently. They can also implode, when the leading power can’t — or won’t — sustain the economic arrangements that make the system work. The British order crumbled when two world wars bankrupted the empire. The American order has long rested on two economic pillars.
The first is simply the economic and <mark>financ</mark>ial wherewithal to <mark>sustain</mark> America’s global power — among other things, to <mark>pay for military</mark> capabilities <mark>that keep revisionist</mark> threats <mark>in check</mark>. The second pillar consists of economic arrangements that reinforce strategic commitments: the international economic leadership, the ties of trade and investment, that bind Washington to its allies and give them all a shared stake in preserving a US-led world.
Both pillars have been remarkably durable. For all the talk of decline, America’s share of global GDP is roughly the same as it was in the 1970s. The dollar dominates world trade and finance. Foreign investors have long been willing to support dollar dominance, and finance large US deficits, because those arrangements help Washington fund its alliance commitments and military muscle. And when the economic arrangements that underpin the order grow outdated or unbalanced, they are typically renegotiated — as happened when the US ditched the gold standard in 1971 and shifted to the floating exchange-rate system we know today.

<mark>The</mark> population in the <mark>US</mark>A <mark>is rapidly aging</mark>. According to the 2020 US Census, the elderly population, that is, people aged 65 and above, increased to 55.8 million in 2020 by 38% from a decade ago.1 Over the next two decades, the elderly population is projected to grow by another 25 million, exceeding 80 million by 2040. As households age, their income typically grows more slowly and may even decline due to retirement and widowhood, while their healthcare expenses rise sharply. Shouldering the dual burden of housing and healthcare stretches the already-limited budget of many elderly households. In 2021, approximately 11.2 million elderly households in the USA were housing-cost burdened, <mark>spending over 30% of</mark> their <mark>income on</mark> housing, an increase from 8.8 million in 2001, according to the Joint Center of Housing Studies of Harvard University (JCHS, 2023). This trend underscores an escalating housing affordability challenge for America’s aging population.
<mark>Elderly households</mark> play an increasingly important role in the <mark>rental housing</mark> market and <mark>face</mark> unprecedented rental <mark>affordability challenges</mark>. The share of elderly-headed renters among all renters has been increasing, from about 12% in 2000 to over 16% in 2021 (JCHS, 2023).2 Moreover, elderly renter households are more likely to be housing-cost burdened—spending over 30% of income on housing—compared to homeowners. In 2020, <mark>over 56%</mark> of elderly renters (or about 4 million) were <mark>rent burdened</mark>, while 26% of elderly homeowners faced similar housing cost burdens (JCHS, 2023). The severity of the housing affordability issue will likely intensify as population aging accelerates in the coming decades.3
Housing affordability has been a persistent challenge faced by renters in the USA over the last decade or so. The median rent in the USA increased from US$841 in 2010 to US$1062 in 2019, which is by about 26%.4 Between 2019 and 2021 alone, the median rent in the USA surged by 12%, which is double the inflation rate of 6% during the same period.5 Consequently, rent control has regained popularity as a policy tool to alleviate housing affordability pressures. In 2021, voters in Saint Paul, Minnesota approved a rent stabilization ordinance to curb rent increases. Rent control is also being implemented at a broader geographic scope, affecting both urban and rural areas. For example, in 2019, Oregon enacted the first statewide rent control (Schuetz, 2019). In the same year, New York also tightened its rent regulations. There were also discussions and proposals for nationwide rent control.6
The renewed public interest in rent control necessitates a closer examination of its impacts. Previous research dominated by economists has highlighted various inefficiencies and distortions to the housing market.7 Rather than focusing on market efficiency, we adopt an access-to-service perspective to analyze how benefits are allocated among different types of tenants and whether such allocations align with their need for assistance.8 In the absence of rent control, many tenants could be displaced by soaring rents. Displacement could have particularly negative impacts on long-term elderly tenants, <mark>severing vital</mark> neighborhood-specific <mark>amenities</mark> and ties, <mark>such as</mark> access to <mark>healthcare, caregivers, and social networks</mark>. Advocates for affordable housing contend that rent control offers essential insurance for these tenants and can play a role in promoting equity (Chew, 2020). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first paper to analyze the impacts of rent control on elderly renters.9 Given that elderly tenants face significant affordability challenges, it is reasonable to hypothesize that they might benefit more from rent control, given the benefits tend to increase with tenancy duration. This article aims to examine whether and to what extent rent stabilization can benefit elderly renters in the context of New York City (NYC), which has the longest history of implementing rent regulation in the USA.

 In this study, we utilize the New York City Housing Vacancy Survey (NYCHVS) from 2002 to 2017—a unique, high-quality housing dataset for analyzing rent control policies—to examine the severity of rent affordability challenges faced by elderly renters in NYC and the magnitude of benefits they receive from rent stabilization compared to younger renters. We measure rent affordability challenges by computing rent-to-income ratios and defining households as rent burdened if they spend over 30% of their income on rent. For benefits, we analyze both the access to rent-stabilized units and the rent discounts received by rent-stabilized tenants. Rent discount is the implicit subsidy from rent stabilization. It is defined as the difference between the counterfactual market rent10 of a rent-stabilized unit and its actual contract rent, estimated using the hedonic pricing model following Chen et al. (2023).11 We document that elderly tenants face substantially greater rent burdens than younger renters, with elderly tenants being about 19 percentage points (40%) more likely to be rent burdened. We also find that elderly tenants are about 10 percentage points (19%) more likely to live in a rent-stabilized unit. Additionally, elderly rent-stabilized tenants receive, on average, US$276 (or about 75%) more in rent discounts per month. These findings suggest that rent stabilization could improve equity along the age gradient—providing more assistance to elderly renters who face greater rent affordability challenges. However, further analysis reveals that the “elderly advantage” in NYC’s rent-stabilized market is primarily driven by the longer tenancy durations of elderly tenants. After controlling for tenancy duration, elderly tenants are less likely to live in rent-stabilized units, and the aforementioned differences in the magnitude of rent discounts between elderly and younger tenants disappear. In other words, rent stabilization does not favor the elderly; it benefits longer-term and more stable tenants, who tend to be elderly. This is a subtle yet crucial distinction because not all elderly renters are privileged with the same level of housing stability. Our results suggest that elderly tenants with shorter and less stable tenancy, who are more likely to be racial minorities, immigrants, or those with low levels of education and income, benefit less from rent stabilization. Our study sheds light on the nuanced equity consequences of rent stabilization. While NYC’s rent stabilization may promote equity across age groups, the correlations among housing stability, race, and income suggest that it does not always produce equitable outcomes along racial and socioeconomic lines. These seemingly conflicting equity consequences highlight the multidimensional nature of equity—underscoring that policies like rent stabilization have complex and multifaceted implications. Literature review This article contributes substantially to the rent control literature, which can be grouped into two broad categories. The first examines how rent control influences housing-market outcomes, such as housing prices, rents, land values, and housing supply,15 and misallocation and housing quality.16 Other studies explore broader social outcomes—such as crime, mobility, gentrification, and displacement—or focus on labor market effects.17 The second category, to which our work belongs, examines how rent control subsidies are distributed. Foundational analyses of first-generation rent control (Early, 2000; Gyourko and Linneman, 1989; Linneman, 1987) paved the way for more recent investigations. Current research on second-generation rent control (often referred to as rent stabilization) typically considers disparities across income levels and racial groups (Chen et al., 2023; Pollakowski, 2003), as well as immigrant–native differences (Bian et al., 2025). To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to investigate how rent stabilization subsidies are allocated between elderly and younger tenants. A defining feature of our approach is its emphasis on equity, which we view as providing greater support to those who are in greater need. By first examining rent burdens and then assessing the impact of rent stabilization, we evaluate how these subsidies affect different age groups. We also investigate whether these policy benefits are proportionately allocated to elderly tenants. Our study also aligns with the broader literature on elderly families’ housing behaviors and outcomes. Personal and household circumstances play a substantial role. For instance, Bonnet et al. (2010) find that widowhood often leads to relocation into rental properties and smaller homes, while Painter and Lee (2009) show that poor health and single head-of-household status increase the likelihood of leaving homeownership. Living closer to one’s children also reduces the probability of downsizing or becoming a renter. Financial considerations are equally crucial. Evidence indicates that limited household income prompts elderly homeowners to move (Feinstein and McFadden, 1989; Reschovsky, 1990), and high housing debt further influences their decisions, making them more likely to downsize (Bian, 2016) or to switch from owning to renting (Bian and Lin, 2022). However, most existing research centers on elderly homeowners. Meanwhile, one in five elderly households are renters, many of whom face acute housing affordability challenges—an issue that has received less attention. Our study addresses this gap by focusing on elderly renters. Finally, our work contributes to the literature on how housing instability affects family well-being. Many families, especially lower-income families, relocate involuntarily rather than by choice (Holupka and Newman, 2011), and housing instability has been linked to negative outcomes in health, child development, and social capital. Frequent moves disrupt access to healthcare and social services, making it more difficult to maintain regular appointments (Jelleyman and Spencer, 2008; Kirby and Kaneda, 2006; Sandel et al., 2018). Unstable housing also undermines children’s educational, social, and emotional development.18 Moreover, housing instability often weakens community ties, erodes social capital, and exacerbates neighborhood challenges such as crime and disinvestment (Morenoff et al., 2001). We extend this literature by showing that housing instability curtails the benefits of public policies: even among elderly renters, those with unstable housing are less likely to secure rent-stabilized units and, when they do, receive smaller rent discounts. Policy background and data Policy background In NYC, fewer than one-third of households are homeowners, while more than two-thirds are renters. NYC employs rent stabilization as a key strategy to preserve rent affordability. About half of all rental apartments—approximately one million units—are rent stabilized. Roughly 40% of rental units are unregulated, market-rate housing, and the remaining units include public housing and other subsidized options. NYC also has the longest history of implementing such a policy compared to other cities in the USA. The prevalence and long history of rent-stabilized housing in NYC make it an ideal setting for examining such policies. For eligibility, rent stabilization typically applies to buildings constructed before 1974 that contain six or more units. Unlike federal housing programs, rent stabilization is not means tested, which means that it has no income requirement, making it seemingly equal for all NYC residents to access. Some advocates tout this equal access as a tool for advancing equity in the housing market (Chew, 2020). Yet, it is unclear whether this approach facilitates equitable access across different age groups. To fill this gap, we employ access to rent-stabilized units as a key policy outcome variable. In addition to access to rent-stabilized units, we also examine the rent discounts received by rent-stabilized tenants. Rent stabilization caps rent growth and shields incumbent tenants from steep rent hikes. The rent caps are established annually by the Rent Guideline Board (RGB), which considers a mix of factors, including economic conditions, owner costs, and housing market dynamics. Additionally, it protects renters from eviction without cause and ensures the right to lease renewals. Since rent stabilization caps rent growth rates, tenants of rent-stabilized units can accumulate rent discounts over time. This is because the rent gap between two otherwise similar units, one rent stabilized and the other market rate, will widen with years of tenancy as the rent of the market-rate unit will likely grow faster, resulting in larger rent discounts for the rent-stabilized unit vis-à-vis the unregulated unit.19 There are legitimate concerns about the potential quality deterioration of rent-stabilized properties due to reduced maintenance efforts by landlords to offset revenue losses (Gyourko and Linneman, 1990). However, NYC’s updated rent stabilization guidelines mitigate these issues by permitting landlords to request rent increases above the set limits if they make substantial property improvements. For example, landlords can request additional rent increases if they have made building-wide improvements that benefit all residents through the Major Capital Improvements (MCI). This demonstrates that rent stabilization effectively reduces rents for tenants, providing an average monthly saving of approximately US$400 per unit from 2002 to 2017, even after adjusting for property quality. Data We use data from the NYCHVS, a repeated cross-sectional survey known for its uniqueness in housing research. The NYCHVS is particularly suited for examining rent control issues for several reasons. First, it accurately captures the rent regulation status of each housing unit, validated through administrative records. This feature distinguishes NYCHVS from other housing datasets, such as the American Housing Survey (AHS). Second, the survey includes comprehensive measures of housing quality, enabling a more precise estimation of rent discounts than previous studies. Third, it provides extensive details on tenant demographics and socioeconomic characteristics. Additionally, NYC provides representative samples of the entire housing stock in NYC. ## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case never explicitly weighs its impact (degraded deterrence/escalation to war) against the Con's likely impacts (housing affordability, market liquidity, small investor rights). No card extends a magnitude, probability, or timeframe comparison in the back-half. A weighing card would tag something like "Deterrence failure = existential nuclear risk outweighs economic impacts — magnitude and irreversibility" (e.g., a 2024 RAND or Brookings piece on opportunity-cost framing) and sit at the bottom of Advantage One or as a separate "Weighing" contention.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No card preemptively answers the Con's core generics: "ban kills mom-and-pop LLCs," "corporate ownership is <5% of SFR stock," "supply-side reform solves better," or "federalism/state preemption." An offensive spike would be a 2024-25 card tagged "Corporate SFR buyers are overwhelmingly institutional, not small LLCs — ban is narrowly tailored" (e.g., Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta 2024 dataset) or "State bans have failed — federal action necessary" (e.g., NAHB 2025 survey of state-law loopholes), placed in the 1AC as a dedicated "Spikes" mini-contention.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case contains no embedded frontlines to the responses it will actually face (e.g., "definition of 'corporate' captures family trusts," "ban chills all investment," "enforcement impossible," "marijuana ops will just rent"). A defensive spike would be a card like "DOJ already uses LLC transparency rules to trace grow-house ownership — ban is enforceable" (FinCEN 2024 final rule coverage) or "Chinese TCOs *buy* to avoid detection — renting leaves paper trail" (DEA 2023 congressional testimony), inserted as a "Preempts" block after the Tusinski '25 card.
