Open-source case doc read by Summit BM (season Elo rank #90) on the 2024-2025 november-december PF topic — "Resolved: The United States should substantially reduce its military support of Taiwan." — at: The Princeton Classic.

Shell
A, interpretation: debaters must disclose all non-identity constructive positions
previously read in the tournament open source with highlighting, tags, and any
paraphrased text on the 2024-2025 OpenCaselist PF wiki under their school and team
at least 20 minutes before the round in which they’re read.
B, violation:
They don’t
C, standards:
1. First, resource access - disclosure makes valuable and winning content public, benefitting small
schools without varsity to teach norms, and letting programs break into the national circuit
where they couldn’t before. Debate is really inaccessible right now, and the barrier to entry for
under-priviledged programs, novices, and non-circuit schools is incredibly high, as there’s no
access to coaches or camps to teach you both how to find high enough quality evidence to
compete, and how to structure that evidence in a case that adheres to debate circuit norms.
Open source solves by both proliferating high quality evidence top-down, and by demonstrating
how that evidence is structured in the case document. Antonucci ‘5 confirms that open source
systems are preferable and may be the only structural, rule-based answer to resource inequities
[by] reducing entry barriers. They finalize that an open source system would fundamentally
change the evidence economy without targeting anyone. Thus, disclosing only increases
inclusion in the debate space.
a. Even if theory’s bad for small schools, many couldn’t make it to these rounds in the first
place without access to open source resources
b. And since most rounds are substance, not theory, we improve the vast majority of them
by providing better prep and deeper engagement at the cost of a few disclosure debates
2. Academic integrity – without disclosure we can’t check proper citations or whether your
evidence is miscut. If a teacher finds out a student plagiarized, the student gets failed, similarly,
you should lose for not having academic integrity so it’s an independent voter. Even if the linked
cards in full-text disclosure are good, we have no idea how they have been cut. Three minutes is
never enough time to check if all cut cards in the round have been ethically cut. Open source is
the best check on misconstruing evidence by demonstrating both how the card was cut and how
it is being represented in the context of the argument. Ethical evidence is a prerequisite to an
educational and fair debate. Without it there’s an incentive to always misrepresent evidence to
a competitive end.

3. Third, competitive equity - big schools use connections and scouts to get flows and prep teams
out, so disclosure gives everyone access to cases as they’re read in-round, letting judges evaluate
rounds fairly
a. In this round especially, reading substance puts us at a disadvantage cause they didn’t
disclose and we did, giving them time to prep perfect speeches so reading theory is the
only way to equalize
4. Fourth, depth of clash. Without the full cut card and paraphrase disclosed, time is spent before
tournaments collecting evidence from the wiki trying to discern what it means and how it
functions in the argument. With open source, all of that time is instead allocated to
understanding the arguments and how to interact with them, improving the depth of clash
debates beyond surface level PF clash, where teams intentionally read squirrely arguments or
hide nuances to win by surprise.
D, voters
1. Vote on education since it’s a portable skill that schools fund debate for, and fairness lets judges
correctly determine winners and makes debate worth participating in. Inclusion is a voter, it
controls the internal link to both and it’s an impact multiplier that maximizes education and
fairness in the community.
2. Drop the debater, three warrants a. First, you should endorse the better norm because it improves all future debates,
outweighing harms in this one on scope
b. Second, getting dropped deters future abuses like not disclosing, since all teams want to
win they’ll disclose to improve their competitive chances
c. Third, dropping the argument would be dropping their whole case, and in that instance
you should vote for us for at least being right about what norm is best
3. Additionally, use competing interps over reasonability - it improves direct clash and prevents
subconscious intervention on what’s “reasonable”
4. And don’t grant them RVIs, three warrants a. First, they don’t win for proving they’re fair, it’s a floor not a ceiling
b. Second, that’d make theory risky to run, especially against good theory debaters who
can be abusive without repercussion
c. Third, even if it’s fine to not disclose, that doesn’t make disclosing abusive, so we
shouldn’t lose for it
Fiat is illusory, meaning if you affirm or negate nothing happens. Whether or not they read the
substance, it’s pointless. You as a judge have more of a tangible impact affirming or negating a theory
round because it sets norms in the debate space. It is important that most of your offense is on theory
debate so you should have responded in second constructive. Voting for us on theory lets us set norms in
the debate space.
Thus, negate

“[eDebate] open source? resp to Morris”; December 8;
http://www.ndtceda.com/pipermail/edebate/2005-December/064806.html //]
a. Open source systems are preferable to the various punishment proposals in circulation. It's better to
share the wealth than limit production or participation. Various flavors of argument communism appeal
to different people, but banning interesting or useful research(ers) seems like the most destructive
solution possible. Indeed, open systems may be the only structural, rule-based answer to resource
inequities. Every other proposal I've seen obviously fails at the level of enforcement. Revenue sharing
(illegal), salary caps (unenforceable and possibly illegal) and personnel restrictions (circumvented faster
than you can say 'information is fungible') don't work. This would - for better or worse. b. With the help
of a middling competent archivist, an open source system would reduce entry barriers. This is especially
true on the novice or JV level. Young teams could plausibly subsist entirely on a diet of scavenged
arguments. A novice team might not wish to do so, but the option can't hurt. c. An open source system
would fundamentally change the evidence economy without targeting anyone or putting anyone out of
a job. It seems much smarter (and less bilious) to change the value of a professional card-cutter's work
than send the KGB after specific counter-revolutionary teams.
