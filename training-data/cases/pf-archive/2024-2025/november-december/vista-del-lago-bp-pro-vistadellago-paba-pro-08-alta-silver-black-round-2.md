Numerous <mark>scholars have analyzed when and why Beijing uses force</mark>. Most reach a similar conclusion: <mark>China attacks </mark>not when it feels confident about the future but <mark>when it worries its enemies are closing in.</mark> As Thomas Christensen, the director of the China and the World Program at Columbia University, writes, the Chinese Communist Party wages war when it perceives an opening window of vulnerability regarding its territory and immediate periphery, or a closing window of opportunity to consolidate control over disputed areas. This pattern holds regardless of the strength of China’s opponent. In fact, Beijing often has attacked far superior foes—including the U.S.—to cut them down to size and beat them back from Chinese-claimed or otherwise sensitive territory. Examples of this are plentiful. In 1950, for instance, the fledgling PRC was less than a year old and destitute, after decades of civil war and Japanese brutality. Yet it nonetheless mauled advancing U.S. forces in Korea out of concern that the Americans would conquer North Korea and eventually use it as a base to attack China. In the expanded Korean War that resulted, China suffered almost 1 million casualties, risked nuclear retaliation, and was slammed with punishing economic sanctions that stayed in place for a generation. But to this day, Beijing celebrates the intervention as a glorious victory that warded off an existential threat to its homeland. In 1962, the PLA attacked Indian forces, ostensibly because they had built outposts in Chinese-claimed territory in the Himalayas. The deeper cause was that the CCP feared that it was being surrounded by the Indians, Americans, Soviets, and Chinese Nationalists, all of whom had increased their military presence near China in prior years. Later that decade, fearing that China was next on Moscow’s hit list as part of efforts to defeat “counterrevolution,” the Chinese military ambushed Soviet forces along the Ussuri River and set off a seven-month undeclared conflict that once again risked nuclear war. In the late ’70s, Beijing picked a fight with Vietnam. The purpose, remarked Deng Xiaoping, then the leader of the CCP, was to “teach Vietnam a lesson” after it started hosting Soviet forces on its territory and invaded Cambodia, one of China’s only allies. Deng feared that China was being surrounded and that its position would just get worse with time. And from the ’50s to the ’90s, China nearly started wars on three separate occasions by firing artillery or missiles at or near Taiwanese territory, in 1954–55, 1958, and 1995–96. In each case, the goal was—among other things—to deter Taiwan from forging a closer relationship with the U.S. or declaring its independence from China. To be clear, every decision for war is complex, and factors including domestic politics and the personality quirks of individual leaders have also figured in China’s choices to fight. Yet the overarching pattern of behavior is consistent: Beijing turns violent when confronted with the prospect of permanently losing control of territory. <mark>It tends to attack</mark> <mark>one enemy to scare off others.</mark> And it rarely gives advance warning or waits to absorb the initial blow.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
ABSENT: The case contains no explicit impact framing, magnitude/probability/timeframe comparisons, or pre-written weighing mechanisms (e.g., "probability first," "scale outweighs," "try-or-die"). Cards like Joske 18 and Roy 24 describe risks of conflict but never connect them to a structured weighing argument that can be extended in Summary/Final Focus. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Weighing — Probability of invasion is near-zero; risk of miscalculation from over-support outweighs" and cite a source quantifying invasion likelihood vs. escalation risks from U.S. arms sales, placed as a standalone contention or as the final card of Contention 1.

### Offensive spikes
Roy 24 — "Xi doesn’t necessarily need to solve the Taiwan problem during his tenure to earn a legacy… Xi has three huge domestic projects… A struggling economy does not make Beijing more likely to launch a war… The natural reaction to a lack of strength at home is to be more cautious in foreign affairs, not more aggressive."
This card preempts the common Con argument that Chinese economic decline increases diversionary war incentives by arguing the opposite: weakness breeds caution, not aggression, directly undercutting a core Con link story.

### Evidence quality
ABSENT: The evidence base relies heavily on secondary commentary (Babones 15, Roy 24, Magnier '9/13, Gilley '10) and journalistic reporting (Jiangtao 10-19) rather than primary-source data, government assessments, or peer-reviewed studies with transparent methodologies. No cards cite classified/official net assessments, PLA capability studies, or quantitative datasets on arms sales vs. conflict outcomes. A card filling this gap would cite a source like "DoD 2024 China Military Power Report" or "CSIS 2024 Taiwan Invasion Wargame Results" with a tag like "Empirical — PLA lacks amphibious lift for cross-Strait invasion; wargames show 70%+ failure rate even without U.S. intervention," placed in the inheritance/link level of Contention 1.

### Collapse flexibility
ABSENT: The case appears structured around a single narrative — China won't invade regardless of U.S. policy — with all cards feeding that one thesis. There are no distinct, independent impact scenarios (e.g., "U.S. arms sales provoke accidental escalation," "arms sales divert resources from higher-priority theaters," "arms sales undermine diplomatic off-ramps"). A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Independent impact — U.S. military support crowds out diplomatic engagement; reduces crisis management channels" citing a source like "Kissinger 2023 / Blinken 2024 speech on guardrails," placed as a separate sub-point or Contention 2.

### Warrant depth
Roy 24 — "Beijing is nowhere near the level of superiority that would guarantee a successful invasion given the probability of US and Japanese military resistance… A cross-Strait war would disrupt regional economic activity, threatening the livelihoods of millions of Chinese. The resulting social turmoil could endanger Xi's rule."
This card provides multi-step warrants: (1) military capability gap (lift, logistics, joint ops), (2) economic cost mechanism (regional disruption → domestic unrest), (3) regime survival logic (Xi prioritizes domestic projects over gambling), rather than merely asserting "invasion unlikely."

### Internal consistency
ABSENT: The case contains a tension between cards arguing invasion is *impossible* (Babones 15: "never have the capacity… not now, not later, not ever") and cards arguing invasion is *unlikely but possible if provoked* (Jiangtao 10-19: "dangerous flashpoint," "accelerating arms race," "security situation… deteriorate"; Beckley and Brands '21: "China attacks when it worries its enemies are closing in"). Babones 15 claims technical impossibility; Jiangtao and Beckley/Brands treat conflict as a live risk sensitive to U.S. policy. A cohesive case would resolve this by either (a) dropping the "impossible" claim and framing as "low probability but high sensitivity to provocation," or (b) keeping "impossible" and explaining why Jiangtao's "flashpoint" rhetoric doesn't translate to actual invasion risk.

**Score: 4/10** — The case assembles relevant, high-authority cards with strong warrants on the core "invasion unlikely" thesis and includes one effective offensive spike and one defensive spike, but lacks embedded weighing, collapse flexibility, and evidence diversity, and contains a significant internal contradiction between "impossible" and "flashpoint" framings that opponents can exploit.
