Case for the Negation: Arlington LC

Hyde ‘16 explains that during the late 1800’s, Africa was divided by European powers with no consideration given to the people actually living there. When African colonies gained independence, they inherited the poorly drawn borders that left many ethnic groups divided and sparked numerous separatist movements.

However, contrary to common assumptions, lasting stability and peace is now within reach. Carter ‘19 found that, in Africa, both intrastate violence and civil wars have declined in frequency and intensity over the past twenty years.

The impacts of this stability are tangible. Economically, the ADBG ‘24 reports that in 2024, Africa accounted for eleven of the twenty fastest growing economies. And, GDP growth is projected to remain high. More generally, the WHO ‘22 found that life expectancy in Africa has increased by 10 years since 2000. There have been marked improvements in health services and infectious diseases prevention. At its core, people are finally beginning to live more prosperous, healthy, and long lives.

As affirming <mark>would upset this</mark> remarkable period of stability and bring progress to a grinding halt, our sole argument is protecting African stability.

Currently, the AU tames border disputes. As Palmo ‘24 explains, the constitutive act of the AU states that it is committed to respecting the territorial integrity of established countries and practices the principle of non-interference. AU recognition of Somaliland would reverse decades of precedent and signal that the AU is ready and willing to redraw borders and recognize breakaway regions.

This would dramatically fuel secessionist movements in two key ways:

First, by stoking radicalization and recruitment. According to Thomas ‘20, studying the past century of African secessionist movements, successful recruitment is dependent on the perceived legitimacy of the secessionist movement. If recognition appears likely, Thomas concludes that these circumstances could produce a large number of motivated troops.

Second, by providing the financial backing. Unsurprisingly, Pantazopoulos ‘95, found that secessionist movements require a significant amount of money to buy mercenaries, <mark>weapons, and supplies.</mark> Engelbert ‘05, explains that since African economies are uniquely reliant on resource extraction and exportation, there is significant economic advantage to functioning within an internationally recognized state. Thus, regional elites only provide financial backing to secessionist movements if they believe that there is a likely path to recognition. Otherwise, the economic rewards of the sovereign national state outweigh those of the potential separatist state.

For these reasons, Zahran ‘24 concludes that AU recognition of Somaliland would be a slippery slope, entailing a domino effect of separatist movements across Africa, igniting conflicts, and leading to unimaginable chaos.

The scope of violence would be extreme. Esquerra ‘22 reports that in the last decade alone there have been upwards of twenty five fledgling secessionist movements across Africa that, given the opportunity by voting affirmative, could flare up. Further, this devastation would also extend to Somalia itself as Hagos ‘24 finds that recognition of Somaliland would fuel the secessionist movements in Puntland and Jubaland, <mark>two other breakaway</mark> regions of Somalia.
Specifically, the impact of secessionist movements is two-fold.

First, poverty. Reynaerts ‘21 finds that as a result of increased instability, secessionist movements lower per capita GDP by 24% over a ten year period. This is devastating as Matin ‘20 finds that every 1% decrease in GDP results in a 2% increase in extreme poverty.

Second, civil war. Taylor and Adunimay, conclude that secessionist movements are likely to escalate to civil war as they, inherently, threaten the state’s sovereignty. Indeed, Askar ‘18 confirmed in a holistic analysis that 87% of secessionist movements result in widespread conflict. For example, Esquerra tells how, in Nigeria, the Biafran secessionist movement escalated into a civil war that killed 3 million people, and injured and displaced countless more. AAS ‘21, highlights the international repercussions of civil war, which include the rise of transnational terrorism, emergence of infectious diseases, mass refugee flows, and regional instability.

To uphold African stability and prevent devastation, <mark>negate.</mark>
