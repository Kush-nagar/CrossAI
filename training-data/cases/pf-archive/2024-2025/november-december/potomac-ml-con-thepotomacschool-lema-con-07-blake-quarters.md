Introduction The world economy now finds itself at a critical juncture, facing a series of extraordinary
setbacks that have pushed down growth. Reigniting growth requires a unique combination of targeted
policies, robust international cooperation, and a renewed look at the global economy, with a particular
focus on Africa. Due to its burgeoning and youthful population, abundant natural resources, and a
strategic geographical location that can facilitate global trade, Africa can play a major role in—and should be
front and center of—any renewed efforts for revitalizing the global economy. A decade-long robust, inclusive, and
green growth in Africa will not only move hundreds of millions living in the continent out of poverty but will also
accelerate a global rebound and recovery. However, for this to materialize, Africa needs substantial
investments in its failing and inadequate physical and social infrastructure. With access to basic infrastructure, alongside efficient
institutions as well as its young population, massive natural endowments, and strategic location Africa can seize its economic potential and act

potential, through massive and focused investments in the continent’s human capital and its physical and social infrastructure. I. The global
slowdown: An overview The global economy has entered a prolonged period of slowdown. According to a 2023 World
Bank report, “Nearly all the forces that have powered growth and prosperity since the early 1990s have
weakened.” Even before the COVID-19 pandemic, an aging population, slowing productivity, and growing
barriers to trade and the free movement of people were slowing global growth. Then came the triple back-to-back
shocks: the pandemic, the Ukraine war, and persistently high inflation along with subsequent rapid rate hikes to fight
it. Those shocks, combined with preexisting structural factors, have introduced strong headwinds for the
global economy and its growth prospects in the next decade. If there is no significant policy intervention to
revitalize the global economy, the potential result is a lost decade—not only for certain countries or regions, but for the
entire world. According to the World Bank, the global potential gross domestic product (GDP) growth rate is expected to decline
to its lowest level in three decades, i.e., 2.2 percent per year between now and 2030. Figures 1A and 1B demonstrate that
the current global slowdown, which has become more pronounced following the pandemic, has been gradually developing over the past two
decades. The five-year moving average of the world’s real GDP growth rate has decreased from 3.7 percent in 2000 to 2.4 percent in 2021, as
depicted in Figure 1A. Similarly, growth has also decelerated in terms of GDP per capita, as shown in Figure 1B. The five-year moving average of
the world’s real GDP per capita growth rate has declined from 2.2 percent in 2000 to 1.4 percent in 2021. The global slowdown can be attributed
to various factors, many of which can be reversed through targeted and coordinated policies, including Aging labor forces and consumer
markets, especially in advanced economies, but also in many emerging markets and developing economies (EMDEs) (Figure 2A); declining global

policies that would contribute to improvements in labor productivity and mobility, increasing aggregate demand, and promoting sustainable and inclusive growth at the global level. Some of
these include the following. Increasing labor-force participation and facilitating the movement of labor: Globally, only 59 percent of the population above fifteen years of age is in the labor
force. This is mainly driven by the lower participation of females and youths in the labor force. As seen in Figure 3A, globally, the female labor-force participation rate (47 percent) is less than
two-thirds that for males (72 percent), with some regions—such as the Middle East and South Asia—having very large gender gaps in labor-force participation rates. Moreover, only 40 percent
of the world’s youth (those aged 15–24) is in the labor force, with the Middle East again lagging behind the rest of the world—especially in terms of the female youth in the region (Figure 3B).
Estimates show that increasing female and youth labor-force participation rates closer to the level of prime-age male workers (around 70 percent) could, on average, raise global potential
growth by 0.2 percentage points by 2030. One way to increase the world’s youth labor-force participation rate is to facilitate an easier movement of labor from regions of the world with a
growing young labor force to regions where the population and the labor force are aging. This would require governments in aging economies (with the support of international organizations
such as the World Bank, International Labor Organization (ILO), and United Nations (UN)), to reform immigration policies and promote certain types of visas to attract the needed skills for
various sectors. Enabling greater labor mobility would support the global economy for two main reasons. First, it will allow richer countries with aging populations to capitalize on the

benefit from the remittances. Expanding infrastructure investment: As seen in Figure 4, the current trends in infrastructure investments and needs will result in an $11.9-trillion shortage in

next seven years, $2.1 trillion of additional infrastructural investment is needed to achieve the SDGs. Boosting global investment to about 2–3 percent of the world’s GDP over this decade,
especially in the infrastructure sector, can increase potential growth by about 0.3 percentage points per year. With growing debt levels, the governments in many economies—especially

EMDEs—have been facing increasingly limited capacity to invest in physical and social infrastructure. Hence, there is a need for a global push to strengthen and optimize the frameworks of
private-public partnerships (PPPs) to foster increased engagement of the private sector in infrastructure initiatives. Moreover, quasi-state actors, such as sovereign wealth funds (SWFs) and

secure, yet moderate, return expectations typically associated with large-scale infrastructure projects. Re-globalization and reducing the costs of and barriers to trade: The ratio of world trade
to GDP grew from 25.3 percent in 1972 to 61 percent in 2008, an average annual rate of 2.5 percent (see Figure 5). With the onset of the 2008–2009 global financial crisis (GFC), world

trade-to-GDP ratio (as seen earlier in Figure 2E), while the decade preceding the GFC was characterized by the rise of global trade and globalization, the post-GFC decade can mainly be seen as

protectionism and geoeconomic fragmentation could get. However, reversing these trends, which have been in the making for more than a decade, will require a momentous effort by all
economies around the world—especially the members of the Group of Twenty (G20), among whom geoeconomic fragmentation has been rapidly rising. At the same time, enhancing the trade
regulatory and physical infrastructure is another area that needs to be addressed. This is where investment in physical infrastructure (discussed in some detail above) can help reverse declining

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** No card in the case performs explicit pre-written weighing (e.g., magnitude/probability/timeframe comparisons, "turns the case" framing, or impact calculus tags like "outweighs on scope"). The extinction card (Clare '23) describes great-power war severity but never comparatively weighs it against the Pro's likely impacts (e.g., US-China war over Taiwan, semiconductor collapse). A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Great-power war in Africa outweighs Taiwan Strait conflict — higher probability, larger nuclear arsenals involved, and irreversible extinction risk" and belong after the Elmi '24 card to anchor the terminal impact.

### Offensive spikes
Phillips '24 (WSJ) — "Recognition causes troop withdrawal and empowers Al-Shabaab" — executes an offensive spike by documenting that diplomatic recognition of Somaliland (a plausible Pro advocacy via "reduced support" signaling) triggers Ethiopian troop withdrawal from Somalia, directly empowering Al-Shabaab and destabilizing the Horn. This preempts Pro arguments that reduced US military support creates space for diplomatic solutions by showing the specific mechanism (recognition → withdrawal → terrorist resurgence) that turns Pro's ground offense.

### Defensive spikes
Kiage '24 (Nairobi News) — "Al-Shabaab activities in North Eastern on decline" — functions as a defensive spike by establishing a 60% attack reduction via community-security cooperation, preempting the Pro rebuttal that "terrorism is inevitable regardless of US policy" or "US military presence fuels radicalization." The card anchors the internal link that *current* US-backed counterterrorism architecture is working, so reducing support uniquely reverses progress.

### Evidence quality
**Mixed.** Phillips '24 (Pulitzer finalist, WSJ, Oct 2024) and Clare '23 (80k Hours, meta-analysis of forecasting models) are high-quality: strong author quals, recency, and institutional credibility. Odera '24 (Newsweek op-ed) and Al-Aqidi '24 (Arab News op-ed) are lower-tier: advocacy pieces without disclosed methodology/dataset, vulnerable to evidence-comparison tiebreaks. Kiage '24 (local Kenyan outlet citing HARN NGO) has opaque sourcing — "Horizon Analysts and Researchers Network" lacks verifiable dataset size. A card filling the quality gap would be a 2023-24 peer-reviewed study or US Africa Command report with quantified casualty/territory metrics, tagged "Empirical: US security assistance correlates with 40% Al-Shabaab territorial loss (AFRICOM 2023)," placed as the new C1-A subpoint.

### Collapse flexibility
The case offers **two distinct terminal pathways**: (1) Terrorism contagion → great-power war → extinction (Odera '24 → Phillips '24 → Elmi '24 → Clare '23) and (2) Great-power competition in Red Sea → trade disruption + extremist revival → global instability (Elmi '24 standalone). This allows collapse to either the Al-Shabaab-specific link (if Pro contests terrorism) or the broader Red Sea geopolitics link (if Pro concedes terrorism but disputes Al-Shabaab centrality). However, both routes converge on Elmi '24, creating a single point of failure if that card is neutralized.

### Warrant depth
**Variable.** Phillips '24 provides a detailed causal warrant: recognition → Ethiopian troop withdrawal → Al-Shabaab territorial regain → recruitment surge via nationalist narrative → Houthi/ISIS linkage → advanced weapons transfer. Clare '23 warrants extinction via historical near-misses, nuclear/bioweapon/autonomous tech proliferation, and forecaster probability estimates (10-50% war chance, 0.05-2% existential). Kiage '24 and Al-Aqidi '24 are claim-dense but warrant-thin: they assert attack reductions/escalations without explaining the *mechanism* (e.g., *why* community cooperation reduces attacks 60%, *why* Mohamud's offensive triggered escalation). Odera '24 is largely rhetorical assertion ("100,000% surge") without sourced methodology.

### Internal consistency
**Consistent framing.** All cards cohere around a single narrative: US military support in the Horn (Somalia counterterrorism, AU force sustainment) is the linchpin preventing Al-Shabaab resurgence and great-power proxy war. No contention undercuts another — Kiage '24's "decline" evidence supports the *status quo success* that Phillips '24 warns Pro disrupts. The extinction terminal (Clare '23) is uniformly applied to the great-power war scenario Elmi '24 describes. The only tension: Odera '24 frames Africa as "brink of chaos" while Kiage '24 shows "significant improvement" — but the case sequences them as "progress made, but fragile and reversible," which is strategically coherent.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 7/10** — The case builds a cohesive, dual-pathway impact architecture with strong offensive/defensive spikes and two high-caliber evidence anchors (Phillips, Clare), but suffers from uneven warrant depth in mid-tier cards and lacks explicit embedded weighing to win close impact-comparison debates.
