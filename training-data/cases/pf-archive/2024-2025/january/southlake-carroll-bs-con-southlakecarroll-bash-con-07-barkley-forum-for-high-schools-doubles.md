​​Interpretation: Debaters at a TOC bid tournament must disclose, on the OpenCaselist PF wiki 2024-2025 with their name and the school they attend, the taglines, full citations, and cut cards of all pieces of non-identity based evidence which they have read in a previous round 30 minutes before the round.

Standards:
1] Argument Quality — A world without disclosure rewards debaters for reading responses not because they are good, but because their opponents won’t know how to respond. Disclosure forces debaters to commit to quality.
2] Inclusion — two internal links:
a) Intel – Schools with big programs who bring more students and judges and are better connected will scout more rounds and have more flows; disclosure equalizes that intel disparity.
b) Research – Disclosure forces big, wealthy programs to put their prep on the wiki, that means everyone has access to the best, pay walled evidence, and also keeps smaller programs up-to-date on the meta.
3] Evidence Ethics — Disclosure allows debaters to check each other’s evidence before the round instead of needing to use prep to do so, meaning it’s easier to find and harder to run misconstrued evidence.
4] Prep Skew — We properly disclose rebuttals, which means they can prepare frontlines but we can’t. That means we need to use prep to prepare frontlines when they can beforehand, we are skewed out of the round.

Voters:
1] Fairness — Debate is a competition and fairness indicates your ability to determine the winner. Your ballot carries no value otherwise.
2] Education — It's the reason schools fund debate and the only portable skill of debate.

Drop the debater – two reasons
1] Norm-setting — Voting sets a precedent, wins and losses determine the direction of the activity.
2] Reciprocity — Your ballot can resolve the in-round skew we have been put under.

Competing Interpretations – Reasonability invites arbitrary judge intervention since we don’t know your bs meter.

No RVIs – three reasons.
A] Illogical — You shouldn’t win for being fair—it’s a litmus test for engaging in substance .
B] Norming — I can’t concede the counterinterp if I realize I’m wrong, forcing bad norms
C] Baiting — Abusive debaters will just bait theory and get really good at the RVI debate creating a chilling effect on theory which disincentivizes people from checking back abuse.

The harassment of government critics and the concentration of political power in the hands of a single clan also fuel concerns as to the degree of openness in Somaliland’s political system.¶ Somaliland unilaterally declared independence from Somalia in 1991. Even so, Mogadishu rejects Somaliland’s independence and no country has recognised it. At the same time, its reputation for orderly polls and relatively consensual politics has come under strain recently, and disputes among politicians caused a two-year delay of the presidential vote. ¶ In the end, Somaliland’s institutions and political establishment largely withstood the stress test. Other, non-Isaaq clans reside in Somaliland’s western and eastern regions. In the western region of Awdal, members of the Dir clan family have long complained of marginalisation by the Isaaq. Clare-23 [Stephen Clare, 06-XX-2023, [Former Research Fellow @ Forethought Foundation], "Great Power War", 80,000 Hours. https://80000hours.org/problem-profiles/great-power-conflict/]
How bad could a modern great power war be? Over time, two related factors have greatly increased humanity’s capacity to make war. 33 First, scientific progress has led to the invention of more powerful weapons and improved military efficiency. Second, economic growth has allowed states to build larger armies and arsenals. Since World War II, the world economy has grown by a factor of more than 10 in real terms; the number of nuclear weapons in the world has grown from basically none to more than 9,000, and we’ve invented drones, missiles, satellites, and advanced planes, ships, and submarines. Ghengis Khan’s conquests killed about 10% of the world, but this took place over the course of two decades. Today that proportion may be killed in a matter of hours. First, <mark>nuclear weapons could be used.</mark> Today there are around 10,000 nuclear warheads globally.34 At the peak of nuclear competition between the United States and the USSR, though, there were 64,000. If arms control agreements break down and competition resurges among two or even three great powers, nuclear arsenals could expand. In fact, China’s arsenal is very likely to grow — though by how much remains uncertain. Many of the nuclear weapons in the arsenals of the great powers today are at least 10 times more powerful than the atomic bombs used in World War II.35 Should these weapons be used, the consequences would be catastrophic. By any measure, such a war would be by far the most destructive, dangerous event in human history, <mark>with</mark> the potential to cause billions of deaths. The probability that it would, on its own, lead to humanity’s extinction or unrecoverable collapse, is contested. But there seems to be some possibility — whether through <mark>a famine caused by</mark> nuclear <mark>winter</mark>, or by reducing humanity’s resilience enough that something else, like a catastrophic pandemic, would be far more <mark>likely to reach</mark> <mark>extinction</mark>-levels (read more in our problem profile on nuclear war). Nuclear weapons are complemented and amplified by a variety of other modern military technologies, including improved missiles, planes, submarines, and satellites. They are also not the only military technology with the potential to cause a global catastrophe — bioweapons, too, have the potential to cause massive harm through accidents or unexpected effects. What’s more, humanity’s war-making capacity seems poised to further increase in the coming years due to technological advances and economic growth. Technological progress could make it cheaper and easier for more states to develop weapons of mass destruction. In some cases, political and economic barriers will remain significant. Nuclear weapons are very expensive to develop and there exists a strong international taboo against their proliferation. In other cases, though, the hurdles to developing extremely powerful weapons may prove lower. Improvements in biotechnology will probably make it cheaper to develop bioweapons. Such weapons may provide the deterrent effect of nuclear weapons at a much lower price. They also seem harder to monitor from abroad, making it more difficult to limit their proliferation. And they could spark a global biological catastrophe, like a major — possibly existentially catastrophic — pandemic.
