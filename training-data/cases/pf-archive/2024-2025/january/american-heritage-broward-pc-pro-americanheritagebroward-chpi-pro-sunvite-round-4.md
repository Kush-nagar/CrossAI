Combating Terrorism Center 19 - Michael Horton, November 2019, “How Somaliland Combats Al-Shabaab”
[https://ctc.westpoint.edu/somaliland-combats-al-shabaab/] Accessed 01/07/2025 ES

Analysts and officials have made frequent predictions about the decline and demise of al-Shabaab over the years.1 However, the <mark>al-Qa’ida-allied terrorist group </mark>has not only survived but<mark> continues to thrive</mark> in much of Somalia. On September 30, 2019, al-Shabaab launched attacks on two high-profile targets. It attacked a military base that hosts U.S. Special Forces soldiers at Balegdole in Lower Shabelle (southern Somalia), and then its operatives also targeted an Italian armored convoy carrying military advisers in Mogadishu. Both attacks failed. Al-Shabaab did not succeed in penetrating the outer defenses of the military base nor did they did injure or kill any of the military advisers.2 However, both <mark>attacks demonstrate al-Shabaab’s ability to target extremely well-guarded </mark>sites and <mark>individuals.</mark>
Since 2006, when al-Shabaab began to coalesce as an organization, <mark>billions of dollars have been spent by the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>and the international community to fight the group</mark>.3 The expenditure of vast sums of money and the eventual deployment of 22,000 soldiers by the African Union Mission in Somalia (AMISOM), <mark>however, have failed to defeat the organization.4</mark>
The key to al-Shabaab’s resiliency and its resurgence is two-fold. First, the failure of the Somali Federal Government to police and govern its territory consistently and effectively provides al-Shabaab with a high degree of operational freedom.5 Second, al-Shabaab’s ever-increasing organizational competence allows it to out-govern the government and other armed factions in many parts of Somalia. This competence extends well beyond its war-fighting capabilities. While important, these are not as critical as the group’s ability to operate what is in effect a shadow government that is often more effective, efficient, and predictable than the Somali Federal Government.6 It is the presence of this often efficient shadow government, far more than its armed operations or ideology that arguably allows al-Shabaab to maintain its influence across much of Somalia.

Notably, <mark>al-Shabaab</mark> has<mark> failed to establish a</mark>n enduring<mark> foothold in</mark> the unrecognized Republic of <mark>Somaliland</mark>. There, the government of <mark>Somaliland exerts </mark>consistent <mark>control over </mark>most of <mark>the territory that it claims.</mark> Al-Shabaab has not launched a large-scale attack in Somaliland since 2008 when it struck the presidential palace, the Ethiopian consulate, and UNDP offices in Hargeisa, Somaliland’s capital.7 The reasons for al-Shabaab’s failure, at least so far, to establish a foothold in Somaliland are due in large part to the Somaliland government’s ability to disrupt al-Shabaab’s attempts to insert itself and its operatives into communities where it could then establish its shadow government.b This ability is<mark> predicated on </mark>the<mark> Somaliland </mark>government<mark>’s fostering</mark> of a virtuous circle. This virtuous circle begins with effective, <mark>locally derived governance that supports broad community buy-in.8 This </mark>then<mark> provides the critical human intelligence (HUMINT)</mark> <mark>that allows </mark>the government<mark> to combat militancy</mark>. This capacity to combat militancy contributes to the security and governance that yields the broad support that<mark> allows the circle to perpetuate itself.</mark>

Accurate, rapid, and actionable<mark> HUMINT is </mark>Somaliland’s<mark> force multiplier in its war against </mark>al-Shabaab and other<mark> militant groups. Somaliland has </mark>no air force, no helicopters, and no drones, and its<mark> </mark>police and <mark>military struggle with minimal budgets that are not likely to increase.</mark>c<mark> Somaliland already spends an estimated 35 percent of its national budget on its security services, police, and military.</mark>21Somaliland’s army is small with an estimated total force of under 8,000 soldiers.22 The Somaliland police field a nationwide force of under 6,000 men and women.23<mark> Housed within the police force are the Special Police Unit (SPU), which guards foreign organizations and those who work for them, and the Rapid Response Units (RRU), which are dedicated counterterrorism forces. The Somaliland Police and the SPU have and continue to receive aid and training from the United Kingdom.</mark>24<mark> The Somaliland military and coast guard also receive some aid and training from the United Kingdom and the European Union.</mark>25

In 1992, in response to the civil war in Somalia, the United Nations imposed an open-ended arms embargo on the country that is still in place. Since the United Nations considers Somaliland to be a part of Somalia, it cannot import weapons or materiel that might be used for kinetic operations. This means that Somaliland’s army and police forces suffer from acute shortages of critical equipment. Communications equipment is in particularly short supply.dRecruits to Somaliland’s police and army are required to supply their own personal weapon or purchase one before enlisting.e This is partly due to budget constraints and the embargo and partly a means of removing small arms from general circulation.26 Despite these shortages and budget constraints, Somaliland’s police and army have proved themselves to be effective at combating al-Shabaab.
One of <mark>the key</mark>s<mark> to their success are the</mark> formal and informal <mark>intelligence gathering</mark> capabilities of Somaliland’s National Intelligence Service (NIS) and the army’s and police’s own intelligence officers.f Informal intelligence gathering networks are extant throughout Somaliland where they are nested<mark> within local communities.</mark> Formal networks led by officers from the NIS, army, and police exist alongside and in conjunction with the informal networks <mark>that act as early detection systems</mark> or tripwires.27

### Embedded weighing

The case presents Terrorism and Democracy as independent impacts without a pre-written framework for how to prioritize them against likely Con impacts (e.g., AU institutional integrity, Somali territorial integrity, or regional destabilization). A weighing card would be needed — e.g., a tag like "Terrorism outweighs on magnitude — Al-Shabaab threatens 15M+ across Horn; democracy impact is structural but slower" — placed at the top of C1 or as a standalone "Weighing" module before the first contention.

### Offensive spikes

No evidence tags reference opponent arguments. An offensive spike would look like: "Secession precedent empirically false — African Union recognized South Sudan 2011 without cascade; Somaliland unique colonial borders" (cite: African Affairs 2022), placed in C2 after the democracy evidence to inoculate against the "bad precedent" argument.

A defensive spike would be a card tagged "HUMINT durable — community buy-in survives non-recognition; 30-year track record" (cite: CTC Sentinel 2023), placed immediately after the Horton 2019 card in C1 to shield the terrorism solvency claim.

Recency is the only weakness (2019, accessed 2025), but the structural analysis remains relevant. **C2 — "The Brenthurst Foundation 21 - Greg Mills et al., June 2, 2021, 'Somaliland: The Power of Democracy'"** — High-profile authors (former heads of state, democracy practitioners), but the excerpt provided is largely claim-dense with thin warrants (no election data, no Freedom House scores, no institutional metrics). **JEC 24 Khan 2024** — Peer-reviewed journal, good recency, but the excerpt is a broad regional terrorism survey that does not specifically quantify Somaliland-area terrorism as "around a third of global terrorism" (the tagline claim is not supported by the quoted text, which cites Bandyopadhyay & Sandler 2023 for South Asia/ME/SSA aggregates). **Iraqi Open Access Journals 21** — Low-tier source (Basrah University journal), geographic/historical description only, no strategic analysis. **FPRI 21 Schwartz** — Strong think-tank source, but excerpt cuts off mid-sentence; no warrants on AU mandate or Somalia negotiation leverage provided in the doc.

No alternative solvency paths (e.g., recognition → direct security cooperation → intelligence sharing → CT; recognition → diaspora investment → police funding). The case is pinned to one link per contention; a Con team need only break one link to collapse the entire offense.

### Internal consistency
**PARTIAL EXECUTION / TENSION:** C1 argues Somaliland *currently* contains Al-Shabaab effectively *without* recognition (HUMINT works under status quo). C2 and the CFR 24 card argue recognition is *necessary* for loans/economic survival to sustain governance.
