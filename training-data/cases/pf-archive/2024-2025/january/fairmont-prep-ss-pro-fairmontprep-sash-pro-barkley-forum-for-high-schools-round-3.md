Omolo 25 [Valtino Omolo, Research Officer @ Institute for Security Studies & BA in Criminal Justice/Political Science @ United States International University Africa, 1-16-2025, Illegal yellowfin tuna fishing exposes gaps in Somalia’s maritime security, ENACT Africa, https://enactafrica.org/enact-observer/illegal-yellowfin-tuna-fishing-exposes-gaps-in-somalia-s-maritime-security, Willie T.] **IUU Fishing = Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing**
On 9 July 2024, a local Somalia news outlet reported that illegal tuna fishing by Chinese vessels off the Somalia coast had reached alarming levels. Yellowfin tuna, one of the world’s most commercially important fish species, is central to this concern. The high global demand for the yellowfin tuna and its significant economic value make it a target for criminal networks seeking to make profits from illegal fishing activities.¶ Since 2015, global commercial tuna catches have consistently surpassed sustainable catch limits to meet the high demands of global markets. In 2021, Planet Tracker warned that without urgent action, tuna stocks could collapse by 2026, with a minimum decline in adult biomass of at least 70% over 10 years. This indicates a potentially drastic reduction in the adult tuna population if sustainable measures are not adopted.¶Somalia’s waters are a major fishing ground for tuna, and it is estimated that illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing costs the country US$300 million annually.¶ In response, the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) Global Maritime Crime Programme, in partnership with Somalia’s Ministry of Fisheries and Blue Economy, convened a high-level stakeholder meeting in Mogadishu in December 2023. The meeting focused on the critical aspects of Somalia’s fisheries governance, enforcement and prosecution.¶ The Indian Ocean Tuna Commission (IOTC), an intergovernmental organisation responsible for managing tuna and tuna-like species in the Indian Ocean and adjacent seas, is currently falling short of its mandate. The challenge is that, while the IOTC can develop recommendations and conservation measures, it has no direct law enforcement mandate. Enforcement largely rests with individual member states, many of which lack the resources or capacity to combat IUU fishing effectively within their own jurisdictions.¶ The transnational nature of IUU fishing within the Somali context, often linked to organised criminal groups, exacerbates this problem by undermining regional efforts to conserve tuna stocks.¶ Since 2015, global commercial tuna catches have consistently surpassed sustainable catch limits¶ Driven in part by foreign criminal syndicates, IUU fishing has also created a complex, transnational challenge that the IOTC is ill-equipped to address with its current mandate. As noted by the European Parliamentary Research Service, IUU fishing can be linked to other transnational organised crimes, including money laundering, corruption and slavery on board fishing vessels.¶ In addition, IUU fishing activities have been frequently cited as a justification for acts of piracy by Somalia-based gangs, showcasing the direct link between IUU and organised criminality off the coast of Somalia.¶ Although some positive steps were taken at the IOTC meeting in May 2024, including a 15% cut in bigeye tuna catches and the adoption of electronic monitoring standards, significant gaps and challenges remain.¶ Within the IOTC, the European Union’s lobbying power and resistance to stricter regulations on fish-aggregating devices (harmful manmade structures that attract fish to fishing vessels) have created a rift with coastal states advocating for strict measures. Consequently, overfishing continues, with fleets from China and European countries reportedly the primary culprits.¶ In December 2018, former Somali fisheries minister Abdillahi Bidhan inked a deal allowing Chinese companies to fish within 24 nautical miles of Somalia’s shores at a cost of US$1 million. According to the Ministry of Fisheries and Blue Economy, this agreement would be renewed based on annual independent stock assessments – and it is still currently active.¶ However, there is a lack of transparency in such deals, says Mohamud Nur Hasan, a member of the Parliamentary Subcommittee on Fisheries and Natural Resources. The deal, with its vague conditions, is an example of how the Somali government struggles to manage its fishing industry transparently.¶ It is estimated that illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing costs Somalia US$300 million annually¶ In this context, a 2019 report revealed that the Liao Dong Yu fleet (a Chinese foreign vessel) was not operating within the 2018 stipulated law. Instead, the fleet had obtained fishing licences from Puntland authorities through questionable means. This was reportedly facilitated by businessman and senator Dahir Ayande, a close ally of Puntland President Said Abdullahi Deni, pointing to the influence of political associations in undermining the governance of Somalia’s fishing industry.¶ In the meantime, Chinese vessels continue to be involved in illegal tuna fishing off the Somalia coastline, using prohibited and destructive methods of fishing. These include using large purse seines and longline vessels to catch immense volumes of tuna, bottom trawling, selective equipment that can capture certain fish while excluding others, and even dynamite fishing – using explosives to kill and collect fish. These unsustainable practices are not only depleting the fish stocks but are negatively affecting Somalia’s maritime ecosystem, further threatening local fisher’s livelihoods.

Hargeisa, Somaliland, February 13, 2017 – In a letter to the UN Secretary-General signed by President Sillanyo on Saturday, February 11, 2017, the Government of Somaliland asserts its sovereign rights under international law and announces a new 24-nautical mile Contiguous Zone in addition to the 12-mile Territorial Sea and a 200-mile Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ).¶ The new Contiguous Zone and #EEZ will enable Somaliland to better police maritime territory and combat crime, including in relation to customs, immigration, and people and arms trafficking; protect natural resources, such as fisheries and oil and gas deposits belonging to the people of Somaliland; and support international efforts to promote peace and security in the Gulf of Aden.¶ The maritime zones will be carried into law in accordance with the #Somaliland Constitution. In due course, the Government will submit the geographical coordinates and a chart delineating the zones to the #UN Secretary-General. The Government will also communicate its decision to neighboring states and other interested parties through diplomatic channels.¶ Today’s Declaration makes it clear that Somaliland repudiates Somalia’s 2014 EEZ proclamation that purports to lay claim to Somaliland’s waters and will exercise control over its sovereign territory to the fullest extent possible. This follows the decision by the Somaliland people in 1991 to exercise their inalienable right of self-determination, withdraw from their failed union with Somalia and restore independence won from Britain in 1960.¶ The 2001 Somaliland Constitution lays claim to the islands, the Territorial Sea, and the continental shelf. Under the 1982 UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), a state is entitled to declare a series of maritime zones, including a 12-mile Territorial Sea, a 24-mile Contiguous Zone, a 200-mile Exclusive Economic Zone, and the continental shelf beyond it, subject to delimitation through negotiation with neighboring states. Different legal regimes apply to each zone.

Idaan 24 [Gulaid Yusuf Idaan, Multiple master’s degrees in international law, diplomacy, and international relations, 2024, THE SOMALILAND COASTAL GUARD SAFEGUARDING MARITIME SECURITY IN THE HORN OF AFRICA, Academia.edu, https://www.academia.edu/116314788/THE_SOMALILAND_COASTAL_GUARD_SAFEGUARDING_MARITIME_SECURITY_IN_THE_HORN_OF_AFRICA, Willie T.] **SCG = Somaliland Coast Guard**
Main duties of the SCG include protecting Somaliland's territorial waters from piracy, illegal fishing, and other maritime crimes, conducting search and rescue operations in cases of distress at sea, facilitating maritime trade by ensuring the safe passage of vessels through Somaliland's territorial waters, and collecting intelligence on maritime threats and sharing it with other law enforcement agencies. Equipped with modern patrol boats, radar systems, and other equipment, the force is prepared to carry out its duties effectively. ¶ SCG'S BASES ¶ The SCG has strategically positioned several bases along Somaliland's coastline to enhance its operational effectiveness. Among these, the Berbera Base stands as the headquarters for the Coast Guard, facilitating command and control operations. Additionally, the Hargeisa Base serves as a crucial training center, ensuring the continuous development of Coast Guard personnel. ¶ Complementing these primary facilities are strategically located bases such as the Lughaya Base, Zeila Base, and Maydh Base. These bases play pivotal roles in protecting Somaliland's borders with Djibouti, Ethiopia, and Puntland, respectively, safeguarding against potential threats from various directions. Each base is equipped with essential infrastructure and resources to support the SCG's missions, including berthing facilities, maintenance workshops, and communication systems. By strategically positioning these bases, the SCG can effectively monitor and respond to maritime security challenges across Somaliland's extensive coastline, contributing to the overall safety and security of the region. ¶ DEVELOPMENT OF SCG ¶ The development of the Somaliland Coast Guard (SCG) has been marked by the prioritization of personnel training and capacity-building, with support from various international partners such as the European Union, the United States, and the United Kingdom. Training efforts encompass a spectrum of vital skills including navigation, communication, maritime law enforcement, and search and rescue. Complementing these efforts, the SCG has also undergone equipment and vessel acquisition, including the reception of two 35-meter patrol boats from the United States in 2020, outfitted with cutting-edge communication and navigation systems alongside requisite weaponry for effective maritime law enforcement. ¶ Recently, the SCG has taken significant strides by joining the IORIS community, facilitated by training sessions provided by CRIMARIO between November 4th and 9th. A total of seventeen officials undertook Foundation and Advanced courses, equipping them with the necessary skills to utilize the IORIS Platform for information exchange, document sharing, and leveraging mapping features and AIS data. Moreover, advanced functionalities such as data importing and layered image overlays were also covered, enhancing the SCG's ability to create a comprehensive maritime situational awareness. The significance of this development was underscored by Lt Col. Khadar M. Isse, emphasizing the potential of IORIS in fostering regional cooperation and enhancing daily operational effectiveness. ¶ Admiral Ahmad Hure Harriye, representing the Somaliland Coast Guard, formally solidified their participation in the IORIS community by signing the partnership agreement, recognizing the imperative of information sharing and cooperation in the Gulf of Aden to counter criminal and terrorist activities. The commitment to collaboration was echoed by Admiral Harriye, expressing gratitude to CRIMARIO for facilitating access to the Indian Ocean HUB and emphasizing the collective strength that comes from unified efforts. CRIMARIO reaffirmed its ongoing support for the SCG, ensuring their readiness to navigate future challenges effectively. In addition to training and capacity-building, the development of the SCG has also involved the acquisition of new equipment and vessels. The force has received several new patrol boats, including two 35-meter patrol boats donated by the United States in 2020. These vessels are equipped with advanced communication and navigation systems, as well as weapons and other equipment necessary for maritime law enforcement. ¶ SUCCESS OF SCG ¶ The success achieved by the Somaliland Coastal Guard (SCG) in its relentless efforts to combat piracy and illegal fishing has significantly contributed to enhancing economic prospects for local communities while fostering stability within the region. Through the vigilant safeguarding of Somaliland's territorial waters, the SCG has effectively empowered the nation to actively engage in regional trade activities and capitalize on its strategic positioning along the Gulf of Aden. In assessing the multifaceted impacts of the SCG's success, it becomes evident that its efforts extend far beyond mere law enforcement at sea. Rather, the SCG serves as a catalyst for broader socio-economic development and regional stability, playing a pivotal role in shaping the trajectory of Somaliland's maritime domain. This essay delves into the myriad dimensions of the SCG's success, elucidating its implications for local communities, regional dynamics, and the broader maritime security landscape. ¶ At the heart of the SCG's success lies its unwavering commitment to curbing piracy and illegal fishing activities within So maliland's territorial waters. Piracy, once rampant in the region, posed a grave threat to maritime commerce and security, undermining the stability of coastal communities and impeding economic progress. Illegal fishing, likewise, depleted marine resources, jeopardizing the livelihoods of local fishermen and exacerbating tensions over territorial sovereignty. Against this backdrop, the SCG emerged as a formidable force, employing advanced surveillance technologies, enhanced maritime capabilities, and robust interagency cooperation to combat illicit maritime activities effectively. ¶ The tangible benefits of the SCG's success are palpable across various facets of Somaliland's socio-economic landscape. By curbing piracy and illegal fishing, the SCG has engendered a safer maritime environment, thereby bolstering investor confidence and facilitating increased maritime trade. Local fishermen, once beleaguered by the scourge of illegal fishing, now enjoy greater access to marine resources, leading to improved livelihoods and enhanced food security. Moreover, the SCG's proactive stance against maritime crime has fostered a sense of security and stability within coastal communities, laying the groundwork for sustainable development and social cohesion.

Glaser 23 [Sarah Glaser, Senior Director of Oceans Futures @ WWFUS, 11-18-2023, Spotlight: Horn of Africa, WWF Oceans Futures Project, https://oceansfutures.org/horn-of-africa#GOVERNANCE%20SOLUTIONS, Willie T.] **author and date found in methodology section**
The Horn of Africa has some of the most productive fishing grounds in the world, including waters within the Somali Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ). Fishing fleets travel from across the globe to access the near-shore reef ecosystems and highly migratory species such as tropical tuna, mackerels, and billfish that travel along the Somali Current. However, the richness of these waters has not historically supported domestic fisheries.¶ With the longest coastline in mainland Africa, the waters of the Somali EEZ are larger than its land territory. This vastness makes it incredibly difficult to monitor and patrol. The prolonged civil war (1991–2012) meant investment in maritime security suffered, and weak law enforcement capacity, ineffective government and criminal prosecution systems, and economic insecurity exacerbated the issue. During the mid-2000s, the Horn of Africa became synonymous with piracy. The impact of piracy was significant, in part, because of Somalia’s location at the Bab el-Mandeb Strait, one of the world’s most geostrategic shipping chokepoints.¶ While conflict in Somalia’s waters has been significant, there has also been tremendous progress over the past decade. Piracy was nearly eliminated, and the government has increased maritime domain awareness. Our hotspot analysis shows Somalia continues to rank high in the risk of fisheries conflict, but greater information sharing and integration with the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission could help change that course.¶ Fisheries conflict in the Somali region emerged from unmanaged competition over fish stocks, exacerbated by institutional instability. The presence of foreign fishers, military intervention in response to piracy, and territorial disputes made the Horn of Africa a textbook example of the consequences of decades of unmitigated illegal fishing.¶ Foreign Fishing¶ An estimated $450 million is lost to illegal, unreported, and unregulated (IUU) fishing in the Somali EEZ each year. Distant water fishing fleets follow commercially valuable fish stocks around the Western Indian Ocean. Somalia’s limited capacity to effectively license foreign vessels, monitor and patrol their EEZ, and enforce existing regulations means foreign vessels are not deterred from fishing illegally.¶ In the 1990s, following the collapse of the federal government, Somali coastal communities took matters into their own hands. Certain Somali opposition groups, such as the Somali Salvation Democratic Front (SSDF), began “arresting” vessels and demanding they purchase unofficial fishing licenses. By the mid-1990s, Somali fishers were arming themselves to defend their fish stocks from the illegal foreign vessels. In 1997, Somalis in four armed speedboats forced a foreign fishing vessel with a crew of 34 foreign nationals to the coastal village of Garacad. The crew was accused of illegal fishing and released four months later, after paying a fine of $200,000.¶ Eventually, organized pirate groups took over this informal licensing scheme, increasingly controlled by warlords demanding ransom for crew. Much of the financial backing for this pivot in piracy was from prominent businesspeople and politicians who used “licensing foreign vessels” for extortion. Warlords fought over fishing grounds, targeting fishing vessels inside disputed areas. Three Taiwanese fishing vessels, Chung Yi 218, Cheng Ching Feng, and Hsin Lien Feng 36, were hijacked in 2005 near Kismayo in southern Somalia. The vessels were captured despite paying application fees to the local authorities (the local warlord). Their hijackers, ex-members of the Juba Valley Alliance militia, alleged the vessels were fishing illegally.¶ This hijack-for-ransom strategy transformed piracy into an organized “industry.” Attacks expanded against local fishing vessels, yachts, and cargo ships. Somali pirates seized a Yemeni fishing boat in May 2008, using the vessel as a base and the Yemeni crew as decoys. A Dutch cargo ship delivering drilling platform components was hijacked at the same time in the Gulf of Aden.¶ Transit across the geostrategic Bab el-Mandeb Strait became more treacherous, and the threat of piracy jeopardized global commerce. Naval warship coalitions entered the region to deter piracy, but Somali and Yemeni fishers report the threat they faced from pirates was magnified by the arrival of foreign warships. However, the threat of piracy did temporarily deter foreign fishing. An unintended consequence of warships quelling the piracy threat was a return of distant-water foreign fleets. In 2015, 86 percent of fishers reported seeing foreign fishing vessels near the Somali coast. By the 2019–2020 fishing season, at least 200 Iranian-flagged fishing vessels were fishing illegally within the Somali EEZ.¶ Coastal Somali communities are frustrated that foreign fleets have returned. They are unable to protect their resources and see no intervention from the federal government or international community. Public trust in governance institutions is eroding and there are threats of another wave of piracy on the horizon.¶ Foreign Militaries¶ The piracy problem grew beyond the capability of the fledgling federal government; the Somali government’s request for assistance brought foreign naval warship coalitions into the region. Their mission was to protect cargo vessels passing through the area and interrupt piracy attempts. For example, a British naval warship rescued a hijacked Yemeni vessel that had been sailing to Somalia to sell oil and fish. However, artisanal fishers throughout the region protested foreign warships that denied locals access to fishing grounds, sabotaged fishing nets, and confused their vessels for pirates’. In 2009, approximately 75 nm off the coast of Yemen, an Indian naval vessel approached a Yemeni dhow, mistaking it for Somali pirates. The fishers were forced to jump into the sea and tread water for two hours. All of their fish spoiled. The following year, seven Yemeni fishing boats were fired upon by a Russian helicopter and forced to board the Russian navy destroyer RFN Marshal Shaposhnikov. The fishers were robbed of their boats, money, IDs, GPS units, and clothes. The fishers were then put on a single boat and sent back. The Russian navy shot at their boat as they sailed away. Yemeni fishers claimed that the abuse cost them about 30 million Yemeni rial in damage from searches and seizures and that the forces in the region are often as dangerous as the pirates themselves.¶ Targeted attacks against Yemeni fishers exponentially increased during the Yemeni Civil War between the Yemeni government, backed by a Saudi-led coalition, and the Houthi movement. Both sides of the conflict targeted fishing vessels as part of their offensive strategy. The Iran-backed Houthi militias destroyed fleets of fishing boats, eliminating the sole source of income for coastal residents. The Saudi-led coalition used starvation as a weapon of war by deliberately targeting the infrastructure of Yemen’s food production and distribution. While both sides targeted fishing vessels, the Saudi-led coalition was significantly more detrimental. In May 2018, a Saudi-led coalition launched 27 airstrikes over 24 hours. Among the series of strikes, in Hudaydah province, a fisher was killed, one was injured, and three went missing following aggression from warplanes that targeted their boat. In July of that year, there were two other reported incidents of Saudi warplanes targeting fishing boats in Al-Hawak district, killing eight fishers and injuring 28. By 2019, Yemeni fishers were being abducted and tortured. The Tihami Elite, special forces affiliated with the Saudi-led coalition, abducted 17 Yemeni fishers. They were held at a prison for three days, during which they were subjected to beatings and electric shocks and had their possessions confiscated. Many fishers stopped practicing their livelihoods for fear of being targeted by fighter jets and gunships.¶ Disputed Fishing Grounds¶ Yemeni and Somali fishers have a history of informal trading agreements across the Gulf of Aden. Yemenis would trade fuel and ice (subsidized in Yemen) with Somalis for fish or access to fishing grounds. Yemenis and Somalis both traveled the waters freely. As fish stocks began to decrease, Somali fishers became more serious about enforcing the sovereignty of their waters. Distant water fishing fleets were responsible for much of the stock decline, using high efficiency gear like commercial trawlers and purse seines, but the artisanal Yemeni fishers became easy scapegoats. In 2006, The Somaliland Coast Guard arrested 50 Yemeni fishers and seized their eight boats. Somaliland announced the event as part of a campaign to enforce territorial sovereignty, while Yemen reported the event as a pirate attack. Similar events took place every year through 2010, putting the Yemeni fishers at risk of targeted attacks by pirates, warships, and Somali vessels.¶ Yemen also battles for access on its western coast. The Hanish Islands in the Southern Red Sea are a region of high conflict and contested control between Yemen and Eritrea. The islands provide strategic military location and the waters are rich in fisheries. Eritrea and Yemen disputed the islands in 1995 and an international court awarded them to Yemen in 1998. However, the two countries have disagreed on the interpretations of the rulings regarding fishing rights in the area. During February, March, and April 2013, Eritrean authorities seized over 100 Yemeni fishing boats with about 700 Yemeni fishers on board. Like Somali efforts in the Gulf of Aden, this was part of a campaign to enforce their interpreted territorial sovereignty. The tension escalated to conflict again in 2020 when Yemeni fishers were harassed by the Eritrean Navy. The fishers were beaten and their boats were confiscated. The following month, 15 armed boats from Eritrea's navy seized 120 Yemeni fishers in the Hanish islands. The Yemeni Coast Guard responded by arresting eight members of the Eritrean navy that had allegedly seized Yemeni fishing vessels. The men were released in exchange for 55 Yemeni fishers.¶ The Horn of Africa has incredibly high biodiversity of fishes, from the demersals associated with reefs to pelagics that benefit from the high flow of intense nutrient upwelling, called the Great Whirl. The global demand for high quality tuna drives intense targeting in the region by longline and purse seine vessels from Taiwan, Japan, Spain, India, the Maldives, Iran, China, Oman, Pakistan, Yemen, the Seychelles, and France (among others). Tropical tuna migrate long distances. Their ranges cross many countries’ EEZs and international cooperation between coastal countries and distant-water fishing nations is vital. The Indian Ocean Tuna Commission (IOTC) is a voluntary Regional Fisheries Management Organization (RFMO) that monitors the stock status of tuna and tuna-like species, develops regulations for the region, and promotes cooperation among regional members. Somalia has been a member since 2014.

Kassim Abdullahi Jimale a , Richard Wall a b , Domenico Otranto, "Ticks and tick-borne pathogens of domestic animals in Somalia and neighbouring regions of Ethiopia and Kenya," Volume 243, July 2023, 106944// Shah https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0001706X23001316

Ticks and tick-borne pathogens (TBPs) of domestic animals in Somalia and neighbouring regions of Ethiopia and Kenya are reviewed to identify knowledge gaps in these regions, where unrestricted livestock movements across borders are common. Major scientific databases, such as PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, CABI, and Google Scholar were searched, to retrieve articles based on papers published between 1960 and March 2023. Thirty-one tick species representing six genera (Rhipicephalus, Hyalomma, Amblyomma, Haemaphysalis, Ornithodoros and Argas) were reported to infest domestic animals, mainly livestock. Overall, the most represented species were Rhipicephalus pulchellus (up to 60% of specimens identified), followed by Hyalomma dromedarii (up to 57%), Hyalomma truncatum (up to 57%), Amblyomma lepidum (up to 21%), Amblyomma variegatum (up to 21%) and Amblyomma gemma (up to 19%), with morphological characterization being the principal method of tick identification. In addition, 18 TBPs, including zoonotic pathogens (e.g., Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever virus), were detected, with Babesia spp., Theileria spp., and Rickettsia spp. being the most commonly reported. Received: 23/06/15; accepted 03/01/17 Introduction Reliable and timely health information is critical input for health systems to develop and perform at high level of effectiveness, efficiency and quality. For any country, well-processed, synthesized and presented health information is profoundly needed for making informed evidence-based decisions to improve population health. A comprehensive integrated system that provides such information is known as Health information Systems (HIS) (1), which aims to support the decision-making process with relevant evidence, and thus ultimately contributes to improve health status. More importantly, information could be described as “the guidance of health systems”, since it is good information and evidence that leads to improved governance and stewardship; provision of quality health care services; and more efficient management of resources (money, manpower, drugs, supplies and technology) (2,3). Health information systems generate data from various sources including health facility data, administrative records, household surveys, censuses, vital registration, national health accounts and health research. There is no standard formula of data sources that should be applicable in every setting. As a principle, it is advisable to define – at the initial phase of setting up HIS – what data should be collected as “core health indicators” with their appropriate and cost-effective system of data collection and reporting. This step has not been always straightforward in many countries (4). For example, the HIS in low-income countries has long been under-developed and poorly performing (5). Similarly, in resources-constrained settings, it has been widely recognized that poor availability and quality of information hinders formulation of health policies and plans, design and implementation of public health programmes, or allocation of resources. A review made of HIS implementation has shown challenges: 1) resource and infrastructure limitations; 2) poor information and communications technology (ICT) for health; 3) absence of enforced legal framework for information management; 4) limited use of common interoperability standards; 5) lack of an appropriately trained workforce; and 6) fragmented HIS sub-systems (6). Other similar research papers revealed the following: poor data quality; limited use of available information, weaknesses in data analyzing capacity, and poor HIS management practices (7,8). In striving to improve HIS, most countries in the Region have comprehensively reviewed and assessed their HIS with support of the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Health Metric Network (HMN).
