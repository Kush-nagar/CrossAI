East <mark>Africa</mark> has <mark>experienced</mark> more frequent <mark>droughts</mark> since 2005, occurring <mark>every three years</mark> (Haile et al., Citation2019). Somaliland suffered 11 severe droughts from 1964 to 2017 (Abdulkadir, Citation2017; FAO, Citation2021b). The most devastating events occurred in 1973–1974, 1984, 1991, 2010/2011, and 2016/2017, affecting the Sanaag, Togdheer, and Sool Regions for prolonged periods. These droughts caused damage and losses that exceeded USD 874 million (Pape and Wollburg Citation2019). <mark>Drought is</mark> also <mark>the main cause of livestock deaths</mark> (<mark>up to 60%</mark>) <mark>in Somaliland</mark>, severely affecting pastoralists’ livelihoods (The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification [IPC], Citation2021). According to the United Nations and UNICEF, drought emergencies in Somaliland and Somalia have dire consequences for millions of people. They displaced 771,400 people searching for water, food, and pasture and put more than 1.4 million children at risk of acute malnutrition. The drought impacted 6.1 million Somalis in total (UN News, Citation2022; UNICEF, Citation2022). More than 1.15 million people left their homes between January 2016 and May 2018, often when they were already weak and hungry from the drought (Fanning, Citation2018).¶ Floods are another hazard affecting the livelihoods of Somaliland people. The country has faced more than 30 weather-related disasters since 1990, including 12 droughts and 19 floods (World Bank, Citation2022). Cyclone Sagar in 2018 was one of the worst floods, killing 50 people and affecting 168,000 more in north-western Somaliland. The cyclone brought a year’s worth of rain, destroying most livestock, water systems, health facilities, and schools in some areas (UNICEF, Citation2018). Floods also pose a health risk by contaminating drinking water and causing diarrhea, which was among the top five causes of death in Ethiopia, Eritrea, Somaliland, Somalia, Burundi, Djibouti, Uganda, and South Sudan, across all age groups in 2019 (Carlton et al., Citation2016). Diarrheal diseases are likely to increase with increasing temperature, as common bacterial pathogens multiply faster and last longer at higher temperatures. Abaya et al. (Citation2011) found that climate variability worsened existing health problems in the Somali region of Ethiopia, where most people lack sanitation and safe water.¶ Despite their stability, Somaliland’s financial assets are affected by climate-related events. The FAO (Citation2021b) reported that households faced multiple shocks, such as climate change, reduced income, high food prices, loss of employment, illness, and insecurity, based on The Resilience Index Measurement and Analysis (RIMA) study in Somaliland. According to the International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW, Citation2022), many animals are sold desperately in the country. Therefore, climate change and variability pose a serious challenge for developing countries such as Somaliland, where most people have a low adaptive capacity to cope with climate change in their livelihoods (Asfaw et al., Citation2021). These statistics indicate that climate change has had a devastating impact on the livelihoods of the people of Somaliland, especially those who depend on livestock and crop production.¶ In conclusion, Somaliland’s livelihoods require urgent action from the government and international organizations to cope with climate variability. They should collaborate to help vulnerable communities access vital resources and build their resilience to climate change. Resilience is the ability to prevent, anticipate, absorb, accommodate, or recover from disasters and crises in a timely, efficient, and sustainable manner (FAO, Citation2021a). It involves protecting, restoring, and improving livelihood systems that face threats to agriculture, nutrition, food security, and food safety (FAO, Citation2020c). Pastoral communities can improve early warning systems, animal health, livestock restocking, and natural resource management. Agropastoral households promote income diversification and livelihood strategies through training, extension services, market access, and non-farming activities. These measures can reduce poverty by improving food security and nutrition, increasing income levels, sustaining crop production, and mitigating climatic vulnerability (Adem et al., Citation2018; Habib et al., Citation2023).¶ 3.2. Impact of climate variability on agriculture activities in Somaliland¶ Most people in Somaliland depend on agriculture and livestock for their income and livelihood; however, these sectors are highly vulnerable to climatic shocks and stresses. Agriculture contributes approximately 25% of the gross domestic product (GDP) and is the second most important economic activity after livestock (Ministry of National Planning and Development [MoNPD], Citation2011). The livestock sector accounts for 70% of the population, generating 30% of the GDP and 85% of foreign export earnings (MoNPD, Citation2012, Citation2017). Gebremichael (Citation2012) conducted a study on the resilience of households’ livelihoods to hazards in Somaliland. The study found that in the past five and ten years, there has been a trend of failure of the big (Gu) and small (Dyer) rains, with a resulting shortage of water and pasture, the spread of livestock diseases, flooding, intensification of soil erosion, crop failures, and resource base conflicts. These hazards have caused the loss of agriculture and livestock assets, which are the main sources of income and food for people.¶ Sharmake et al. (Citation2022) examined trends in rainfall and temperature over the last three decades in western Somaliland, a country that relies on rain-fed agriculture. They found that rainfall decreased and temperatures increased, adversely affecting rainfed agriculture and leading to crop failure, food insecurity, and migration. Several studies have also documented the significant impacts of climate change and variability on the agriculture and livestock sectors in Somaliland, as well as on people’s way of life and cultural identity (Abdi et al., Citation2022; Thalheimer et al. Citation2023; Warsame et al., Citation2021, Citation2022; Warsame & Abdi, Citation2022). Drought is the most severe event, accounting for up to 80% of the agricultural damage and loss. Agriculture is the main source of livelihood for approximately 450 million people, or 48% of Africa’s population, who live in extreme poverty, especially in rural areas (World Bank, Citation2015). The African Union (AU) comes in for a lot of criticism. Most recently this is from within its own ranks. The AU Commission chairperson, Moussa Faki Mahamat, set out his frustrations after an AU summit in February 2024. The commission is the executive organ which runs the AU’s daily activities. This is based on more than 15 years of researching its political and legal development. The AU was formed in 2002 to replace the Organisation of African Unity (OAU). Its institutions include the AU Commission, the Pan-African Parliament and the African Court of Human and Peoples’ Rights, but the real power lies in the hands of its assembly, composed of heads of state and government. And the AU Commission cannot compel member states to comply with AU rules. Most member states refuse to comply with the decisions of the human rights court. In 2003 the Drought Monitoring Centre-Nairobi (DCMN) was adopted as a specialised IGAD institution, and advanced into the IGAD Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (ICPAC). In 2022 IGAD set up a Climate Security Coordination Mechanism, to be integrated within ICPAC.¶ Exemplifying IGAD’s responsiveness to specific regional concerns, the organisation <mark>has shown</mark> significant <mark>initiative in addressing</mark> the particular needs of <mark>pastoralists</mark>, a vital yet marginalised livelihood in the Horn and East Africa, especially vulnerable to the impacts of climate change. In response to drought induced challenges affecting pastoral livelihoods and mobility patterns, IGAD established the IGAD Centre for Pastoral Areas and Livestock Development (ICPALD) in 2011. This initiative, aligned with the recognition of pastoralism’s importance among IGAD member states, aims to <mark>enhance rangeland management</mark> in crossborder areas of <mark>Ethiopia, South Sudan</mark>, Sudan <mark>and Uganda</mark>; an approach that contrasts donor biases favouring crop production (Interview, Welthungerhilfe Area Manager in Somaliland, August 2023) and policies aiming at restricting migration. In 2021, IGAD Council of Ministers adopted the IGAD Protocol on Transhumance, that focuses on supporting and regulating safe cross-border mobility and use of common rangelands by pastoralists. The protocol represents a proactive approach to climate change adaptation linked with migration and mobility, specifically addressing the interrelated dynamics of climate change, livelihood insecurity and conflict dynamics (in particular conflicts related to land access). The focus is on demonstrating how <mark>providing</mark> adequate <mark>support for pastoralists</mark> and related environmental resource management – in this case <mark>land</mark>, <mark>pasture and water</mark> – can contribute to maintaining peace (Interview, IGAD official, August 2023). Additionally, IGAD’s 2023 Sustainable Ecosystem Management (SEM) project3 aligns with similar aims of the protocol, addressing critical pastoral challenges in a region facing a drought crisis. These efforts and frameworks have been strengthened through support and collaboration between IGAD and international organisations, such as the UN, the European Union (EU) and the International Organization for Migration, as well as through bilateral assistance.
