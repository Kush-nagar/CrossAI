In 2006, U.S. Customs and Border Protection decided to outdo the border walls of the past and build a
great barrier of data — a system of ground sensors, remote-control cameras, and radars that transmit
real-time data to border agents — along the U.S.–Mexican border. But as of late 2008, only 28 miles of
the “virtual” fence, known officially as the Secure Border Initiative Network, or SBInet, are up and
running. The finished job is expected to run 6,000 miles along the northern and southern borders of the
United States at a cost of $30 billion. The Department of Homeland Security (DHS) awarded the initial $2
billion contract for the project to Boeing, which promised to have large sections of the fence up and

running by 2008. The first phase ran six months late and used commercially-available technology that was
replaced almost immediately with fancier gadgets. In theory, border agents can use the information to
intercept illegal transit, but after taking the pilot project for a test-run, border agents told the
Government Accountability Office (GAO) that it was “not an optimal system” for their needs. From the
program’s inception, the GAO warned about the vagueness of the requirements set out in the contracting
order, a problem that plagued two predecessors, the Integrated Surveillance Intelligence System and
America’s Shield Initiative. Now land-management issues have delayed the project. A September 2008
agreement with the Department of the Interior over DHS’s use of government land should have come
through last July, but DHS failed to file the necessary paperwork. The department also will have to grab private property
from some border land owners by eminent domain. Testifying before Congress in September, Randolph C. Hite, the GAO’s director of
information technology architecture and systems, put the problem bluntly: “Important aspects of SBInet remain ambiguous
and in a continued state of flux, making it unclear and uncertain what technology capabilities will be
delivered and when, where, and how they will be delivered.”

As was the case in the Cold War, the most plausible scenario for U.S. and Russian military forces to engage in large-scale combat is in Europe. It
is worth considering first how even a very limited attack or incident could set both sides on a slippery slope to rapid escalation. If armed conflict
looks at all likely, both sides would have overwhelming incentives to go early with offensive cyber and counter-space capabilities to negate the
other side’s military capabilities or advantages. If these early cyber and space attacks succeed, it could result in huge military and coercive
advantage for the attacker – with few or even no direct casualties. It may appear very unlikely that the attacked side would retaliate strongly in
response to some damaged computers and some malfunctioning satellites in outer space.Moreover, if the attacks fail to have the
desired effect, the other side may not even notice. Large-scale cyber and space attacks – preferably before a kinetic
conflict even starts – therefore may appear a low-risk, high-payoff move for both sides. LIMITED CYBER AND SPACE
ATTACKS WITH CASCADING EFFECTS ON CIVIL SOCIETY With each side having emplaced cyber implants to disrupt or
destroy the other side’s military systems and critical infrastructure – including war-supporting
infrastructure as well as purely civilian infrastructure, a small spark in cyberspace could rapidly
escalate. The spark could come from an intentional cyber attack that had unintended cascading effects,
or from proxies or false flag attacks. Thus, cyber and space attacks intended to be highly discriminative against military targets
may cascade to affect critical infrastructure essential to the broader society and economy. If this occurred, an attack intended to be
precise and limited to military targets instead could result in the widespread loss of electrical power, water, or
other essential services, with resulting economic disruption and potential loss of life. The attacked side could
feel compelled to respond at least in kind. Alternatively, a tit-for-tat cycle may occur, as one side may believe it could
gain coercive advantage by intentionally demonstrating its ability to hold at risk the other side’s critical
infrastructure through cyber, counter-space, and perhaps sabotage attacks. There is debate within the expert community as to
whether cyber attacks alone could have devastating effects, but it does appear likely that combined cyber and precision attacks on
critical infrastructure could devastate an economy and society. Whether such attacks escalated through
a gradual tit-for-tat or more rapid counterpunching, such counter-value strikes could lead to major
conflict and potentially nuclear war.

and unauthorized assertions of state sovereignty interfering in contractual agreements between and
among sovereign states. Such assertions and actions would undermine the reliability of national

commitments and violate widely accepted norms of international law. Likewise, both countries have a deeply vested
interest in boundary integrity and maintenance. The 1944 and 1970 treaties express that interest, giving the IBWC both jurisdiction and

relationship. The 1970 Boundary Treaty, in particular, has allowed the two countries to place high

would also expose the United States to legal jeopardy were Mexico to challenge the placement of
barriers in the Rio Grande by seeking a remedy in international law. Both countries are best served by
honoring the boundary treaty system that has worked so well to secure their respective national

Water, as a resource, presents four fundamentally political characteristics: 1) its extreme importance in the
life, daily life and existence of humanity, 2) its scarcity, 3) its poor distribution and 4) the fact that it is a
resource shared between different nationalities and social categories. - Water as a sine qua non for human existence:
For millennia, water has been considered, along with land and air, the most important natural resource (Butts, 1997; Chaplin, 2001; Westall &
Brack, 2018). And as scientist Albert Szent-Gyorgyi once stated, “Water is the matrix, the mother and the medium of life. There is no life without
water” (Szabo, 2011). As evidence, the search for extraterrestrial life by organizations like NASA often boils down to a simple strategy: “follow
the water” (Jones & Lineweaver, 2010). It is a sine qua non of human existence because of its use in life itself [with every aspect of our lives
being tied in some way to water], as well as agriculture, industry [every product produced depends on water], energy [every watt of energy
produced relies on water], health [every sanitation facility depends on water] food [every morsel of food depends on water], and many other
aspects of civilization. - Water as a scarce resource: 70% of the earth’s surface—the blue planet, is covered by water, which feeds the perfect
illusion of an infinite resource. Because if the earth is blue, it is only on the surface. In reality, water occupies only 0.023% of the earth’s mass
and only 2.6% of this water is fresh. The ice caps store more than 68% of it (Oki, 2006; Shiklomanov, 1998), 30% is hidden in the water tables,
the rest is quietly wandering between rivers, lakes, and other atmospheric humidity. In the end, less than 1% of the water on Earth is both fresh

and liquid, or 0.007% of the land mass (U.S. Geological Survey, 2021)—a drop in the bucket in an ocean of drought. - Unevenly distributed

activism in Latin America. Without a sensible analytical framework, China’s deepening ties with the region is merely treated as a part

they cannot find allies to join. This study attributes such argument not only to the lopsided attention to traditional hard balancing, but also to

Chinese policy banks have emerged as an important alternative to traditional lenders for the countries that have had difficulties in borrowing in global capital markets due to high risk (Dollar 2017). This means that they have

According to Kaplan (2016), Chinese lenders are characterized by risk tolerance, avoiding harsh policy conditions or credit being contingent on a
country’s macroeconomic performance. However, it is pointed out that they often require oil sales agreements or equipment purchases from the borrowing governments. Several Latin American countries have been leveraging their future oil assets and profitability to attract Chinese lending. Policy Loans as an Instrument of Soft
Balancing Keohane (1984) links control over capital to hegemony—in politics, money can buy friendship and loyalty. According to this view, the emergence of China as a major lender in Latin America should pose a threat to US hegemony in the region. However, are these recent dynamics a reflection of China’s soft balancing
against the regional hegemon? It is pivotal to ask whether its lending behavior across the region is motivated by its geopolitical goals or a consequence of independent economic motives for profit. We argue that China’s outbound loans feature geopolitical reasons rather than profit maximization goals. China’s outbound loans,
mainly issued through its policy banks, China Export-Import Bank and China Development Bank, are almost exclusively official lending, signifying that the Chinese state plays a critical role in its policy banks’ lending decisions (Zhang and Heyang 2019). Indeed, these policy banks operate under the direct ownership and supervision
of the Ministry of Finance and the Chinese State Council (Horn et al. 2019). The government ownership of policy banks allows Beijing to strategically employ state-driven lending to enter new markets, secure natural resources, and further China’s global ambitions. Chinese policy loans have always had a strategic element. In our
empirical analysis covering 18 countries in the region from 2005 to 2018, we specifically estimate the effects of maintaining a strategic partnership with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions. We also inquire how a host Latin American country’s ties with the United States affect the
likelihood of Chinese policy loans. One may point out that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are driven not by Beijing’s soft balancing strategy but rather by demands from Latin American governments. It could be that the countries that are marginalized by the United States or non-beneficiaries of the traditional Western
lending agencies are pursuing a strategy of diversification and turning to China as an alternative source of financing (Urdinez et al. 2016). This argument gives agency to Latin American countries and points to the ideological affinities between China and a number of left-wing Latin American governments in the recent past (Kaplan
2019). In light of these considerations, we introduce two “control” dependent variables, an empirical strategy similar to the one employed in Urdinez et al. (2016), to support our hypothesis that Chinese lending decisions are supply-driven—i.e., strategically-driven—rather than demand-driven. We compare the determinants of
Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions with those of two other forms of China’s capital exports, OFDI by Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and private-owned enterprises (POEs). Geopolitical underpinnings are expected to be most obvious in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks. Investment decisions by SOEs are likely
to be less conditioned by geopolitical interests of the Chinese government, as there is less room for state involvement in the decision making process. Political bias of this kind is expected to be much weaker when OFDI decisions are made by POEs, in which private agents play the main role in searching for yield. If we observe that
the determinants of lending decisions and OFDI decisions by SOEs and POEs are significantly different from one another, it can be said that Beijing has a significant degree of agency in its engagement with Latin American countries—otherwise, the countries in the region would be almost equally receptive to the three forms of
Chinese capital exports. We can then argue that Chinese policy loans are primarily a product of an underlying soft balancing strategy of the Chinese government, rather than an outcome of the will of a number of Latin American countries to approach Beijing for loans. It would be naive to say that all the outbound investment
decisions are made at the behest of Beijing. However, it would be equally wrong to assume that the Chinese government does not exercise control over SOEs operating in industries and areas of strategic importance when they make OFDI decisions. Some point out that the extent to which the Chinese state drives SOEs’ decisions is
overstated (Cogman, Gao, and Leung 2017). Indeed, a careful scrutiny of China’s SOEs reveals that their linkages with the state are becoming thinner and outbound investment decisions by SOEs, especially those involving acquisition deals, are increasingly driven by commercial logic rather than dictates of the state (Collier 2018).
However, following a developmental state path, the Chinese government still subordinates its SOEs through several regulatory institutions such as the State Council, Ministry of Commerce, People’s Bank of China, State Administration of Foreign Exchange, and State Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, while
relinquishing control of less important enterprises (Mattlin 2009; Luo, Xue, and Han 2010; Chen 2018). On the other hand, Chinese POEs are significantly less stifled by the institutional environment for their OFDI compared with SOEs, especially in terms of Chinese government influence that they have to deal with. For example, all

SOEs are required to apply for OFDI approval from the Ministry of Commerce, whereas OFDI projects by POEs only require approval by provincial-level Foreign Economic Relation and Trade (Luo, Xue, and Han 2010). Similar to our work, a binary dependent variable is employed to observe if a Latin American government has a Chinese bilateral loan outstanding as a

However, the analysis is focused on the effect of host governments’ partisanship on the likelihood of Chinese policy loans and ultimately the influence of Chinese lending on fiscal policy choices of Latin American governments. Institution-Building Efforts as an Instrument of Soft Balancing The growth of Sino-Latin
American trade and Chinese investments and lending to the region has been complemented by the improving diplomatic relations between the two sides. In 1990, just five years after Premier Zhao Ziyang embarked on the first high-level visit to Latin America, President Yang Shangkun paid the first visit as the head of the state.
China declared that its relations with Latin America should be based upon the strategy of non-interference. In the 1990s, China’s Going Out strategy, championed by President Jiang Zemin, identified the region as a target for strategic partnership. The beginning of the twenty-first century saw cooperation between China and Latin
America deepen rapidly. President Jiang Zemin’s tour to the region in 2001 followed by that of President Hu Jintao in 2005, and subsequent exchange by senior Chinese and regional officials and business leaders, drew the two sides markedly closer to each other. Moreover, in 2004, China earned observer status in the OAS. The
China-Chile FTA entered into force in 2006, China-Peru FTA in 2010, and China-Costa Rica FTA in 2011. In its first ever policy paper on the region in 2008,3 the Chinese government officially declared that it would adopt a more strategic approach to Latin America. The paper emphasized support for new regional institutions,
including CELAC, which have the potential to counterbalance the US-led organizations, such as the OAS and IDB. Since then, China has demonstrated robust institution-building efforts, and it appears that such efforts are paying off. China has successfully immersed itself in a web of regional institutions and relationships, both
bilateral and multilateral, through which it now pursues its strategic goals, such as initiating military engagements and sales, extending the reach of the BRI, garnering regional support for the One China Policy, internationalizing its currency, and securing investments in strategically important waterways. China has continued to
strengthen bilateral strategic relationships in the region. China has established comprehensive strategic partnerships—the third highest level of bilateral ties in its diplomatic lexicon— with Brazil (2012), Mexico (2013), Peru (2013), Venezuela (2014), Argentina (2014), Chile (2016), and Ecuador (2016) and strategic
partnerships—the fourth level of bilateral ties after comprehensive strategic partnerships—with Costa Rica (2015), Uruguay (2016), and Bolivia (2018). The characterization of these partnerships mean more than diplomatic talking points. Their stated purpose is to coordinate more closely on regional and international affairs, even
including military ones. In its 2015 National Defense Strategy White Paper, the Chinese government reaffirmed its interest in maintaining friendly military ties with Latin American counterparts (State Council Information Office of the People’s Republic of China 2015). After Xi Jinping took office in 2013, China’s diplomatic offensive
to the region has been more active than ever. In 2014, Xi, during his trip to Brazil for the BRICs Summit, proposed to build a new “1+3+6” cooperation framework: “1” refers to the China-Latin American Countries and Caribbean States Cooperation Plan (2015–2019), which is expected to serve as a blueprint for economic growth and
sustainable development; “3” denotes three engines—trade, investment, and financial cooperation—that will drive practical cooperation; and “6” means six cooperation priorities, such as energy and resources, infrastructure construction, agriculture, manufacturing, scientific and technological innovation, and information
technology. China’s participation in CELAC and the establishment of the China-CELAC Forum opened a new chapter for China’s strategic engagement in the region. The first Ministerial Meeting of the China-CELAC Forum was held in 2015. According to Yu (2015), the meeting was a landmark in Sino–Latin American relations,
signaling that cooperation between the two sides substantially expanded into the geopolitical and strategic realms. The Beijing Declaration issued by the Forum was a clear demonstration that neither China nor Latin America was satisfied with the US-dominated unipolar system. The second white paper on Latin America and the
Caribbean released in 2016 was another forthright statement of China’s strategic approach to the region. While reaffirming its commitments to the “1+3+6” cooperation framework, the Chinese government vowed to explore a “3x3” model for capacity cooperation. First, three passages—logistics, power, and information
technology—would be constructed to boost domestic demand in Latin America. Second, three financing channels— funds, loans, and insurance—would be expanded to provide financing to bilateral projects. In support of the implementation of the model, China pledged financing of $30 billion in total. When materialized, the two
projects will ultimately transform the existing Sino-Latin American relations. The “1+3+6” and “3x3” frameworks signaled a subtle yet significant shift in China’s policy focus, from trade to more diversified engagement. While China under the leadership of Xi was embracing Latin American countries in a more active manner, US-Latin
American relations deteriorated further since President Donald Trump took office. The growing US disengagement in the region under the Trump administration was marked by such actions as withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership, reduction of US foreign aid, and hostility toward Mexico and Latino immigrants in general.
The Trump administration also rolled back the previous administration’s Cuba policy. The “America First” doctrine has in effect once again created a strategic void in the region, giving China an opportunity to fill the void and promote its interests. In 2017, China renewed its efforts to diplomatically isolate Taiwan in Latin America.
Central American countries have constituted the biggest bloc of Taiwan’s few remaining world allies. Panama shifted its diplomatic alignment from Taiwan to China in 2017, followed by the Dominican Republic and El Salvador in 2018. In exchange, China promised investments and loans to these countries. Beijing’s renewed activism
in promoting its One China Policy resulted in US concerns. For instance, immediately after El Salvador’s switchover, the White House announced that it would reevaluate its diplomatic relationship with El Salvador, warning that the “short-term economic benefits” offered by China would lead to “dependency and domination.” At
the same time, the Chinese leadership, taking advantage of bilateral strategic partnerships and its position in the CELAC, has endeavored to extend the BRI to the region with noticeable success. Beijing issued a formal invitation to the BRI at the Second Ministerial Meeting of China-CELAC Forum in 2018. It was an effort to gain
CELAC’s endorsement on the “natural extension” of the BRI, Xi’s signature foreign policy objective. Panama was the first country in the region to sign a cooperation agreement under the BRI banner, and many others including Bolivia, Chile, Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama, Peru, Uruguay,
Venezuela, and several other Caribbean countries have followed suit, with the purpose of gaining access to infrastructure financing. However, one should note that some of the largest economies in the region such as Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico have not yet signed any BRI agreements. This shows that Beijing’s charm
offensive in the region has its limits. Another recent piece of evidence of China’s soft balancing against American power in the region is the expansion of its currency swap program with Argentina. In 2018, China and Argentina agreed to nearly double their existing currency swap deal, bringing the total to $18.7 billion. They first
agreed to a currency swap program to boost Argentina’s falling reserves in 2009. The recession-struck country once again turned to China as a source of financing and used some of the funds to cover its emergency deficits. Though its effect is marginal, the currency swap echoes China’s intensifying efforts to gradually promote the
renminbi into a global currency. In parallel, the extension of the BRI to Latin America is expected to bring about the more widespread use of the renminbi. While China’s soft balancing strategy relies on a low-key approach for the purpose of avoiding direct confrontation with the United States, China’s
increasing presence in the areas where America’s vital interests are at stake is likely to trigger strong
pushback from the United States. Panama is a case in point. After establishing formal ties with China in 2017, Panama officially pledged its
support for the BRI. In less than two years, the two states have signed nearly 20 bilateral agreements in trade and infrastructure. Chinese
state-owned enterprises represented by China Ocean Shipping Companies Group are actively involved in investments around the Panama Canal.
In response, US Secretary of State Mike Pompeo, while visiting Panama in October 2018, warned Panama against accepting the BRI loans
(Churchill 2018). Furthermore, the US Southern Command’s 2019 posture statement associated China’s infrastructure investments on the
Panama Canal with US security interests. The Panama Canal could once again turn into a flashpoint for great-power conflict. Wary of China’s
increasing ambition, Washington’s view on US-China relations in the Western Hemisphere seems to be gradually moving from win-win to
zero-sum. The trilateral relationship in the region has been further complicated by the deepening US-China tension. The ongoing trade conflict
between the two major powers, which began in 2018, froze their bilateral relations. Both sides have been setting titfor-tat tariffs and other trade
barriers on each other. Although they have recently agreed to discuss the rolling back of tariffs in phases, it seems uncertain when this all-out
trade war will come to an end. In addition, along with the increasing trade deficit with China, concerns regarding the dramatic increase in
Chinese OFDI have been growing in the United States. The 2017 US National Security Strategy warned that the “national innovation base”
should be protected from Beijing’s attempts to vacuum up core US companies and technologies. In response, the Foreign Investment Risk
Review Modernization Act was legislated in 2018. It is widely believed that this effort was primarily aimed at addressing concerns over Chinese
OFDI in the United States. Despite the growing suspicion of the United States toward the expanded role played by China in Latin America and
the deteriorating US-China relations, Beijing does not seem to have practiced any strategy of hard balancing in the region. The escalating US
containment toward China both within and outside the region has not yet caused Beijing to look to more assertive or outright behavior in the
region, strengthening our arguments about China’s intentions of soft balancing against the regional hegemon. Conclusion Pursuing a
multifaceted approach as a major trading partner, investor, lender, and diplomatic partner, China has established a remarkable presence in Latin
America over a relatively short period of time. We argue that Beijing’s intensifying economic and political clout in the
region is an outcome of its soft balancing strategy, designed to advance its geopolitical goal of increasing its
influence in America’s backyard without directly confronting the regional hegemon. China has used its financial
power—loans by state-owned policy banks—as a tool to draw the countries with strategic value to Beijing into its
political orbit. In that regard, it can be argued China’s policy banks are the key institutions underpinning China’s soft balancing strategy in
the region. Chinese soft balancing is also evident in its continuing activism in strengthening diplomatic arrangements with the countries in the
region and engaging in the CELAC and the China-CELAC Forum. China’s expanding economic and political influence in the
Western Hemisphere has interrelated implications for the intensifying US-China strategic competition. China’s
growing leverage in the region will serve to shift the regional balance of power in favor of China if the Chinese
economy continues to grow and Beijing remains committed to the region. Beijing’s activism in regional institutions will be
useful in expanding its role as a major trading partner, investor and lender in the region, and translating its economic clout
into geopolitical influence. China’s growing role in the region is not likely to immediately transform into more assertive behavior. Yet its
institutional arrangements and economic interdependence between the two sides will allow China to gradually
extract concessions from regional powers, chipping away at American dominance in the region. In the short run,
China’s rallying of like-minded countries, or countries adversarial to the United States, can serve as a tool to advance narrow strategic interests
such as the One China Policy and a hedge against the excesses of US hegemony. In the long run, it may lead to the emergence of a
counterbalancing coalition against the US-led unilateralism. Ultimately, if China sustains the momentum, China’s growing
influence in Latin America can seriously impair the ability of the United States to exercise effective

leadership, not only in the Western Hemisphere but also in other areas of strategic interest. For one thing, if China consolidates its
position as an indispensable partner to the region, more countries will begin to look to Beijing not only as an economic but also
as a political alternative that can replace the hegemonic position of the United States. For another, if America’s
backyard turns into China’s playground, the United States will then be, as realists argue, less free to exert its power outside the Western
Hemisphere.
