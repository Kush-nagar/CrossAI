ABSTRACT Aside from NATO, the Five Eyesintelligence network between the USA, the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand is the world's most enduring multilateral arrangementof its type.
While theFive Eyesnetwork does not constitute a formal security alliance in the classic sense of the term,
itdoesemulatesignificantfeatures of how alliances operatein practice, including active burden-sharing and intra-alliance bargaining. Most analysts claimthat the
USA dictates in hierarchical fashion the terms and conditions of how the Five Eyes network functions, and that junior partners have little alternative but to fall in line if they want to preserve the
flow of high-grade intelligence from Washington. Using Australia as a case study, this article shows that a more fluid relationship has been at play, one that challenges
conventional assumptions about asymmetrical alliances and the role of junior partners. Intelligence cooperation between states is a perennial feature of international relations. As the sources and methods of collection have become increasingly sophisticated, the value yielded by intelligence exchanges has risen accordingly. For allies of the USA, by far the world’s pre-eminent collector of all-source intelligence, this has been especially the case. As Loch Johnson (2008, 64) points out: ‘the US continues to have the
largest and most expensive intelligence apparatus in the world, indeed in the history of humankind’. It is essentially because of this, critics charge, that Washington dominates intelligence cooperation among allies due to the sheer scale of its collection capabilities, its desire to exert control over other states, and the well-established hostility on the part of US intelligence agencies towards non-compliant foreign governments. The evident asymmetry that characterises the relationship between the USA and its
allies, according to this perspective, produces outcomes that are inimical to the interests of smaller countries, which have little choice but to accept the consequences of the power imbalance (see, for instance, Beeson 2015; Klein 1988). In this article, I challenge the claim that junior intelligence partners of the USA are victims of a power imbalance that produces outcomes antithetical to their interests. Just as smaller states often seek out generic security alliances with major powers, they also covet the benefits
that flow from intimate intelligence-sharing arrangements with more powerful states. As with alliances broadly, junior partners that have intelligence-sharing arrangements with more powerful states are willing to sacrifice some autonomy in return for pay-offs in other areas. While many states eschew asymmetrical security alliances with the USA, the strengthening of the USA’s alliances since the end of the Cold War is testament to the value that smaller states believe they extract from these arrangements. This
article advances this argument by examining the so-called ‘Five Eyes’ network, which is the formal intelligence-sharing relationship between the USA, the UK, Canada, Australia and New Zealand. In placing Australia’s role in the Five Eyes network in the forefront of the analysis, I argue that the network should, in fact, be seen more in terms
of an alliance than a ‘club’among Anglosphere countries, as it is often portrayed. Australian governments have issued several Defence White Papers since the mid-1970s, but only in the most recent Defence White Paper—released in February 2016—was Australia’s participation in the Five Eyes intelligence network covered
in detail (Grayson 2016). This was embedded in discussion of the bilateral alliance with the USA, with the document noting that ‘Australia’s membership of the Five Eyes intelligence community (comprising Australia, the United States, New Zealand, the United Kingdom and Canada) provides Australia with information superiority and intelligence cooperation that is vital input into our defence planning’ (Department of Defence 2016, 122). Notably, the 2016 Defence White Paper also made specific reference to
the ‘close’ intelligence cooperation Australia has with Canada as ‘members of the Five Eyes intelligence community’ (138). For Australian policymakers, the Five Eyes arrangement is inextricably bound up with the bilateral
alliance with the USA, and in many respects is an ancillary element of that alliance. However, as I argue, the Five Eyes
network itself should be regarded as emulating many of the key features of alliances.1 Here, I define an
alliance to be a formal bilateral or multilateral commitment by states to support other states in
defending their security. Alliances can be distinguished from coalitions or alignments of common security
interest among states. The latter rarely, if ever, contain any treaty commitments and are usually
shortterm relationships of convenience that lack the depth and intimacy characteristic of formal alliance
relationships. As Kenneth Waltz (1979, 166) has argued, alliances are typically directed at specific threats: ‘The common interest is ordinarily a negative one: fear of other states’. Yet, the purpose of alliances can change over time and need not always be directed at a specific state or states. The Five Eyes network reflects many of the
core characteristics that appear in the literature on alliances, including burden-sharing, intra-alliance bargaining and a deepening normative commitment over time (Snyder 1997). Indeed, looking at Five
Eyesas an alliancecanhelpshedimportantinsights into how its members interact, and the dynamics that shape the behaviour of each state. This article addresses two interlinked questions. First, to what
extent does the Five Eyes intelligence network mirror attributes we would expect to find in alliance relationships? And second, what can Australia’s role in the network tell us about whether its inherent asymmetries disadvantage junior partners? These questions are significant because they go to the heart of how security arrangements are managed in the contemporary world. In particular, the questions focus on the proposition of whether junior partners receive benefits commensurate with the risks they run
in being allied to more dominant, and potentially domineering, partners. Examining Australia’s approach to the Five Eyes alliance helps to address these questions and makes an important contribution to a key theme of this special issue—namely, how material and transactional considerationsin the national security policymaking arena
intersect with normative dynamics.The operation of contemporary alliancesis in many respects the ideal case study; they invariably involve
detached cost-benefit calculations, but the most enduringalliances arealsounderpinned bygenuine and
robust normative commitment. This is frequently overlookedin the secondary literature on alliances, which is almost always dominated by realist analysis that

discounts the influence of non-material variablesin international relations. The article proceeds in four parts. The first section outlines many of the prominent themes in the secondary literature on alliances and focuses particularly on the role of junior alliance partners. This provides an important framework for analysing
the evolution of the Five Eyes network and Australia’s role within it. Section two outlines the principal attributes of the network and how it evolved from a series of discrete wartime agreements as part of a broader alliance effort into a worldwide multilateral alliance in its own right. The third section of the article examines how Australia has engaged in Five Eyes over time, how its role as a supplier and consumer of intelligence has shaped this engagement, and whether the costs to Australia have exceeded the
benefits. The final section of the article draws conclusions from the preceding analysis and charts directions for future research in the field. Understanding the dynamics of alliances The formation of alliances, how they operate over time and whether they can be sustainedover the long term areall contingentona
range of factors. In particular,theperceived reliability of security guaranteesand assurancesis centralto
alliances,andthis, in turn, isinfluenced bythereputationof the major powerto ‘deliver’ on past commitmentsin
international relations(see Mattes 2012; Miller 2003). As the country with the greatest experience of alliance management, the USA is closely attuned tothe issue ofcredibilityin its
alliance relationships, and successive administrationssince World War II have beenconscious ofthe need to uphold
commitments in one part of the worldto ensurethe USA’s reliability as an ally is preserved globally. Reassuring alliance partners of US commitment to
their security is a hallmark of the assorted declarations that follow alliance summits. In order to back up these verbal reassurances, Washington routinely authorises high-profile military deployments to signal US resolve to protect allies’ security during periods of tension with rivals in different theatres. In practice, alliances are much more than mere
transactional arrangements. To be sure, alliances are formed initiallybecause of perceived threats from
other states, and the military cooperation and intelligence exchanges that underpin alliances are designed to counter these threats. In this sense, the traditional realist explanation for why alliances form is accurate. But realists have failed to explain why alliances endure when threats either change in scope or dissipate altogether. NATO’s endurance after the Cold War is the most prominent case in point. Using realist theory, many incorrectly predicted that the
demise of the Soviet threat would trigger NATO’s demise (Wallander 2000). This prediction either overlooked or underestimated the extent to which alliances acquire important normative characteristicsover time thatserve to bind members. For NATO, it mattered that by the early 1990s members were committed to democracy and to the European project broadly defined. These shared
principlesreinforced the importance of the alliancein projecting shared values, which was evidenced subsequently by the robust demand for NATO membership on the part of former Warsaw Pact countries. At the bilateral level, the US alliance with Australia is another case where shared norms have been strengthened
over time, even as the threat environment has remained relatively stable (Gill and Switzer 2015). During the 1950s and 1960s, the tone of alliance interactions between Washington and Canberra was highly transactional—exemplified by tension over US ambivalence to support Australia during the Konfrontasi with Indonesia—but by the 1990s, shared democratic values had become the central theme running through US-Australia alliance declarations. For major powers, alliances with weaker states can be
beneficial in maintaining bases overseas and key access routes, formalising a network of support among like-minded states and exerting influence (and potential control) over certain states. Despite Washington’s periodically stated concerns over the tendency of allies to freeride on security guarantees, the USA places great store in its alliance relationships as evidence of its global reach, appeal and strategic influence. For junior allies, a security alliance with a major power provides more obvious benefits due to
the asymmetrical nature of the arrangement. These benefits include the prospect (if not necessarily the guarantee) of military protection in crises, preferential access to high-end military equipment and joint training, and a sense of reassurance that promotes confidence in the execution of foreign policy and international engagement more generally. As one recent study has argued, junior allies can be very effective in practice at extracting maximum benefit from alliances if they have more at stake on any given
issue area under negotiation with the major power; this ‘asymmetry of interests’ can yield outcomes that belie the material power imbalance between allies (Shin, Izatt, and Moon 2016). However, although materially weaker states derive enhanced security from alliances, no two countries seek exactly the same returns from an alliance. While all countries covet security, and although the ‘capability aggregation’ yielded by alliances multiplies that security, states do not search for the same returns on alliances.
NATO’s 28 members have varying objectives in this respect, and its eastern European members are vulnerable in different ways to the perceived growing security threat from Russia (Kroenig 2015, 53-57). Japan and South Korea, although sharing certain alliance aims, have different perspectives on what they want from Washington by way of security assurances. Preoccupied with the immediate military threat from North Korea, Seoul tends to be more focused on operational-level US assurances that entail direct
military support, while Tokyo, which is focused on the implications of China’s rise and its military modernisation efforts, seeks more general assurances about continued US resolve to remain the predominant major power in the Asia-Pacific in the twenty-first century (Allen and Sugg 2016). Countries expect certain pay-offs from alliances. As Stephen Walt (2009, 116) argues: ‘Members of any alliance are usually tempted to shift the burdens of providing security on to others, while simultaneously seeking to
maximise their own influence within the alliance itself. The expectation regarding pay-offs flows from the view that the extent of investment in alliance relationships should yield roughly commensurate results. For more junior partners, the diminution of strategic autonomy that arises from allying with a major power should be offset by the prospect of military protection. States invest resources and energy in building up alliances, and these costs can be significant. And they are not merely financial; they also
relate to potential political and strategic risk. By allying with a major power, junior parties will expose themselves to a situation where they may be expected to support the major-power ally even when these policies are at odds with the junior ally’s interests. As James Morrow (1991, 906) writes,it is wrong to assume that states become allies because they have identical interests; indeed, ‘without divergent interests, an alliance would be unnecessary because each party would come to the other’s aid simply in
order to pursue its own (identical) interests’. Although allies will generally strive to ensure that disagreements are kept out of the public domain, there will be occasions where disagreements between allies become public. One of the most prominent cases was strong opposition by several NATO countries to the US-led invasion of Iraq (Oliver 2003). Yet, it is important to stress that public opinion is not always the decisive factor in shaping how junior allies’ elites respond to alliance challenges. There was popular
opposition in Western Europe to the eployment of new-generation US nuclear systems in the late 1970s and early 1980s, but most of these deployments on NATO soil went ahead (Garthoff 1983). More recently, Sarah Kreps (2010) has shown that adverse views among the general publics of France, Canada and Germany did not deter the elites in these countries from engaging militarily in Afghanistan, despite in some cases sustaining a large number of combat fatalities. It is important to be cautious in drawing a
straight line between public opinion and how allies seek to manage their alliance with a major power. The Five Eyes intelligence alliance It is difficult to think of a single arrangement that has triggered more conspiracy theories than the Five Eyes intelligence network. Accusations persist that the network is an exclusive club designed to reinforce the global influence of the Anglosphere, and that its highly secretive nature is an anachronism in a world where the general public’s call for accountability and
transparency is a strong theme permeating the discourse of globalisation. The apparent blurring of foreign and domestic intelligence since 9/11, which was dramatically underscored by the Wikileaks and Snowden disclosures, has triggered unease about the scope for authorised and unauthorised electronic espionage directed by intelligence agencies against their own citizens and those of their allies. This has not been helped by the slowness with which these agencies have ‘informed the public about how the
remit of electronic surveillance goes beyond the simplistically perceived “spying on persons of interest” to a broader range of foreign and national intelligence collection priorities and threats’ (Walsh and Miller 2016, 356). The Five Eyes network is the world’s oldest formalised intelligence network and has its origins in the significant expansion of Allied intelligence cooperation and exchange during World War II. This cooperation was especially evident in the area of signals intelligence (SIGINT) and led to the
signing in 1943 of the British-US Communications Intelligence Agreement, of which Australia was a member. It was also around this time that the bilateral intelligence relationship between Canberra and Washington began to develop, largely as a result of Australia being the headquarters of the Allied Southwest Pacific Command (Andrew 1992). While most joint US and Australian intelligence activities were discontinued after 1945, ‘those concerned with SIGINT were reorganised as the central element in the
post-war UK-US-Australian intelligence relationship’ (Ball 2001a, 235).The Five Eyes network was founded with the conclusion of the top-secret UKUSA Agreement of 1947 that formally codified the division of spheres of responsibility for SIGINT collection ‘between the First Party (the United States) and the Second Parties (Australia, Britain, Canada, and New Zealand)’ (Richelson and Ball 1990, 5). While SIGINT lay at the heart of the network, the Five Eyes countries established detailed cooperation activities
during the Cold War in areas as diverse as ocean surveillance, covert action, human intelligence collection and counter-intelligence (Richelson and Ball 1990, 6). In its early years, the network was characterised by tensions between London and Washington over British resentment at having been assigned a secondary status within UKUSA, along with some friction between British collection agencies and their Australian counterparts over the former’s ‘wish to keep their Commonwealth associates in a subordinate
position’ (Aldrich 2010, 93).Over time, the Five Eyes network has evolved into a large-scale intelligence alliance with a global reach. This reach has been multiplied through a combination of software and hardware commitments among members. As space-based satellites became an increasingly central feature of military communications from the 1960s onwards, a series of ground stations was constructed in Five Eyes member states to relay collected SIGINT. This hardware commitment by US allies was crucial
in the maintenance and operation of systems that provided wide coverage of the Soviet Union, China and other intelligence-collection targets during the Cold War. A number of ground stations were built in Canada, New Zealand and the UK.In Australia, the Pine Gap and Nurrungar facilities provided important coverage of Soviet and Chinese military activity, in addition to more generic communications traffic (see Ball 1987, 1988). These facilities had a dual use in preserving strategic stability while at the same
time potentially enhancing nuclear warfighting options for the USA (Stares 1985, 140-141). On the one hand, they were capable of detecting early missile launches as well as the detonation of nuclear warheads over Soviet and Chinese territory, which had the potential to contribute to ‘launch-on-warning’ nuclear retaliation, as well as promoting flexible (re)targeting options in the midst of a nuclear exchange. However, the ground stations also contributed to the monitoring of missile telemetry, which helped
reveal by technical means ether the USSR was complying with its commitments under international arms control agreements. These facilities were regarded as so important that Australian officials conceded that they would in all likelihood be among the top Soviet nuclear targets in the event of a global war.2In the software domain, one of the most salient features of Five Eyes has been the so-called Echelon program, which since the 1960s has involved the interception from civilian satellites of communications
on the basis of keywords submitted by each Five Eyes member country. Through highly sophisticated computer programs (called ‘dictionaries’) at ground relay stations, these communication intercepts are sorted according to these keywords and ‘automatically forwarded to analysts’ (Richelson 2012, 350-351). Like all transactions surrounding the Five Eyes alliance, deliberations surrounding the Echelon program have been highly secretive, and knowledge of it has been the result of a combination of investigative
reporting and detailed scholarship (for the path-breaking analysis, see Hager 1996, ch. 3). It is driven essentially by the collection capabilities of the USA’s National Security Agency and its scale is reportedly immense. As Martin Rudner notes: compared to the earlier SIG1NT systems deployed during the Cold War, which were designed primarily to intercept diplomatic, espionage, and military communications, Echelon had a broadbanded capacity to monitor virtually all types of electronic communications among
public and private sector organizations and individuals in almost every country (Rudner 2004, 201). The contemporary global reach of Five Eyes is exemplified by the formal burden-sharing responsibilities among its members. As Jeffrey Richelson summarises: Under the present division of responsibilities, the United States is responsible for SIG1NT in Latin America, most of Asia, Russia, and northern China. Australia’s area of responsibility includes its neighbours (such as Indonesia), southern China, and the
nations of Indo China. Britain is responsible for Africa and the former Soviet Union west of the Urals. The polar regions of Russia are the responsibility of Canada, and New Zealand’s area of responsibility is the Western Pacific. Specific tasking assignments are specified in the SIGINT Combined Operating List (Richelson 2012, 349). Another key feature of the Five Eyes alliance is the sharing of intelligence with so-called ‘Third Party’ countries such as Denmark, France, Singapore and South Korea. According to
top-secret documents released as a result of the Snowden disclosures, this has reportedly included the exchange of information gathered as a result of ‘the tapping of high speed fibre optic cables at 20 locations worldwide’ (Dorling 2013a). Yet, despite this, the Snowden disclosures also revealed that the Five Eyes partners have, in turn, directly targeted many of these ‘Third Party’ countries, several of which, like Germany and France, are NATO allies (Borger 2013).The Five Eyes network is not the only
multilateral intelligence-sharing network in the world. The Club de Berne, which was formed in 1971, is an intelligence-sharing arrangement involving European Union security service chiefs. It has its own communications network, and much of its contemporary focus appears to be on information exchange with respect to counterterrorism (Aldrich 2004, 738). Several other networks also exist that involve the formal exchange of intelligence among countries, as distinct from the routine sharing of intelligence
between friendly states that occurs regularly in international relations. Probably the most advanced of these is the intelligence exchange that occurs within the framework of NATO’s Special Committee, which has existed since 1952 and comprises the heads of all the alliance’s security intelligence services (Lefebvre 2003, 531). Ironically, given the robustness and endurance of NATO as a multinational alliance, even this is not a serious rival to the Five Eyes network in terms of its depth, breadth and coherence.The
US Department of State’s former Coordinator for Intelligence Resources and Planning, Jennifer Sims (2006,202), has written that multilateral intelligence networks are generally inefficient arrangements because ‘intelligence costs tend to rise and benefits decline as the number of parties increases because the importance of ensuring competitive advantage in support to policymakers biases most intelligence services against sharing sensitive sources in multiparty arrangements’. However, the Five Eyes network,
which emulates important traits of alliances, can be seen as the one major exception to this general rule. This is especially the case for junior partners, which, as I argue below, benefit inordinately from the network. The depth of cohesion among the Five Eyes countries is symbolised by the reported commitment that they will not target each other with intelligence-collection activities (Manhire 2015). Notably, the National Security Agency (2007) has acknowledged that, even during the lowest point of US-New
Zealand relations in the 1980s when the Australia, New Zealand, United States Security (ANZUS) Treaty was formally ruptured, US policy was ‘not [to] splinter our relationship’ with New Zealand’s primary SIGINT agency. Although it is easy to exaggerate the coherence of purpose among any group of sovereign states, the individual intelligence agencies of the five partner states interact with an affinity strengthened by their common Anglo-Saxon culture, accepted liberal-democratic values and complementary
national interests, all seasoned with a profound sense of confidence in each other and a degree of professional trust so strong as to be unique in the world (Cox 2012, 5). Australia and the Five Eyes network As noted above, Australia’s initial engagement in the Five Eyes network was essentially a product of its close relationship with Britain and its intimate wartime intelligence collaboration with the USA in the South-West Pacific theatre. During the 1940s and 1950s, Australia remained an enthusiastic junior
partner in the network. Indeed, efforts by the Chifley and Menzies governments to build a coherent Australian intelligence community after 1945 were shaped ineluctably by Britain and the USA. Not only did the structure and operation of Australia’s post-war intelligence agencies tend to mimic their UK and US counterparts, but direct assistance from the latter with respect to security and collection and assessment techniques played a crucial role in the development of the Australian intelligence community’s
capabilities; the most notable case was MI5’s pivotal role in the creation of the Australian Security Intelligence Organisation in 1948 (Andrew 2009, 370-371). By the 1970s, however, Australia’s approach to intelligence assessments had moved closer to the US model of‘a more autonomous national intelligence organisation’ (Woodard 2001, 10). Ironically, this shift was accelerated under the Whitlam government, which coincided with a period of unprecedented bilateral tension with Washington (see Curran
2015). The Five Eyes alliance has become more important to Australia over time. As Australia’s Attorney-General George Brandis has noted, the post-9/11 homeland security agenda has accelerated ‘information sharing for counter-terrorism purposes, countering violent extremism, cybercrime, encryption, and foreign investment in critical infrastructure’.3 As alluded to in the previous section, the profile of Australia’s involvement in the network was raised in the 1980s with revelations concerning the activities of
a series of SIGINT ground relay stations on Australian territory staffed by US and Australian personnel. The so-called ‘joint facilities’ located at Nurrungar, Pine Gap and the Northwest Cape in Western Australia were critical installations in the day-to-day operation of the US global military communications system. Less conspicuous have been the myriad foreign intelligence liaison links between individual Australian agencies and their counterparts in Washington, London, Ottawa and Wellington. These links include
having Australian intelligence officers embedded in counterpart UKUSA agencies, as well as intelligence officers drawn from the four partners embedded in Australian agencies. The liaison relationships are overseen and managed at the Australian end by the National Intelligence Coordination Committee based in the National Security Division of the Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet (ONA n.d.).5 In the contemporary context, Australia’s involvement in the Five Eyes network has two essential
dimensions: supply and consumption. In terms of supplying the network, the Australian Signals Directorate plays a primary role through its facilities based near Darwin, Geraldton and Canberra, which contribute directly to the National Security Agency’s collection program (Dorling 2013b). The Snowden disclosures revealed that most of the collaboration between the Australian Signals Directorate and the National Security Agency has focused on Indonesia and China, and encompasses economic espionage as
well as military and political intelligence-gathering. This reportedly has also included the provision of detailed data by the National Security Agency to the Australian Signals Directorate in relation to Indonesian government officials located in various ministries (Risen and Poitras 2014). The reach of Australia’s intelligence-gathering efforts against Indonesian targets was underscored by leaked revelations by Australian Signals Directorate officials in a 2014 PowerPoint presentation that the agency had targeted the
mobile phones of the Indonesian president and his spouse (Bachelard 2014). Like Canada and New Zealand, Australia’s involvement in the Five Eyes alliance is unambiguously as a junior partner to Washington—as distinct from Britain, whose established global intelligence capabilities and historical role in Europe make it more of a senior partner. Australian governments rarely comment on what they regard as the primary value of Five Eyes, but when they do, two key themes are apparent. The first is a classic
refrain from junior alliance partners that if they did not have access to the capabilities provided by larger powers, they would have to outlay more on safeguarding their security. As one former senior Defence official has remarked: ‘the Five-Eyes community makes it possible for us to be part of a highly effective intelligence grouping without having to maintain the burden of constellations of self-funded satellites’ (Jennings 2013). Put bluntly, from this perspective, Australia’s freeriding on great and powerful
friends in accessing the highest-quality intelligence is a virtue that must be preserved. The second theme that is evident in public comments by serving and former Australian officials is that the density of interactions in the multilateral Five Eyes context promotes deeper trust at the bilateral level with members. In a February 2016 media conference with his New Zealand counterpart, Prime Minister Malcolm Turnbull observed that: because of the complete trust between Australia and New Zealand, as part of the
Five-Eyes security relationship, we are able to share experiences, share techniques, share learnings, and that is absolutely vital for both of our countries to continually do a better and better job in keeping us safe from cyber dangers (Turnbull 2016). This theme of closer bilateral intelligence relations between Canberra and Ottawa through the Five Eyes network was acknowledged publicly by the Canadian Minister of National Defence during his visit to Australia in 2016 (Nicholson 2016). ustralia’s involvement in
the Five Eyes network is more often than not seen through the prism of the country’s bilateral intelligence relationship with the USA. As Nick Bisley (2013, 406) observes, the intelligence relationship with Washington is widely seen in the Australian government ‘as the alliance’s most important benefit’. Indeed, so integrated is the intelligence relationship with the broader Australia-US alliance that it is difficult to distinguish it from Australia’s participation in Five Eyes, based as it is on US leadership. Australia’s
leading intelligence expert has observed that the fundamental bases of the US-Australian alliance are not the ANZUS Treaty or the many dozen other defence cooperation agreements between Australia and the United States, but the UKUSA Agreement of 1947-48 concerning SIGINT cooperation and the maintenance of the ‘joint facilities’ in Australia (Ball 2001a, 237). It has been estimated that the two-way intelligence flow between Australia and the USA is roughly 90 percent in Australia’s favour, with Canberra
providing niche contributions overwhelmingly in relation to South-East Asia and the Pacific region (Dupont 2007, 56). Echoing the alliance studies literature, however, an obvious danger for junior partners is that the asymmetry inherent in the Five Eyes arrangement exposes them to potential manipulation and control by the dominant major partner. This risk was recognised by the landmark Hope Royal Commission on Intelligence and Security, which reported its findings in 1976. In a section marked ‘Top
Secret—Australian Eyes Only’, the report noted that there were ‘risks and costs’ associated with Australia’s foreign intelligence relationships: Typically, critics of Australia’s involvement in the Five Eyes network and the bilateral intelligence relationship with the USA (the two are usually treated synonymously) allege that Australia is exposed to increased physical risk by having high-value intelligence installations on its soil, and increased politic l risk stemming from what is portrayed as over-reliance on US
intelligence flows in particular (Weiss, Thurbon, and Mathews 2007, 172-173). As noted earlier, since the 1980s, successive Australian governments have conceded that the joint facilities expose Australia to being a likely nuclear target, but have also emphasised that this is outweighed by the contribution these bases make to national security. The question of political risk resulting from dependence on US intelligence product is harder to address because it is not something that can be easily gauged. While the
relative scale of US intelligence-collection assets and analytical capabilities surpasses that of other states by a substantial margin, whether Washington leverages this to exert dominance over or ‘entrap’ its allies in commitments they would otherwise avoid remains an open question. As Michael Wesley (2006) has observed, this perception was encouraged by the Iraq War experience, and in particular by the fact that ‘perhaps 97 per cent of the raw intelligence on Iraq’s WMD [weapons of mass destruction] was
sourced from American and British collectors’. The Howard government’s decision to use this material to support its own case for joining the US-led coalition on the basis of an alleged Iraqi WMD threat promoted a view that Australia was not sufficiently independent in the realm of intelligence (Barker 2003). Yet states make foreign policy according to a wide range of factors. Insofar as intelligence plays a role in shaping foreign policy choices, it is the assessments, as distinct from the unfiltered material and
information, which are the crucial variable. Canada and New Zealand both had access to the same intelligence sources through the Five Eyes network as Australia, but chose not to participate in the US-led invasion of Iraq. For Australia, there were many other reasons to be part of the invasion force, not least the quest for a free trade agreement with the USA. There may be an argument that the collection and provision of overwhelming amounts of raw intelligence by one state can serve to skew the assessments
of others, but the argument that this alone risks trapping a junior ally into committing to a conflict cannot be sustained. In the area of intelligence analysis, ‘Australian assessments of international (and especially Asian) developments differ sharply at times from those of the United States as a result of Australian diplomatic reporting, or specialist expertise, or the force of national or political interests’ (Gyngell and Wesley 2007, 139). While the implied risks of entrapment may be minimal for Australia as a junior
Five Eyes partner, another prominent alliance dynamic—intra-alliance bargaining—has been a feature of the relationship. As it implies, intra-alliance bargaining entails discussions over specific terms of the operation of an alliance. This can be distinguished from negotiations over a treaty or agreement that establishes or redefines an alliance. Intra-alliance bargaining is part of the cut and thrust of alliance interaction and typically involves junior partners seeking specific support or assurances from major-power
allies. Despite evident political tensions between Washington and Canberra over the provision of US assurances to Australia in the build-up to the International Force for East Timor mission in 1999, requests from Australian authorities for increased intelligence support were met favourably by Washington (Cohen and O’Neil 2015, 39-44). This included the deployment in proximity to East Timor of US intelligence-gathering assets in the air and at sea, as well as the reported delivery of a host of heavy-duty
electronic equipment to Australian intelligence agencies in Canberra (Ball 2001b, 51). Conclusion For smaller states in international relations, alliances bring benefits, but they also raise potential risks. Material power imbalances in relation to senior alliance
partners mean that junior partners remain vulnerable to manipulation that is antithetical to their national interests. Wishing to retain the benefits derived from being allied to a stronger military power with like-minded world views, junior allies historically have often found themselves bending to the will of major powers. Junior alliance partners accept the diminution of strategic autonomy in exchange for direct material benefits, such as the provision of military equipment on favourable terms, as well as
reassurances that the major power will come to their aid if they are threatened or attacked by an adversary. For junior partners, a large part of alliance management involves minimising their own vulnerability to being manipulated while simultaneously maximising the gains they receive from being allied to materially stronger countries. US then patently structured its nuclear forces into a strategic triad, dividing its nuclear arsenal more or less equitably between air-launched nuclear
weapons, land-based and submarine-launched ballistic missiles. This was to ensure that even if the US was targeted by a massive Soviet nuclear attack,
the former would have enough survivable second-strike capability that could bring upon the latter unacceptable levels of damage. However, the Soviet
Union’s nuclear force structure, based principally on its landbased ballistic missiles and retaining only a small proportion of nuclear weapons in strategic
bombers and submarines, suggested that it did not subscribe to MAD. Rather, this force structure suggested that the Soviet Union believed in a doctrine
whereby in the event of a superpower war, it would launch a massive attack against the US designed to completely destroy the latter. In other words,
MAD might have plausibly persuaded the US that it did not need to initiate nuclear war; the same cannot be definitively said of the Soviet Union. From

first announced in 1947, articulated U.S. commitments to counteract communism worldwide, and set the stage for America’s strategic postures
in East Asia. The moment communist troops invaded South Korea on June 25, 1950, President Truman ordered the 7th Fleet to sail into the

islands from U.S. protection plans––which would be most vigorously pushed for during the Eisenhower Administration, especially by Secretary

under the U.S.-friendly President Chiang Kai-shek. It also dominated America’s foreign policy debates for many years. The most serious foreign

Last but not least, abandoning Taiwan would destroy the delicate balance between deterrence and
reassurance, a balance that is critical to regional stability. Current US policy toward Taiwan aims to achieve dual
deterrence. Aside from deterring China from attacking Taiwan, Washington also seeks to deter Taiwan from
taking political initiatives that could destabilize the situation.52 Deterrence alone, however, is not
sufficient to maintain the peace. Effective deterrence requires both coercive threats and credible reassurances. In addition to
issuing the threats, the deterring state also needs to reassure the target state that if it does not alter the
status quo, its core interests would not be deprived. Otherwise, the target will have no incentives to heed

the threats. As Thomas Schelling points out, “any coercive threat requires corresponding assurances.”53
Thomas Christensen notes that successful deterrence requires “a mix of credible threats and credible
reassurances” about the conditions under which those threats would be carried out.54 The same logic applies to dual
deterrence. In such a situation, the deterring state needs to deter not one but two states from destabilizing the status quo. On the one
hand, the deterring state communicates coercive threats to both target states about not changing the status quo. On the other hand, it needs to
reassure the target states that their interests will not be sacrificed if they do not take steps to change the status quo. Without
reassurance that their interests would not be jeopardized, the target states would have no incentives to
abide by the deterrent threats. The 1995–1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis demonstrates the imperatives of
maintaining a delicate balance between deterrence and reassurance. When Washington decided to
issue a visa to Taiwan President Lee Teng-hui to visit his alma mater, Cornell University, in 1995, it deassured Beijing
about the US commitment to the one-China policy. Beijing responded to “creeping Taiwan independence” by
launching missiles off Taiwan’s ports, prompting Washington to dispatch two aircraft carriers to the
region. In the aftermath of the crisis, President Bill Clinton attempted to calm Beijing’s fear while visiting Shanghai in 1998 by publicly
announcing the “Three Noes” (no support of Taiwan independence; no support of “two Chinas” or “one China, one Taiwan”; and no support of
Taiwan’s membership in any international organization that requires statehood). But he overcorrected. As Andrew Nathan points out, this
“intentional tilt toward Beijing” was counterproductive. It led President Lee to harden Taiwan’s position
by declaring that cross-Strait relations were akin to “special state to-state relationship.” Tensions quickly escalated, with Beijing
freezing all contacts with Taiwan. Nathan concludes, “The United States’ policy of reassuring Beijing has also deassured
