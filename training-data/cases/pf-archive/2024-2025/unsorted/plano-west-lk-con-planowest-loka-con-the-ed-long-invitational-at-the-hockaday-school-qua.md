Sacks 23 [David Sacks, 6-20-2023, "Why Is Taiwan Important to the United States?", Council on Foreign Relations, https://www.cfr.org/blog/why-taiwan-important-united-states]//scooper
China is intensifying its military, economic, and diplomatic coercion of Taiwan, which it considers a piece of lost territory that must be returned, by force if necessary. While a war between China and the United States over Taiwan is neither imminent nor inevitable, rising tensions raise important first-order questions that need to be addressed: Why does Taiwan matter and why should Americans care about its fate? How would Chinese aggression against Taiwan impact the United States? What, if anything, can and should be done to protect U.S. interests? While the United States is thousands of miles from <mark>Taiwan</mark>, the island’s fate <mark>will have major implications for U.S. security and prosperity.</mark> What happens in the Taiwan Strait will also bear on fundamental questions of international order and the future of democracy. Our recent Council on Foreign Relations-sponsored Independent Task Force Report, U.S.-Taiwan Relations in a New Era: Responding to a More Assertive China, explains that the United States has vital strategic interests at stake in the Taiwan Strait and examines how the United States should protect these interests. <mark>Taiwan sits in an important position in the world’s most economically consequential region.</mark> As Assistant Secretary of Defense Ely Ratner noted, “<mark>Taiwan is located at a critical node within the first island chain, anchoring a network of U.S. allies and partners</mark>—stretching from the Japanese archipelago down to the Philippines and into the South China Sea—<mark>that is critical to the region’s security</mark> and critical to the defense of vital U.S. interests in the Indo-Pacific.” Taiwan’s inherent military value cannot be wished away. Instead, its location dictates that <mark>its fate will in large part determine the balance of power in the region.</mark> With Taiwan outside of its control and U.S. allies and partners arrayed throughout the first island chain, China’s military will struggle to project power far beyond China’s shores. However, if China were to annex Taiwan and base military assets, such as underwater surveillance devices, submarines, and air defense units on the island, it would be able to limit the U.S. military’s operations in the region and, subsequently, its ability to defend its Asian allies. <mark>With Taiwan under China’s control, it would be far more difficult for the United States to maintain a balance of power in the Indo-Pacific</mark> or prevent a Chinese bid for regional dominance. What happens in the Taiwan Strait will have enormous implications for the future of U.S. alliances in the region, which constitute Washington’s most important asymmetric advantage over Beijing. If the United States chose to stand aside in the face of Chinese aggression against Taiwan and China successfully annexed the island, it would be only seventy miles from Japanese territory and 120 miles from the Philippines. U.S. allies would come to question whether the United States would or even could come to their defense. Having lost confidence in the U.S. commitment to their security, allies would contemplate either accommodating China or hedging against it by growing their militaries or even developing nuclear weapons. Either outcome would result in diminished U.S. influence and increased regional and global instability. A Chinese attack on Taiwan, regardless of its success or whether the United States chose to intervene, would also trigger a global economic depression and shave trillions of dollars off global economic output. <mark>Taiwanese companies manufacture nearly 70 percent of the world’s semiconductors and around 90 percent of the most advanced chips. </mark>If the world loses Taiwan’s production capacity, no other company will be able to fill the gap in the short term. During a Chinese blockade or attack, the production and shipment of semiconductors would come to a halt, leading to a shortage of nearly every product that contains technology, from smartphones to computers and cars. Companies across a range of industries would have to reduce or even halt production. <mark>Taiwan’s fate also has implications for the most fundamental tenets of international order.</mark> Following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, if China were to successfully absorb Taiwan despite Taiwanese resistance, it would establish a pattern of authoritarian countries using force to attack democratic neighbors and change borders. One of the most basic pillars of international relations—that countries cannot use force to alter borders—would be severely undermined. Politically, <mark>Taiwan is one of Asia’s few democratic success stories</mark> and by some measures the region’s freest society. Its open political system demonstrates to China’s citizens that there is an alternative path of development for a majority ethnically Chinese society. If China were to take Taiwan by force, Taiwan’s democracy would be extinguished, and its twenty-three million people would see their rights severely curtailed. As this would come in the wake of China’s crackdown on democracy in Hong Kong, the ramifications would be even greater. The stakes are clear, which is why the United States needs to redouble its efforts to deter China from using force or coercion to achieve unification with Taiwan. While a military confrontation in the Taiwan Strait is neither imminent nor inevitable, the chances of one are increasing. U.S. policy toward Taiwan needs to evolve to contend with a more capable, assertive, and risk-acceptant China that is increasingly dissatisfied with the status quo.

A hefty inflationary shock may be in the cards if the supply chain crumbles due to conflict. The economic stakes for the South China Sea dispute are more than just the area’s utility as a trade route. The region has incredible energy potential as well. Unfortunately, this saga threatens to upend the adoption of renewable energy by Southeast Asian countries. Vietnam and the Philippines have vast offshore wind energy potential, with 160 GW up for grabs on the Vietnamese coastline and 178 GW for the Philippines. Traditional hydrocarbons are also plentiful, with the US Energy Information Agency estimating the region to hold over 40 trillion cubic feet of LNG and 3.5 billion barrels of crude oil. This potential will be untapped if China continues behaving aggressively in these waters. Washington has not been absent from this dispute. Since the 2016 ruling, the U.S. has been attempting to uphold the integrity of the verdict. Routine freedom of navigation missions and endeavors to elevate ties with former adversaries like Vietnam showcase this. But the U.S. can’t go at it alone, as it already struggles to juggle all its global commitments. The fact that <mark>America’s defense capacity</mark> worldwide <mark>is declining, and</mark> its ability to field and repair naval vessels is limited only reinforces this point. Luckily, recent regional developments offer hopeful signs, such as a Filipino-Vietnamese deal on maritime collaboration and a recent warming of ties between Japan and South Korea. This latter development is vital, as together Japan and South Korea account for 46.49 % of global shipbuilding capacity, bolstered by leading regional naval manufacturers such as Hanwha Ocean (formerly Daewoo Shipbuilding and Marine Engineering), Hyundai Heavy Industries, and Mitsubishi Heavy Industries, almost tying China’s 46.59 %. Without initiatives like this, countries like the Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei, and Taiwan will ultimately succumb to Chinese coercion. Japan and South Korea have the capacity to manufacture aircraft for the defense of the region as well, with companies like Korea Aerospace Industries and Subaru Corporation growing to meet the threat posed by China. Even if they band together, <mark>Beijing’s naval</mark> and aerial <mark>capabilities would dwarf those of its neighbors</mark>. Harnessing joint capabilities is the only way to overcome this and will require that the U.S. use its convening power to ensure the growth of these defense ties. Paying particular focus to maximizing joint production and interoperability is essential. In other areas, Washington continues to come up short. The Trans-Pacific Partnership trade deal would have given in-region participants better access to the U.S. market and, in doing so, reduced Chinese leverage. In 2017, then-President Donald Trump pulled the U.S. out of TPP. While President Biden’s announced Indo-Pacific Economic Framework for Prosperity (IPEFP) is a step in the right direction, the overall protectionist turn of American politics dampens the utility of such a bloc. The IPEFP is not so much an agreement as a promise to negotiate, and it is a lackluster one since, by all indications, the Biden administration is unlikely to offer expanded market access. As should be clear, China gets away with aggression because of its economic prowess and sheer size; thus, anything that minimizes its gravitational pull should be on the table. Changing course won’t be easy, as conflicts in Ukraine, the Red Sea, and Israel/Gaza have made clear that the U.S.-defined post-Cold War moment is over, and expansionism and irredentism pursued by authoritarian powers have replaced it. At best, the refusal on the part of the U.S. and the Western allies to raise defense spending is a mistake; at worst, it will fuel more conflict. This isn’t helped by a myopic system of cooperation and arms sales, which delays foreign military sales. Turning countries like Taiwan into “porcupines,” or supporting nations that take on larger foes via asymmetric means, should be a priority. American companies like Huntington Ingalls Industries, Lockheed Martin LMT -1.3%, GE Aerospace, and RTX can deliver naval vessels and missile strike capabilities to achieve this goal with additional defense spending. The U.S. needs to lead in shoring up security in the South China Sea before a significant disruption harms the American and global economy and hamstrings our allies. The time for dithering and delays is over. If America doesn’t wake up to unpleasant realities, it will sleepwalk into disaster.

It is already perfectly clear that China is on a trajectory that has bucked all academic assumptions about the rise of a middle class and the attendant democratization of that country. Instead, <mark>China has accumulated power</mark> so as <mark>to challenge the</mark> longstanding Western-led <mark>liberal order</mark> and its institutions. More than ever, as part of an axis of revisionist, repressive states like Russia and Iran, it has drawn the contours of a clash of ideologies on a global scale – a new cold war that forces all of us to choose sides. The <mark>abandonment</mark> of one of Asia’s great success stories in democratization, of a country whose economy is increasingly important to ongoing efforts to build a new global supply chain as an alternative to the current one (which has become unhealthily reliant on a mercantilist authoritarian giant), <mark>would be</mark> as foolish as it would be <mark>self-defeating</mark>. Not only would <mark>abandoning Taiwan</mark> be morally wrong and risk fuelling fears of similar abandonment in other countries that fall within China’s coveted “sphere of influence,” it <mark>would</mark> also <mark>demonstrate collective weakness in the face of aggression</mark>. <mark>It would reward rules-breaking and belligerence</mark>, <mark>empowering a</mark> political <mark>party</mark> <mark>that</mark> not only represses its own people, but that, arguably, <mark>constitutes the greatest danger to international stability</mark> and the rules-based order. As they choose their positions in this world of great power rivalry, Taiwan’s neighbours need to realize that helping Taiwan deter Chinese aggression, and getting involved in defending Taiwan should that deterrence fail, isn’t simply an exercise in altruism. More than ever, countering the CCP’s darker ambitions and, where possible, encouraging the better angels of its nature, is also a rational decision for each to make in the interest of their own national security. Whatever their motives, advocates of Taiwan’s abandonment seek to constrain our imagination. They want us to believe, despite all the evidence to the contrary, that we live in a fantastical world of a China that never came to be, and that this imagined China would remain even after the monumental folly that would be the “gifting” of Taiwan to the PRC.
