A Further Problem of Selective Prosecution There are two other causes of selective prosecution. The ICC has very little in the way of
resources for pursuing investigations on its own. It is essentially dependent on the cooperation and
support of states to pursue these. It is dependent on financial support, intelligence gathering, and gathering of information in conflict zones by powerful
states. It simply cannot investigate or prosecute crimes without this support. But states, members and non-members, offer support in accordance often with
their own interests or the interests of allies. Hence, one cause of selective prosecution is that the prosecutor may have little or no hope of collecting the necessary
evidence without the support of one of the major powers. And so it may choose not to investigate a case for that reason
alone. A second reason is that one secures the good will of the major powers by not investigating
their nationals or those of their allies and by only pursuing cases that the major supporters favor. This establishes a kind of inherent bias in
the process of investigation that systematically explains the lack of proportionate equality in prosecution. So we have three fundamental causes of the fact that the
ICC only investigates parties that are not major powers or their allies and only investigates situations which major powers favor. The first is that the major powers are
not party to the treaty; the second is that the court can only investigate a situation if major powers give it help; the third is that the court must seek the good will of
the major powers to do its work and avoiding prosecution of the major powers and their allies is a principal condition of that good will. The first two conditions are,
in a sense, external to the court. The first involves a legal constraint on the court, the second involves the difficulty of getting evidence. A court can be doing the best
it can in the circumstances and these first two conditions will provide a limit. The third condition is more internal to the court. The prosecutor will exercise discretion
in a way that favors the major supporters of the court in order to secure their support. She does so for obvious reasons that have some weight but she nevertheless
makes decisions in a way that is systematically biased in favor of the courts major supporters. These three conditions are distinct and have different causes but I
want to argue that they share an underlying unity. They involve ways in which the court’s actions are systematically biased in favor of the major powers and their
allies. The bias I am alleging is not racial bias but it is a kind of invidious bias that undermines the claim to impartiality of the court. It is a bias that is akin to
nepotistic bias. The bias implies a kind of impunity for the major powers and their friends.

justice” debate by examining the ICC's impact on civil conflict termination. Active ICC involvement in a conflict increases the
threat of punishment for rebel and state leaders, which, under certain conditions, generates incentives

prosecution. The impact of ICC involvement is conditional upon the threat of domestic punishment

### Embedded weighing
ABSENT: The case lacks pre-written impact calculus that compares its two core impacts (corruption/legitimacy collapse vs. conflict prolongation/violence escalation) or weighs them against likely aff impacts like deterrence or victim justice. No card explicitly frames "magnitude," "probability," or "timeframe" in comparative terms. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "ICC legitimacy collapse outweighs — selective prosecution creates systemic impunity for major powers, which is a larger magnitude harm than isolated deterrence failures — Christiano 21" and sit at the end of Contention 1 or as a standalone "Weighing" module.

### Offensive spikes
**Card:** Mitrovica 23 (Al Jazeera) — "The US applauds the ICC when it charges Russia with war crimes and excoriates the same body when it starts looking into whether America committed war crimes in Afghanistan and Israel against besieged Palestinians."

### Defensive spikes
**Card:** Wheeler 22 (Opinio Juris) — "The actions and statements of the last five presidential administrations suggest that they do not fully understand what the ICC actually does and that they are not particularly interested in finding out. It also frontlines "U.S. funding improves ICC effectiveness" by implying U.S. involvement deepens politicization, not legitimacy.

### Evidence quality
**Strong card:** Prorok 17 (Cambridge Core / International Organization) — "Active ICC involvement in a conflict increases the threat of punishment for rebel and state leaders, which... generates incentives for these leaders to continue the conflict as a way to avoid capture, transfer to the Hague, and prosecution."
**Why it wins tiebreaks:** Peer-reviewed, longitudinal dataset (2002–2013, all civil conflict dyads), addresses selection/endogeneity, published in *International Organization* (top IR journal). Author is a political scientist with quantitative methodology — far stronger than the think-tank reports and op-eds that dominate the rest of the doc.

**Weak card:** Genocide Watch 24 (undated PDF, no author, "No Publication" listed) — tag claims "several countries on the brink of genocide" but evidence is a raw list of country warnings with no causal link to ICC actions. Would lose an evidence-comparison round to any aff card with a dated, authored, peer-reviewed study on atrocity trends.

### Collapse flexibility
**Contention 1 (Corruption/Selective Justice) offers two independent link chains:**
1. U.S. hypocrisy/weaponization link — Mitrovica 23, Pearson 13, Madhani 25 show U.S. uses ICC selectively → ICC becomes tool of U.S. foreign policy → legitimacy collapse (Christiano 21, Wheeler 22, Amnesty 22).
2. Structural bias link — Christiano 21 identifies three independent mechanisms (non-membership of major powers, evidence dependence on major powers, prosecutor discretion to secure major-power goodwill) that *each* produce selective prosecution regardless of U.S. intent.
**Contention 2 (Counterproductive) offers a distinct impact scenario:** Prorok 17 shows ICC *prolongs* civil conflicts via leader survival incentives → Brahm 23 shows U.S. entry adds mandatory funding → more investigations → more conflict prolongation → Bellamy 18 frames as global violence escalation.
**Execution:** The case can collapse to either contention, or to either link chain within Contention 1, without conceding the other. No single link is necessary for the other to stand.

### Warrant depth
**Strong warrant:** Christiano 21 — tag "three fundamental causes... share an underlying unity... systematically biased in favor of the major powers and their allies" is backed by a 3-step analytical warrant in the card text: (1) major powers not party → legal constraint; (2) court needs major-power help for evidence → resource constraint; (3) prosecutor seeks major-power goodwill → discretionary bias. Each step is explained, not just asserted.

**Weak warrant:** ICC 23 Rules card — tag "2023 Rules bolster provisions to enable reporting mechanisms that play a key role in uncovering and deterring wrongdoing" cites the ICC's own press release describing its own new rules. No independent verification that reporting mechanisms *actually* uncover/deter corruption; the card is a claim by the rule-maker about its own rules. A warrant would require empirical study of whistleblower efficacy under the 2011 vs. 2023 rules.

### Internal consistency
**Tension:** Contention 1 argues the ICC is *already* a politicized tool of major powers (Christiano 21: "systematically biased in favor of the major powers and their allies"; Wheeler 22: "engaging with the Court as a tool to be used against others"). Contention 2 argues U.S. joining *adds* funding that *increases* ICC investigations (Brahm 23 → Prorok 17), implying the ICC currently has *fewer* investigations than it would with U.S. funding — but if the ICC is already structurally biased toward major-power interests (Contention 1), more funding may just mean *more biased investigations*, not more investigations overall. The case doesn't resolve whether U.S. entry changes the *selection* of cases (toward U.S. enemies) or just the *volume*.
