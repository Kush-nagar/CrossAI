Introduction
<mark>Education</mark>, being a social institution serving the needs of society, <mark>is indispensable for society to survive</mark> and thrive. <mark>It </mark>should be not only comprehensive, sustainable, and superb, but <mark>must continuously evolve to meet</mark> the challengesof <mark>the fast-changing and unpredictable</mark> globalized <mark>world</mark>. This evolution must be systemic, consistent, andscalable; therefore, school teachers, college professors, administrators, researchers, and <mark>policy makers</mark> are expected to <mark>innovate</mark> the theory and practice of <mark>teaching</mark> and learning, as well as all other aspects of this complex organization <mark>to ensure quality preparation</mark> of all students to life and work.
Here we present a systemic discussion of educational innovations, identify the barriers to innovation, and outline potential directions for effective innovations. We discuss the current status of innovations in US education, what educational innovation is, how innovations are being integrated in schools and colleges, why innovations do not always produce the desired effect, and what should be done to increase the scale and rate of innovation-based transformations in our education system. We then offer recommendations for the growth of educational innovations. As examples of innovations in education, we will highlight online learning and time efficiency of learning using accelerated and intensive approaches.

Studies prove AI worsens educational outcomes.
Angie Basiouny, Writer and editor at the Wharton School of UPenn, August 27, 2024, Knowledge at Wharton, “Without Guardrails, Generative AI Can Harm Education”, [https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education/] winky
<mark>A new study</mark> led <mark>by</mark> researchers at <mark>Wharton</mark> and Penn <mark>reveals that</mark> using <mark>gen</mark>erative <mark>AI improves</mark> student <mark>performance, but</mark> also <mark>makes it harder for students to learn and acquire new skills.</mark> The researchers designed an experiment with nearly 1,000 high school math students in Turkey to determine whether large language models can harm or help their education. <mark>One group</mark> of students <mark>was given GPT Base</mark>, a chat interface similar to ChatGPT-4, to help them during practice sessions. The co-authors are Osbert Bastani, computer and information science professor with Penn Engineering; Alp Sungu, operations, information and decisions professor at Wharton; Haosen Ge, data scientist at the Wharton AI and Analytics Initiative; Özge Kabakcı, math teacher at Budapest British International School; and independent researcher Rei Mariman. The Generative AI Paradox and How It Impacts Education The paper’s finding is consistent with similar studies, and Hamsa Bastani said <mark>it reflects the paradox</mark> of generative AI: <mark>It can make tasks easier</mark> for people <mark>while simultaneously deteriorating</mark> their <mark>abilities to learn the skills required to solve those tasks.</mark> “We’ve been really interested in how humans interact with algorithms for a while. But I think it gets really interesting with large language models just because of the extent of their reach and the number of people who are using them with such a diversity of tasks,” she said. “One thing that really drew us to this conversation was a lot of teachers are struggling with students copying answers from homework, and they were worried that this would negatively impact their skill-building and their fundamental understanding of concepts. That’s why we decided to dig into this.” “If we use it sort of lazily and … completely trust the machine learning model, then that’s when we could be in trouble.” — Hamsa Bastani The study also found that students who used AI assistance were overly optimistic about their learning capabilities, even the high-achieving students. Teachers, on the other hand, seem to be overly concerned and tend to dismiss the advantages of AI. Bastani thinks that’s because students and teachers aren’t yet trained on how to use AI effectively to augment traditional teaching methods. Bastani and her colleagues said <mark>the study is a “cautionary tale” about deploying AI in education</mark>al settings, and they remind everyone that the software still has significant limitations. Chat<mark>GPT</mark>, for example, <mark>is known to spit out false information known as hallucinations, which can also potentially harm student learning.</mark> Just like in a workplace setting, generative AI in the classroom still requires a lot of human finesse and fact-checking to make it valuable, Bastani said. “If we are thinking of this tool as an assistant and doing the higher-level tasks, checking its outputs and so on, it can be a huge benefit,” she said. “But if we use it sort of lazily and kind of outsource the work that we’re supposed to be doing and completely trust the machine learning model, then that’s when we could be in trouble.”

### Warrant depth
**Mixed.** The **Basiouny 24** card provides deep warrants: experimental design (three groups, practice vs exam performance), mechanism explanation (Base group "depended on software to solve problems and didn't learn underlying mathematical concepts"), paradox framing (easier tasks but deteriorated skill acquisition), and expert quotation ("cautionary tale"). The **Kerr 24** card provides quantified warrants with specific sources (Goldman Sachs report, Google/Microsoft sustainability reports, Washington Post, Bloomberg). The **Evans 24** card offers mechanistic warrants for each claim (cost: "hiring a real human is way cheaper"; overtrust: Stack Overflow decline → knowledge base erosion; motivation: "why would I study if AI is smarter?"; soft skills: "misses opportunity to work in a group"; standardization: "1,000 teachers is not that large a sample"; non-human: "unable to do something beyond lector role"). However, the **Serdyukov 17** card is largely claim-dense without specific warrants for *why* education is "indispensable for society to survive" — it asserts but does not prove the causal link between education and societal survival. The **Williamson 24** card asserts "black box" opacity and "curriculum misinfo" but provides no empirical examples or studies — just conceptual claims. The **McGuigan 21** card cites "some computer science researchers" for the 5-cars training emission claim without naming the study — warrant by vague authority.

### Internal consistency
**Consistent.** Both contentions share a unified framing: **AI creates illusion of efficiency while generating hidden long-term costs** — in education, the illusion of personalized learning masks skill atrophy and motivation loss; in environment, the illusion of optimization masks emissions surges and fossil fuel acceleration. No contention undercuts the other: Evans 24's "AI degrades over time without human expertise" aligns with McGuigan 21's "AI provides legitimizing halo for extraction." Williamson 24's "operational opacity limits accountability" parallels Kerr 24's "companies don't disclose emissions." The case maintains a cohesive narrative: **AI externalizes costs (educational, environmental) while concentrating benefits in corporate hands.** The only minor tension: Evans 24 argues AI is *currently* more expensive than humans, while Kerr 24 implies AI deployment is *rapidly scaling* despite cost — but this reflects different domains (education vs compute infrastructure) and doesn't create a logical contradiction.
