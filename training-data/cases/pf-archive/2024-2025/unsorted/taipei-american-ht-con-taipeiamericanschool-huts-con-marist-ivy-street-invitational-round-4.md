The federal government has already met the border security benchmarks laid down in earlier Senate immigration reform bills. • As the American Immigration Lawyers Association pointed out in a January 2013 analysis, the “benchmarks” for border security specified in the bipartisan 2006, 2007, and 2010 immigration reform legislative packages in the Senate have been largely met.23 Yet each time a benchmark was met, Congress continued to fund ever-greater levels of enforcement without significant increases in adjudication or passage of broader reforms to the immigration system. • The requirements in those <mark>Senate bills for more border</mark> enforcement personnel, border fencing, <mark>surveillance technology</mark>, unmanned aerial vehicles, and detention beds <mark>have</mark> also <mark>been fulfilled and</mark> in many ways <mark>surpassed</mark>.24 ICE detention bed funding reached a record level in FY 2024, funding 41,500 beds, the third highest level in Congressional history.25 And when Congress authorizes ICE to increase detention beds, the agency often detains more than funded. For example, despite Congress appropriating enough money for 40,520 beds throughout the year<mark>, ICE’s use of budgetary mechanisms</mark> in FY 2019 to <mark>shift money from other locations to their detention account led to ICE breaking records by detaining more than 55,000 people</mark>.26 Border security depends on the smart and efficient use of available resources. At the same, border enforcement cannot and should not be done in isolation. Instead, it must be examined in the larger context of reforms needed for the entire immigration system. There is a Serious Enforcement Adjudication Funding Mismatch Despite pouring hundreds of billions of dollars into immigration enforcement over the last two decades, <mark>Congress has </mark>often<mark> failed to provide similar funding </mark>infusions<mark> to the</mark> immigration <mark>court</mark> system. Taken together, from FY 2003 through 2024, <mark>Congress has spent $24 on ICE</mark> and Border Patrol <mark>for every $1 spent on </mark>the immigration <mark>court</mark> system. Over that 22-year period, Congress spent $11.65 billion on the immigration courts (see Figure 3)—$3.5 billion less than the Trump administration spent on border walls alone.27 Given this funding mismatch, the immigration court’s backlog of cases has risen every single year since FY 2007. <mark>As of</mark> the middle of FY <mark>2024, there were more than 3.7 million cases pending</mark> in the immigration court system, an increase of over 2000 percent since 2007. Despite the demand, as of the first quarter of FY 2024, there were only 725 immigration judges across the entire country for an average of nearly 5,000 cases per judge.28 <mark>So long as Congress continues to supercharge enforcement</mark> at the expense of adjudication, these <mark>backlogs will likely persist</mark>, and the funding poured into enforcement will not produce the results intended.

In Michigan, a man in the custody of Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) was sent into a jail's general population unit with an open wound from surgery, no bandages and no follow-up medical appointment scheduled, even though he still had surgical drains in place. A federal inspector found: "The detainee never received even the most basic care for his wound." In Georgia, a nurse ignored an ICE detainee who urgently asked for an inhaler to treat his asthma. Even though he was never examined by the medical staff, the nurse put a note in the medical record that "he was seen in sick call." "The documentation by the nurse bordered on falsification and the failure to see a patient urgently requesting medical attention regarding treatment with an inhaler was negligent." And in Pennsylvania, a group of correctional officers strapped a mentally ill male ICE detainee into a restraint chair and gave the lone female officer a pair of scissors to cut off his clothes for a strip search. "There is no justifiable correctional reason that required the detainee who had a mental health condition to have his clothes cut off by a female officer while he was compliant in a restraint chair. This is a barbaric practice and clearly violates ... basic principles of humanity." These findings are all part of a trove of more than 1,600 pages of previously <mark>secret inspection reports</mark> written by experts hired by the Department of Homeland Security's Office for Civil Rights and Civil Liberties. In examining more than two dozen facilities across 16 states from 2017 to 2019, these expert inspectors <mark>found "negligent" medical care</mark> (including mental health care), <mark>"unsafe and filthy" conditions, racist abuse of detainees, inappropriate pepper-spraying of mentally ill detainees and </mark>other problems that, in some cases, <mark>contribute</mark>d <mark>to detainee deaths</mark>. These reports almost never become public. For more than three years, the federal government — under both the Trump and Biden administrations — fought NPR's efforts to obtain those records. That opposition continued despite a Biden campaign promise to "demand transparency in and independent oversight over ICE." The records were obtained in response to a Freedom of Information Act lawsuit brought by NPR. After two years, a federal judge found that the government had violated the nation's public records law and ordered the release of the documents. The reports provide an unprecedented look at the ICE detention system through the eyes of experts hired to investigate complaints of civil rights abuses, who provide an often unvarnished perspective. These experts have specific expertise in subjects such as medicine, mental health, use of force and environmental health. Sources familiar with these inspections tell NPR that they often uncover problems that other government inspectors miss. "These reports are chilling. They are damning," said Eunice Cho, senior staff attorney at the American Civil Liberties Union's National Prison Project and an expert on ICE detention, when NPR shared the reports' findings. "They really show how the government's own inspectors can see the abuses and the level of abuses that are happening in ICE detention." The reports obtained by NPR depict a wide spectrum of problems in ICE detention. Most immigration detention facilities are managed by private, for-profit corporations that contract with the government, including GEO Group and CoreCivic. Local jails, typically operated by county sheriff's departments, also enter into contracts with the government to hold detainees on behalf of ICE. Legally, immigration detention is considered civil — not criminal — in nature. "Detention is non-punitive," ICE states on its website. <mark>Immigration detention is primarily for holding people who are awaiting the adjudication of their immigration cases. That</mark> can <mark>include immigrants </mark>apprehended at the border who are <mark>seeking asylum; people who entered</mark> the U.S. <mark>illegally and whom the government wants to deport</mark> or deems a public safety risk; and permanent residents who are hit with deportation orders. The main goal of ICE detention is to make sure immigrants show up for their court dates. But the conditions revealed in the inspection reports often appear indistinguishable from prison. The <mark>inspectors found</mark> what they described as <mark>racist harassment of immigrants and retaliation against detainees who filed complaints</mark>. "Examples of mistreatment include a Sergeant entering the female unit and greeting the female detainees by yelling, 'Hello a**holes and bitches,'" an inspector found at the Orange County Jail in Goshen, New York. "Multiple staff make comments such as, if detainees do not like the treatment, they should not have come to our country. A [correctional officer] working in a male unit confronted a group of detainees stating, 'Who's the f***ing p**** who made the complaint against me?'"

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanisms (magnitude, probability, timeframe, or scope comparisons) built into taglines or card text. Cards simply assert harms (backlogs, rights violations, detention length) without pre-written framing for why those impacts outweigh probable Pro impacts like deterrence or border security. An embedded weighing card would carry a tag like "Immigration court backlogs affect 3.7M+ people — magnitude dwarfs marginal deterrence gains" citing a comparative analysis (e.g., TRAC data vs. DHS apprehension stats) and belong in Contention 1 after the backlog evidence.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards preempt or interact with likely Pro positions such as "surveillance deters illegal entry," "technology reduces need for physical barriers," or "asylum fraud justifies detention." The case is purely defensive, describing status-quo harms without offensive link turns or preemptive solvency takeouts. An offensive spike would be a card tagged "Surveillance expansion increases asylum filings — more credible fear claims overwhelm courts" (citing USCIS credible-fear data post-2014 surge) placed as a Contention 2 subpoint to turn Pro's deterrence narrative.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case lacks embedded frontlines against foreseeable Con responses like "funding can be increased for both enforcement and courts," "private detention reform solves rights abuses," or "expedited removal reduces backlogs." Cards only describe problems without pre-butting the solvency mechanisms Pro will likely advance. A defensive spike would carry the tag "Congressional appropriations history proves enforcement always crowds out adjudication — $24:$1 ratio persists regardless of total funding" (citing AIC 24 Figure 3 trend data) and sit at the end of Contention 1 to frontline the "more funding solves" argument.

### Evidence quality
**ABSENT:** Evidence quality is uneven and often insufficient for elite evidence-comparison tiebreaks. The American Immigration Council 2024 factsheet (AIC 24) is strong — advocacy org but primary analysis of government FY 2003-2024 budget data with specific ratios ($24:$1) and backlog counts (3.7M). The New York Times piece (Kanno-Youngs 23) is a news report citing ~650 judges and 2M+ backlog but lacks primary dataset access. The NPR investigation (Dreisbach 23) is high-quality investigative journalism with 1,600+ pages of primary inspection reports across 16 states, 2017-2019, but the card only quotes anecdotes, not systemic rates. The AIC 2020 detention-duration report cites a 2013 California class-action dataset (n=unknown) for the 421-day figure — small, geographically limited, and 11 years old. No cards carry author credentials beyond the Dreisbach bio; no peer-reviewed studies, government IG reports, or large-N quantitative analyses. A tiebreak-winning card would be a 2023-2024 DHS OIG or GAO report with national detention-condition metrics (e.g., "GAO-24-106234: 78% of ICE facilities failed medical-care standards in unannounced 2023 inspections").

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case funnels all offense through a single narrative: surveillance → more detention → court backlogs + rights violations. No alternative impact scenarios exist — no economic harm card, no rule-of-law/due-process collapse card, no international-obligations card, no local-community-cost card. If Pro wins that backlogs are solvable via hiring or that rights abuses are outliers, the entire Con offense evaporates. A second independent collapse route would be a Contention 3 tagged "Surveillance spending displaces asylum officer corps — credible fear backlog hits 1M+, creating de facto asylum ban" citing USCIS 2024 quarterly reports, allowing Con to collapse to asylum-access denial even if detention harms are mitigated.

### Warrant depth
**ABSENT:** Most taglines receive claims without mechanistic warrants. "Surveillance increases backlogs" (AIC 24) asserts a $24:$1 spending ratio but does not explain *why* enforcement spending mechanically crowds out court funding (e.g., appropriations committee jurisdiction, mandatory detention statutes). "Lack of judges" (Kanno-Youngs 23) states 650 judges face 2M+ cases but does not warrant *why* judge hiring lags (Senate confirmation bottleneck, EOIR budget structure). "ICE violates basic human rights" (Dreisbach 23) lists three anecdotes but provides no warrant for *systemic* causation (private contractor profit model, lack of enforceable standards, inspection secrecy). "Immigrants spend long periods" (AIC 20) cites a 421-day California average from a 2013 lawsuit but does not warrant *why* duration correlates with surveillance (mandatory detention triggers, bond denial rates, appellate delays). Each card needs a warrant sentence like "Because 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b) mandates detention for arriving asylum seekers, surveillance-driven apprehensions directly populate detention beds without bond hearings."

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** Contention framing is not explicit in the doc, but the four evidence blocks pull in conflicting directions. The backlog cards (AIC 24, Kanno-Youngs 23) imply the *problem* is insufficient adjudication capacity relative to enforcement — suggesting the *solution* is more judges/courts. The rights-violation card (Dreisbach 23) and detention-length card (AIC 20) imply the *problem* is detention itself — suggesting the *solution* is release/alternatives to detention. If Con argues "hire more judges" to solve backlogs, they legitimize the enforcement-surveillance pipeline that fills detention beds, undercutting the rights/duration offense. If Con argues "release detainees" to solve rights abuses, they concede Pro's claim that surveillance enables catch-and-release. No framing card reconciles this tension (e.g., "Surveillance expands the carceral pipeline; neither adjudication nor release solves because mandatory detention statutes bind both"). A consistency card would tag "Mandatory detention statutes (8 USC 1225, 1226) force surveillance apprehensions into beds regardless of court capacity or humanitarian cost — only surveillance reduction breaks the cycle" and sit as a framework/overview card.

**Score: 3/10** — The case assembles relevant topic evidence but lacks every strategic layer of elite construction: no embedded weighing, no offensive/defensive spikes, inconsistent evidence quality, zero collapse flexibility, claim-heavy tags without warrants, and an internal contradiction between adjudication-expansion and detention-reduction offense that the doc never resolves.
