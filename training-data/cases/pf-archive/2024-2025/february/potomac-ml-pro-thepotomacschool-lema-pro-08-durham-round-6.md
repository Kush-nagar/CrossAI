East <mark>Africa</mark> has <mark>experienced</mark> more frequent <mark>droughts</mark> since 2005, occurring <mark>every three years</mark> (Haile et al., Citation2019). Somaliland suffered 11 severe droughts from 1964 to 2017 (Abdulkadir, Citation2017; FAO, Citation2021b). The most devastating events occurred in 1973–1974, 1984, 1991, 2010/2011, and 2016/2017, affecting the Sanaag, Togdheer, and Sool Regions for prolonged periods. These droughts caused damage and losses that exceeded USD 874 million (Pape and Wollburg Citation2019). <mark>Drought is</mark> also <mark>the main cause of livestock deaths</mark> (<mark>up to 60%</mark>) <mark>in Somaliland</mark>, severely affecting pastoralists’ livelihoods (The Integrated Food Security Phase Classification [IPC], Citation2021). According to the United Nations and UNICEF, drought emergencies in Somaliland and Somalia have dire consequences for millions of people. They displaced 771,400 people searching for water, food, and pasture and put more than 1.4 million children at risk of acute malnutrition. The drought impacted 6.1 million Somalis in total (UN News, Citation2022; UNICEF, Citation2022). More than 1.15 million people left their homes between January 2016 and May 2018, often when they were already weak and hungry from the drought (Fanning, Citation2018).¶ Floods are another hazard affecting the livelihoods of Somaliland people. The country has faced more than 30 weather-related disasters since 1990, including 12 droughts and 19 floods (World Bank, Citation2022). Cyclone Sagar in 2018 was one of the worst floods, killing 50 people and affecting 168,000 more in north-western Somaliland. The cyclone brought a year’s worth of rain, destroying most livestock, water systems, health facilities, and schools in some areas (UNICEF, Citation2018). Floods also pose a health risk by contaminating drinking water and causing diarrhea, which was among the top five causes of death in Ethiopia, Eritrea, Somaliland, Somalia, Burundi, Djibouti, Uganda, and South Sudan, across all age groups in 2019 (Carlton et al., Citation2016). Diarrheal diseases are likely to increase with increasing temperature, as common bacterial pathogens multiply faster and last longer at higher temperatures. Abaya et al. (Citation2011) found that climate variability worsened existing health problems in the Somali region of Ethiopia, where most people lack sanitation and safe water.¶ Despite their stability, Somaliland’s financial assets are affected by climate-related events. The FAO (Citation2021b) reported that households faced multiple shocks, such as climate change, reduced income, high food prices, loss of employment, illness, and insecurity, based on The Resilience Index Measurement and Analysis (RIMA) study in Somaliland. According to the International Fund for Animal Welfare (IFAW, Citation2022), many animals are sold desperately in the country. Therefore, climate change and variability pose a serious challenge for developing countries such as Somaliland, where most people have a low adaptive capacity to cope with climate change in their livelihoods (Asfaw et al., Citation2021). These statistics indicate that climate change has had a devastating impact on the livelihoods of the people of Somaliland, especially those who depend on livestock and crop production.¶ In conclusion, Somaliland’s livelihoods require urgent action from the government and international organizations to cope with climate variability. They should collaborate to help vulnerable communities access vital resources and build their resilience to climate change. Resilience is the ability to prevent, anticipate, absorb, accommodate, or recover from disasters and crises in a timely, efficient, and sustainable manner (FAO, Citation2021a). It involves protecting, restoring, and improving livelihood systems that face threats to agriculture, nutrition, food security, and food safety (FAO, Citation2020c). Pastoral communities can improve early warning systems, animal health, livestock restocking, and natural resource management. Agropastoral households promote income diversification and livelihood strategies through training, extension services, market access, and non-farming activities. These measures can reduce poverty by improving food security and nutrition, increasing income levels, sustaining crop production, and mitigating climatic vulnerability (Adem et al., Citation2018; Habib et al., Citation2023).¶ 3.2.
