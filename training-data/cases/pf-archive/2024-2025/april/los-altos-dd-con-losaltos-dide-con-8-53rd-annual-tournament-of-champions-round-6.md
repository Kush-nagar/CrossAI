1. Long Time Lag Between Planning and Operation The time lag between planning and operation of a nuclear reactor includes the times to
identify a site, obtain a site permit, purchase or lease the land, obtain a construction permit, obtain financing and insurance for construction,
install transmission, negotiate a power purchase agreement, obtain permits, build the plant, connect it to transmission, and obtain a final
operating license. The planning-to-operation (PTO) times of all nuclear plants ever built have been 10-19
years or more. For example, the Olkiluoto 3 reactor in Finland was proposed to the Finnish cabinet in December 2000 to be added to an
existing nuclear power plant. Its latest estimated completion date is 2020, giving it a PTO time of 20 years. The Hinkley Point nuclear plant was
planned to start in 2008. It has an estimated the completion year of 2025 to 2027, giving it a PTO time of 17 to 19 years. The Vogtle 3 and 4
reactors in Georgia were first proposed in August 2006 to be added to an existing site. The anticipated completion dates are November 2021
and November 2022, respectively, given them PTO times of 15 and 16 years, respectively. The Haiyang 1 and 2 reactors in China were planned
to start in 2005. Haiyang 1 began commercial operation on October 22, 2018. Haiyang 2 began operation on January 9, 2019, giving them PTO
times of 13 and 14 years, respectively. The Taishan 1 and 2 reactors in China were bid in 2006. Taishan 1 began commercial operation on

December 13, 2018. Taishan 2 is not expected to be connected until 2019, giving them PTO times of 12 and 13 years, respectively. Planning and
procurement for four reactors in Ringhals, Sweden started in 1965. One took 10 years, the second took 11 years, the third took 16 years, and the
fourth took 18 years to complete. Many claim that France’s 1974 Messmer plan resulted in the building of its 58 reactors in 15 years. This is not
true. The planning for several of these nuclear reactors began long before. For example, the Fessenheim reactor obtained its construction
permit in 1967 and was planned starting years before. In addition, 10 of the reactors were completed between 1991-2000. As such, the whole
planning-to-operation time for these reactors was at least 32 years, not 15. That of any individual reactor was 10 to 19 years.

of backup generators using fossil fuels during downtime), fuel processing and waste disposal (14 per cent) and decommissioning (18 per cent)

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case lacks explicit weighing mechanisms such as "probability," "magnitude," "timeframe," or "reversibility" tags embedded in card taglines. No card includes framing like "Outweighs on magnitude — 93M deaths vs. speculative nuclear benefits" or "Timeframe — renewables deploy in 2-5 years vs. 14.5 for nuclear, making nuclear irrelevant to 2030 climate targets." A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Weighing — Timeframe & Magnitude: IPCC 2022 — emissions must peak before 2025 and fall 43% by 2030; nuclear’s 14.5-year build time makes it structurally incapable of contributing to this deadline, while renewables deploy in months — nuclear investment actively delays the only viable pathway" and belong in the "Impact is climate change" / "Nuclear reactors will never meet the climate deadline" section.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case contains no cards that proactively answer common Pro arguments (e.g., "nuclear provides baseload," "new SMRs solve build time," "nuclear complements renewables," "land use/mining impacts of renewables"). No card is tagged to preempt a specific opponent contention. A card filling this gap would be tagged "Spike — SMRs Don’t Solve: DOE 2024 — first commercial SMR deployment not expected until 2030s, same licensing hurdles, no supply chain — too late for 2030 climate targets" and placed after the "Nuclear reactors will never meet the climate deadline" block to frontline the most likely Pro innovation claim.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case has no embedded frontlines answering the specific responses it will face (e.g., "nuclear waste is manageable," "France did it," "intermittency requires nuclear," "subsidies are small relative to IRA"). No card is framed as "Even if [Pro claim], [Con response]." A card filling this gap would carry the tag "Frontline — France Model Not Replicable: Sovacool 2017 / Dascalu 2024 — France’s buildout took 32+ years with centralized state planning, no NIMBY/regulatory barriers, and pre-existing supply chains; US has none of these conditions — affirms crowd-out, not solvency" and belong in the "L1: Direct fund diversion" or "Nuclear reactors will never meet the climate deadline" section.

### Evidence quality
The case mixes high- and low-quality sources. **Strong:** Mark Z. Jacobson, 24, One Earth (peer-reviewed journal, 2024, dataset of all nuclear plants ever built, PTO times 10-19 years) — wins evidence comparison on build-time data. **Strong:** Lori Bird, 25, World Resources Institute (WRI, 2025, cites 2024 EIA capacity data — 90% new capacity renewables, 45 GW solar/wind added) — elite recency and institutional credibility. **Weak:** Washington, 17, Global Research (aggregator site, no author quals beyond citing Sovacool/Lovins; 2017 data cited in 2025 round — outdated for cost/capacity claims). **Weak:** Paul Dvorak, 10, Windpower Engineering & Development (15-year-old trade publication article). **Moderate:** C.Dascalu, 24, CAN Europe (NGO position paper, not peer-reviewed, but 2024 and EU-policy relevant). The case can win evidence tiebreaks on build-time and renewables-growth cards but loses on lifecycle emissions and crowd-out empirics due to aged/secondary sourcing.

### Collapse flexibility
The case offers **three distinct, non-redundant link paths** to the climate impact: (1) **Direct fund diversion** — Dascalu 24 + Washington 17 (Cooper/Dvorak state-level data) show nuclear investment crowds out renewables/efficiency; (2) **Subsidy-driven crowd-out** — Millsap 19 (Forbes) argues subsidies keep uneconomic nuclear online, blocking market entry for cheaper renewables/gas; (3) **Grid incompatibility** — Dascalu 24 shows nuclear’s inflexibility causes curtailment and blocks renewable interconnection. Each link is independently warranted and can be collapsed to separately (e.g., "Even if no new subsidies, grid incompatibility alone kills renewable integration"). This provides genuine collapse flexibility across funding, market, and physical-infrastructure mechanisms.

### Warrant depth
**Mixed.** **Strong warrants:** Jacobson 24 provides a detailed mechanistic warrant for build-time (lists 11-step PTO process, gives 8 reactor examples with specific year ranges). Bird 25 warrants the 90% renewables claim with 2024 EIA capacity data and GW figures. Millsap 19 warrants subsidy crowd-out with EIA/FRED data showing declining generation despite GDP growth. **Weak warrants:** Dascalu 24 asserts "Every euro invested in nuclear is a euro not invested in renewables" without citing a budgetary trade-off study — it’s a claim, not a warrant. Washington 17 (Global Research) cites Sovacool’s meta-analysis but doesn’t explain the 19-study methodology or why the 66 gCO2e/kWh mean is reliable — just repeats the conclusion. The "93 million deaths" claim from Jacobson 24 is a linear extrapolation (7.1M/yr × 14.5 yrs) with no counterfactual baseline — warrant is arithmetic, not epidemiological.

### Internal consistency
**Consistent framing throughout:** Every contention ties to a single cohesive narrative — **nuclear investment actively harms climate mitigation by delaying/supplanting faster, cheaper, cleaner renewables.** Contention 1 (Trade Off) establishes renewables are already winning; Contention 2 (Affirming undermines renewables) gives three mechanisms (fund diversion, subsidies, grid); Contention 3 (Impact) quantifies the climate cost of delay (IPCC deadlines, 93M deaths, higher lifecycle emissions). No contention undercuts another — e.g., the case doesn’t argue "nuclear is unsafe" (which would distract) or "nuclear is expensive" without linking to crowd-out. The Dascalu 24 card appears in both L1 and L3 but with distinct taglines (fund diversion vs. grid incompatibility), avoiding double-counting. Uniform "opportunity cost" framing from start to finish.

### Overall construction score
**Score: 6/10** — The case excels at collapse flexibility (three independent, well-warranted links) and maintains strong internal consistency around a clear opportunity-cost narrative, with two elite pieces of evidence (Jacobson 24, Bird 25) that can win key evidence comparisons.
