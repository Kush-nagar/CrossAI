Groves 21, of Georgetown University, recounts how, in 2002, U.S. leaders felt it necessary to remove America from the Rome Statute after efforts to address key U.S. concerns failed.

Specifically, Casey 18 of the Justice Department explains that the International Criminal Court, or ICC, is fundamentally unconstitutional as accession would violate Article Three by subjecting American citizens to prosecution in non-U.S. courts and violate the Bill of Rights in three ways: the right to a speedy trial, double jeopardy, and trial by jury.

In the past twenty years, America’s original objections to the ICC have not been resolved.

Thus, Spencer and I are proud to negate:

Our first contention is U.S. military sovereignty:

Currently, the U.S. military has the freedom to operate as it deems is necessary to uphold global peace and stability. However, if the U.S. were to join the ICC<mark> it would severel</mark>y hinder our military in two key ways:

Second, decreased military flexibility. Schaefer 98 finds that upon joining the ICC, the U.S. would be required to submit all military action to the ICC for approval before being implemented, making our military strategy an open book.

The impact is preserving peace.

Specifically, a strong and independent U.S. military is key to preventing atrocities. WH 99 explains how in 1999, U.S. military action stopped a three years long genocide in Yugoslavia that had killed 100,000 people. However, if the U.S. had been a member of the ICC at that time, this necessary action would have been deemed an infringement on Yugoslavia’s sovereignty and blocked.

More broadly, Hunter 19 explains that U.S. military superiority deters adversaries and stops conflicts before they even start. Hunter continues <mark>that flexible deterrent</mark> options are key to defending American interests and projecting national power.

<mark>With respect to the Constitutional</mark> objections, by <mark>join</mark>ing <mark>the ICC </mark>Treaty, the United States<mark> would subject American citizens to prosecution</mark> and trial<mark> in a court that was not established under Article III</mark> of the Constitution for criminal offenses otherwise subject to the judicial power of the United States. <mark>This, it cannot do. </mark>As the Supreme Court explained in the landmark Civil War case of Ex parte Milligan (1866), reversing a civilian's conviction by a military tribunal, "[e]very trial involves the exercise of judicial power," and <mark>courts not properly established under Article III can exercise "no part of the judicial power of the country.”2</mark>

Beyond coordination, political scientists and constitutional theorists have also recognized that game-theoretical logics of repeat play, reciprocity, and reputation can provide further support for constitutional commitment. Here the core idea is that politically powerful groups may be willing to trade their short-term interests for the longer-term benefits of cooperating with other groups in accordance with stable rules. Such accounts have been offered to explain compliance with particular rules or rights. For example. Democrats in control of the national government may refrain from suppressing Republican political speech on the tacit understanding that Republicans will similarly respect free speech when they are in control; or states may refrain from protectionism (or submit to congressional or judicial policing of trade regulation) in order to avoid the noncooperative equilibrium of trade warfare."* More broadly, commentators have invoked reciprocity to explain the "self-enforcing" stability of constitutional "pacts," ranging from the sectional balance rule in the antebellum Senate to the Constitution in its entirety.'" Adherence to such pacts can be modeled as an iterated game in which two or more social groups tacitly cooperate by resisting transgressions by government against any of the groups.'^° But the basic model can be extended beyond the agency context to encompass cooperative relationships between and among factions, where the cooperative equilibrium is either set by or definitive of constitutional rules.
