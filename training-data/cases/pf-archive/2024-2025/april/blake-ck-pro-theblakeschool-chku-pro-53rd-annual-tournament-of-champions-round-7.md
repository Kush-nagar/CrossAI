<mark>The current state of the U.S. power sector can be summed up in just a few words: too much demand for electricity; too small of a power grid</mark>. In the past two years, electricity demand forecasts have risen dramatically, a sharp reversal after nearly two decades of little-to-no electricity growth. <mark>The cheapest and cleanest way to meet most of this skyrocketing demand is by plugging new</mark> solar, wind, and battery <mark>projects into the grid</mark>. It’s also the only way to address this growth without undermining the emissions goals of states, the federal government, and the Big Tech firms whose data-center construction plans are driving much of this booming electricity demand to begin with. But an underbuilt and overburdened U.S. power grid is preventing clean energy from getting built and connected — just when the grid needs it the most. Solving this supply-demand mismatch will be the challenge for utilities, grid operators, regulators, and policymakers. After all, if it wasn’t AI data centers causing this reckoning now, it’d be electric vehicles or green hydrogen facilities or building electrification sometime in the near future. In other words, the era of rising electricity demand is here to stay. Below is a by-the-numbers breakdown of why it’s so hard for the U.S. to meet that need with clean power. Load growth is getting out of control — but not everywhere The latest data on the surge in U.S. electricity demand is not just surprising, it’s ​“shocking.” So says a December report from consultancy Grid Strategies that found that the five-year load-growth forecasts from utilities across the country have jumped almost fivefold from 2022 to late 2024, to nearly 128 gigawatts — a pace that would force the country to increase its capacity to generate and deliver electricity by nearly 16 percent by 2029. That’s an unprecedented growth rate in the modern era.

While natural gas, coal and wind currently supply 80% of our electricity needs, they are unlikely to match the energy appetite of the 21st-century artificial intelligence-hungry data-centers that power nearly every aspect of our life. <mark>Proposals for widespread nuclear adoption</mark> are gaining momentum, but concerns around policy shifts, electricity costs, environmental impact and labor shortages are on the rise. These challenges raise a pressing question: Can the U.S. fast-track nuclear energy adoption — address these issues head-on — and power a $26.3 trillion AI-driven economy while maintaining its global economic edge? To better answer this question, we must first examine how government regulations on nuclear energy have evolved in recent years. Back in 2018, the Trump administration took early steps to revitalize nuclear energy, signing the Nuclear Energy Innovation Capabilities Act and Nuclear Energy Innovation and Modernization Act to streamline licensing for advanced small modular reactors — a cost-efficient alternative for large-scale nuclear reactors. It also launched a high-assay low-enriched uranium initiative to supply the critical fuel needed for next-generation reactors. Continuing this trajectory, the Biden administration took a bolder stance in 2024, unveiling a structured and investment-focused plan to triple U.S. nuclear capacity by 2050, targeting 200 gigawatts through construction of new reactors and restoration of decommissioned nuclear plants. It also allocated $900 million for advanced nuclear reactors, i.e., Generation III+ small modular reactors — marking the boldest federal push for nuclear in half a century. Now, the new administration has signaled its commitment to nuclear energy but with a different approach, prioritizing deregulation over federal investment. On Jan. 20, President Donald Trump signed Unleashing American Energy, an executive order aimed at fast-tracking nuclear plant approvals and cutting bureaucratic delays. With one administration prioritizing investment and the other regulatory speed, neither alone can solve America’s energy crisis. Securing our nuclear future requires confronting deeper risks — cost, workforce, resources and infrastructure that are stalling large-scale, sustainable adoption. To begin with, our first biggest hurdle is time, cost and viability — large-scale nuclear plants demand $35 billion and five to 10 years to complete, while small modular reactors are yet to prove their long-term viability. Beyond cost and time, decommissioned plants have drained the workforce, leaving only one-third of the 200,000 skilled professionals needed in this sector. Compounding this, the radioactive waste — primarily plutonium — produced by these power plants poses severe environmental risks. Last but not the least, these reactors compete heavily for Earth’s most vital resource — water. Cooling a single reactor can require up to 680 million gallons of water per day, adding even more pressure to an already-strained resource. Given these risks and the evolving policies of successive administrations, <mark>we need a unified and long-term plan</mark> — one that harmonizes energy efficiency, federal investment, regulatory stability, workforce development, fair pricing policies and environmental responsibility to ensure <mark>nuclear energy becomes a reliable pillar of the U.S. power grid.</mark> First, energy-efficiency benchmarks should be established and organizations developing AI models must meet these benchmarks, ensuring a single AI query doesn’t consume 10 times more electricity than a Google search query. Second, with over 50-plus reactor designs in circulation, a standardized framework should be established to streamline regulatory reviews and fast-track approvals. Third, academic institutions must step up to train the workforce needed to address labor shortage. Fourth, utility companies should modernize the grid and expand transmission capacity. Our grids cannot rely on 60 million fragile, century-old power transformers. Fifth, energy costs must be fairly distributed — the average American shouldn’t bear the brunt of rising prices. The Department of Energy must ensure the cost burden is distributed fairly, with energy-heavy data center operators shouldering an appropriate share. <mark>Given the soaring demand for electricity — and the rapid adoption of AI — we are edging closer to an energy crisis. Without a unified approach to adopting nuclear power, America faces a dire reality: widespread power shortages within the next decade and energy costs so high they could rival the price of gold. The time to act is now.</mark>

A string of mysterious attacks on power stations across the U.S. has rekindled fears about the vulnerabilities of America’s electricity infrastructure, which security officials have warned presents a growing target to extremists and saboteurs. Attacks and suspicious activity at U.S. power stations reached a decade-long high last year, with more than 100 reported incidents in the first eight months of 2022, according to a TIME review of the Department of Energy’s most recent data, which runs through August. Since then, there have been at least 18 more publicly reported attacks or potential attacks on substations and power plants in Florida, North Carolina, Oregon, South Carolina, and Washington. The tally includes at least half a dozen at Duke Energy facilities in Florida and at least six others on electrical substations in the Pacific Northwest in November and December. In one attack on Thanksgiving, two intruders cut through the fencing around a substation in Clackamas County, Ore., and “used firearms to shoot up and disable numerous pieces of equipment and cause significant damage,” according to an incident report. It’s not clear who’s behind all this. In each of the last three years, law enforcement has foiled plots by right-wing extremists designed to sow chaos by attacking America’s electrical infrastructure. Now, officials say, the rise in incidents suggests the perpetrators may be drawing inspiration from one another, fine-tuning strategies to pursue potentially more damaging copycat attacks. The climate crisis is one of the defining challenges of this generation. Yet countries are not on track in their efforts to limit global warming. In order to reduce greenhouse gas emissions in line with the pathways outlined by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), all available options must be pursued. Analysis by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development’s (OECD’s) Nuclear Energy Agency (NEA) demonstrates that tripling global installed nuclear capacity is part of a realistic path to meet net-zero goals by 2050 and to keep the rise in global mean temperatures below 1.5°C.
