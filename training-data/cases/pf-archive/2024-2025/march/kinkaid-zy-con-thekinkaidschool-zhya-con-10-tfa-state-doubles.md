The world’s <mark>arms dealers</mark> will take a devastatingly different view. Their <mark>primary function</mark>, like that of any other industrial endeavour in a shareholder capitalist system, is<mark> to make money</mark> for shareholders while ensuring decent salaries and even more decent bonuses for the CEO and senior colleagues.

The “specialized knowledges” of the “military–strategic studies complex” <mark>have</mark> long <mark>been</mark> patronized, prioritized and <mark>actioned by the</mark> US <mark>military</mark>. Strategic studies <mark>knowledges</mark> have long been “above lived experience”, yet their power has been <mark>instrumental</mark> <mark>in unleashing</mark> catastrophe, terror and abject <mark>misery</mark> for the very people whose lives they are “above”. But clearly there is “catastrophe” for “us” too: the catastrophe of being overwhelmed by the collusion of power and knowledge, the catastrophe of the militant and deeply unequal world in which we live and the <mark>catastrophe of inaction</mark>—politically, discursively and otherwise. Geographers and other academics have of course been variously actively involved. Within the academy, geographers have illuminated key aspects of the US-led war against “militant Islam”, including its place-making strategies, its territorial responses to terrorist attacks and its exceptional legal and biopolitical geographies (Coleman 2003; Elden 2007; Morrissey 2011; Reid-Henry 2007). I want to conclude more positively, however, by suggesting ways to effectively oppose them. As an academic working in political geography, <mark>a key</mark> starting <mark>point</mark> of resistance for me <mark>is</mark> the careful <mark>detailing</mark> of <mark>the</mark> largely unseen <mark>inner workings of empire</mark> in our contemporary world, ultimately in order to be better able to resist it (which is what this paper has been about). Disrupting and countering the abstracted geopolitical scriptings of strategic studies can take on a variety of forms. This is where the company can sell small quantities of a very large range of products rather than large quantities of a small range of items (Enders et al., 2008). Another characteristic of digital products is that they are non-rivalrous and non-excludable (Brynjolffson, 2016). In economics, a good that is both non-excludable and nonrivalrous means that use by one individual will not reduce its availability to others. For example, a physical newspaper, video/DVD or CD can only be purchased or rented by a single individual at a time excluding others from hiring or buying the same product (i.e. it is rivalrous) whereas digitised movies or music can be streamed multiple times and watched by millions of people simultaneously. This on-demand business model also enhances value through higher availability and lower cost and requires fewer labour hours if any at all. 13 The use of labour hours to value a product or service is also undermined as a theory when considering the availability of free digital services and products via the Internet. Networked platforms serve as more efficient intermediaries owing to their ability to use market mediating mechanisms that scale.
