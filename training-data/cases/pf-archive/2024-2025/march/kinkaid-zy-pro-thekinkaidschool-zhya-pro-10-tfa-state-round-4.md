The Contention is Queering AI --

Queer theory queers the categorization of AI and allows for creative subversion -- interrogating the misuse of data builds queer databases to challenge the status quo

Anh-Ton Tran, [Georgia Institute Of Technology, United States], July 2024, "Making Trouble: Techniques for Queering Data and AI Systems," ACM, https://dl.acm.org/doi/fullHtml/10.1145/3656156.3658393, accessed 3-6-2025 //cy

1 BACKGROUND AND MOTIVATION
The datafication of the world and the compulsion to become "data-driven" have long been critiqued, particularly when "objective" categories embedded in sociotechnical systems may harm those who fall outside of conventional categories [8, 9, 20, 24, 49]. For example, commercial gender facial recognition software powered by image data performs poorly on darker-skinned [10] and transgender [61] people. The recent proliferation of consumer-facing Generative AI (GenAI) products– such as ChatGPT, Dall-E, and Midjourney –seems to cement the encroachment of Data and AI into our everyday lives, further highlighting the importance of attending to where data comes from [32] and the politics of how we design with data [43].

<mark>The current emphasis on Gen</mark>erative <mark>AI </mark>parallels a similar trend in the 2010s. In her foundational HCI paper on queering, Ann Light paints a similar picture with the fast adoption of digital tools in our everyday lives [46]. <mark>She turns to Queer Theory to</mark> promote <mark>queer</mark>ing as a potential <mark>design orientation to resist dominant discourses.</mark> Queering <mark>involves disruption</mark> or breaking down categories [11, 12, 13]; it is <mark>a form of creative subversion</mark> that troubles the status quo. As other HCI scholars note, turning away from the status quo requires more than just observing the current state and (re)designing for it [41]. Instead, <mark>we must question why the</mark> latest <mark>tech</mark>nology <mark>is ingratiating itself </mark>into our lives and who has the most to benefit from its adoption – and who has potential to be harmed. Doing so requires designing in ways that resists the impacts of current technologization and centralization [47]. In this one-day workshop, we invite participants to consider how we may de-stabilize the looming narrative of datafication, algorithmic decision-making, and its latest instantiation of Generative AI. In this workshop we will collaboratively devise techniques for queering AI, but also for the materials that predicate its existence: data.

In this vein, this workshop builds upon Light's provocation to queer by compiling various modalities of queering, grappling with the relational structure between archive and repertoire, a vital concept within Queer Theory [52, 53, 72]. Archives are a repository of evidence, and repertoires are forms of collective memories, imagination, and practices. Oppressed groups often rely on repertoire to pass down knowledge that exists outside of the master narrative [72]. For queer futurist José Esteban Muñoz, traces — remnants of what is left behind — denote this relationality between repertoire and archive [52, 53]. These traces are etched around the artful subversions employed by those who exist outside of the dominant class. <mark>Honoring </mark>the repertoire of <mark>queerness</mark>, we invite workshop participants to <mark>bring </mark>examples of subversions or<mark> (mis)uses of data</mark> and AI <mark>to </mark>build an <mark>archive </mark>of <mark>queer </mark>design <mark>techniques.</mark> These <mark>examples may be subversions of</mark> data and<mark> AI by queer people or using data </mark>and AI <mark>to queer systems</mark>, artifacts, or people (Fig. 1). These aren't mutually exclusive, nor is queering confined to these categories.

Collectively constructing this archive will be messy [31, 39]. The goal is not to be exhaustive, but embrace plurality [29]. In this spirit, our agenda concludes with <mark>an opportunity to play with</mark> these repertoires and craft more <mark>possibilities to trouble the status quo </mark>in HCI and AI. <mark>Utilizing critical making</mark> [57], we invite participants <mark>to </mark>take a hand at <mark>queer</mark>ing something of their own – whether it be their research, their art, or simply their outlook on<mark> this algorithmically determined world </mark>we careen into.

Queerness is ambiguous and an open mesh of possibility -- queer perspectives destabilize binarism of AI and unsettle hegemonic thought

Turtle, Grace 06-25-2022, [researcher and educator @ Delft University] Mutant in the mirror: Queer becomings with AI, in Lockton, D., Lenzi, S., Hekkert, P., Oak, A., Sádaba, J., Lloyd, P. (eds.), DRS2022: Bilbao, 25 June - 3 July, Bilbao, Spain. https://doi.org/10.21606/ drs.2022.782 DOA 3/6/2025 //cy

As a starting point, this paper acknowledges that<mark> queerness is necessarily indeterminate, ambiguous and always in relation</mark>, denoting flexible spaces for the expression of all aspects of non, anti, contra, straight, cultural production and reception. As put by Sarah Ahmed, "queer lives are about the potentiality of <mark>not following certain conventional scripts</mark>, whereby not following involves disorientation" (Ahmed, 2006, p. 189). To reorientate our understanding of human-AI relations, from the ontological and epistemological borderland of queerness and the gender binary, we first need some sense of what queerness means, and how it relates to (design/ing, with, for and through) AI systems. Queerness as <mark>described by </mark>José <mark>Muñoz </mark>in Cruising Utopia: The Then and There of Queer Futurity, can be read as a “horizon imbued with potentiality”,<mark> an "open mesh of possibility”, a rejection of the</mark> here and <mark>now</mark>, and a “basic desire to live otherwise” (Muñoz, 2001, p. 1-2). Always rooted in queer life, struggle and liberation, queerness “is not yet here… we are not yet queer” (Muñoz, 2009, p. 1-2). Accordingly,<mark> queer futurity, is less about expanding</mark> a range of <mark>choices </mark>(liberal freedom) <mark>than </mark>it is about <mark>transforming </mark>the kinds of<mark> beings we desire to be</mark> while embracing the mutating multitudes and entangled lifeworlds that make up the pluriverse, or as referred to by the Zapatistas, “un mundo donde quepan muchos mundos” (a world where many worlds fit) (Escobar, 2016, p. 42). In <mark>considering queer becomings with AI,</mark> we cross a threshold,<mark> intentionally getting lost in</mark> the borderlands of <mark>possibility</mark>, what Gloria Anzaldúa proposes as a psychic, social, and cultural terrain that we inhabit and that inhabits all of us (Anzaldúa, 1987), by which they mean a terrain where we encounter the world differently; losing our way to find our way (Ahmed, 2006). Importantly, this place of uncertainty is not a weakness but a power to be explored (Preciado, 2021). In this way, queerness is just as much about playing with the kinds of beings we desire to be as much as it is about offering a critique of the cultural, sociotechnical systems that shape our becoming with AI.

<mark>To the binary logic of AI,</mark> differently situated knowledge(s) and <mark>other/ed perspectives, are destabilising, </mark>thus<mark> demonstrating that binaries,</mark> be they imagined, cultural or technological<mark> are not natural nor </mark>always <mark>necessary</mark>. Rather, the notion of queerness speaks to a politics of transitivity—relating to, or characterised by, transition, bringing into question modes of gendered embodiment within all humans and non-humans and their configurations (Halberstam, 2016, 2017). In this sense, a <mark>queering of AI is</mark> a proposal <mark>to unsettle western thought,</mark> including the dominant techno-deterministic, cultural fictions, social imaginaries and technical propositions<mark> that shape </mark>how we create for, through, and with<mark> AI relations</mark> (Revell et al 2021, p. 57). Calling for new postures or rather, “queer turnings,” (Ahmed, 2006) towards AI, may help shift how we relate to, and move with AI differently. Mutations arising through queerness offer designers a borderland perspective to decode, indeed, to deterritorialise AI's fixed binary historical understandings and how it is deployed within everyday life (Deleuze & Guattari, 2005, p. 69). My point of departure, then, is to think about mutations as a means of unsettling common understandings of or postures towards AI today. <mark>This</mark>, I hope, <mark>will offer </mark>designers a mode of leaning into performativity, improvisation and <mark>uncertainty in their dealings with AI </mark>and the “mutable and the immutable features of our existence” (Levitas, 2013. p. 137).

Embrace the mutant mirror -- queering AI creates mutant, fluid images not grounded in normativity and categorization -- it creates survival spaces for queer bodies and refuses heteronormativity

Turtle, Grace 06-25-2022, [researcher and educator @ Delft University] Mutant in the mirror: Queer becomings with AI, in Lockton, D., Lenzi, S., Hekkert, P., Oak, A., Sádaba, J., Lloyd, P. (eds.), DRS2022: Bilbao, 25 June - 3 July, Bilbao, Spain. https://doi.org/10.21606/ drs.2022.782 DOA 3/6/2025 //cy

I titled this ongoing experiment as the Mutant in the Mirror for the obvious reason that my self-reflexive experience of observing the images documented in Figure 1 quite literally feels like <mark>staring into a mirror, with mutants staring back at me. </mark>The potential variations of myself function as possibilities of myself, a uniquely queer skin anticipating changes to my cyberidentity. It asks, <mark>do I present as ambiguous?</mark> I am a body in mutation? It is my intention.

Figure 1 presents some of the mutating synthetic image data produced through the experiment. The images act as a discursive device used to evoke reflections on the kind of queer, mutating becomings with AI discussed in this text.<mark> Generating </mark>multiple <mark>mutant images </mark>and synthetic data through the StyleGAN model<mark> provided</mark> me with<mark> a space to play with my</mark> own indeterminacy,<mark> fluid boundaries, </mark>(dis)identifiability<mark> and resistance to classification </mark>and categorisation. The particular model used in this experiment was trained using a small personal dataset of 70 images of myself across different places in time, some <mark>passing more feminine</mark> and some as <mark>more masculine.</mark> Also included in the training dataset were images of <mark>other humans </mark>(grandmother, mother, etc.), and<mark> non-humans</mark> (dog, places, etc.) that shape my sense of time, self and world. <mark>The 3000+ image(s)</mark> and video(s) generated through the experiment function as a mirror, through which<mark> I get lost, disoriented and reoriented,</mark> turning differently 6 Mutant in the mirror towards myself, my body, my corporal and subjective experience of the world. In this way, the Mutant in the Mirror comes to function as<mark> both a looking glass and a crystal ball</mark>, surfacing (speculations of) alternative versions of myself—mutating—<mark>becoming with AI.</mark> What started as an experiment in reimagining the notion of "fixed labels, fluid complexity" concerning who decides how we are read in the world<mark>, challenging deterministic binary </mark>modes of <mark>classification</mark> and categorisation (Crawford, 2021), concludes as an exploration into a metaphysics of interconnectedness (Anzaldúa, 2015). Indeed, a reflection on what (queer) intraactions with AI systems might tell us about ourselves when we co-perform with them.

So what did the experiment tell me about myself?<mark> Am I </mark>a woman or a man? A subject or an object? Corporeal or incorporeal? Or, am I<mark> something in-between or outside </mark>of these <mark>dichotomies</mark>? Am I hairspray? Or am I a toy poodle? (see figure 2) Design’s current instantiations and orientations towards AI tend towards inscribing binary classifications, engendering human and non-human bodies and experiences—resulting in the misclassification or erasure of nonbinary or trans bodies and experiences via cultural (sociotechnical) classification systems encoded with homonormative (masculine) logic and meaning (Bell & Broad et al., 2021; Keyes 2018). <mark>The Mutant in the Mirror calls </mark>the<mark> binarization </mark>of AI <mark>into question,</mark> offering a <mark>glimpse into </mark>designing interventions for refusal to 'perform' in a way that was prescribed as much as it provides<mark> openings for imagining</mark> and enacting the world in <mark>unpredictable ways</mark> while reaching more diverse probabilistic outcomes.

The classification presented in Figure 2, with a high probability that I am hairspray and a low probability that I am lipstick, suggests that the mutable self is reducible to a set of categorical signifiers when in reality data is textural and culturally rich. By playing with AI systems that at once negotiate deterministic and probabilistic outcomes—while neatly classifying and categorising people—one could quickly grasp that this is an area that requires critical design review. As everyday life practices increasingly rely on AI systems, what is at stake are our computational sovereignty (Bratton, 2016) and our ability to imagine and enact different realities as self-determining subjects (Parisi, 2019; O'Shea, 2021), individually or collectively. It is not evident how the classifications depicted in Figure 2 came about. However, through the experiment, it becomes clear that, to paraphrase James Bridle, we (designers) must find ways to deal with an incomputable world while acknowledging that it is irrevocably shaped and informed by computation.<mark> Recognising that AI </mark>systems are encoded, and thus <mark>can be decoded</mark> and re-written,<mark> allows for </mark>a <mark>different</mark> kind of <mark>logic </mark>and meaning to take root. Such a rewriting of AI may<mark> challenge how the self can be interpreted </mark>and labelled by and through AI systems in its open-ended becoming.

Upon reflection on the experiment, I am reminded by Muñoz’s (1999) proposal that we view identification as code, a raw material that can be cracked open and reconfigured. <mark>This experiment</mark> opens doors to<mark> rethinking categorical signification, challenging</mark> the notion of categories and <mark>classification under</mark> its current deployments within and by<mark> AI systems.</mark> In this way, (dis)identification can be seen as a possible avenue for designers to engage with the incommensurate, that which resists fixed definition or measurement. Or otherwise put, disidentification and the failings to read by computational systems may present opportunities to negotiate calculative reasoning, the fine line between incomputability, and cultural computability of AI in more imaginative ways. Along these lines, <mark>mutations,</mark> rebelling<mark> against classification</mark> (Preciado, 2021), can be seen to<mark> make space for </mark>representation and empowerment of<mark> minority identities</mark>, identifications and positionalities <mark>rendered unthinkable by the dominant culture, </mark>whereby the potential for computational sovereignty and digital self-determination can be realised. Moreover, <mark>as</mark> an act of <mark>refusal, the mutant starts a conversation </mark>with us about AI predictions, categories and classifications.<mark> It testifies that AI needs not be deterministic</mark> in moving to a singular final destination; it must not assume that how we are read must be chosen for us,<mark> as if we were singular and not plural</mark>, static and unchangeable.

Inject queerness into AI -- the result exposes and subverts limitations of normativity in favor of queer utopias beyond binary recognitions of gender and existence

 Joe Parslow (2023) [Head of research @ Bruford College, queer lecturer] Kings, Queens, Monsters, and Things: Digital Drag Performance and Queer Moves in Artificial Intelligence (AI), Contemporary Theatre Review, 33:1-2, 128-148, DOI: 10.1080/10486801.2023.2173592 DOA 3/6/25 //cy

In the video installation Queering the Dataset, Jake took a dataset of 70,000 images of human faces and inserted into it 1000 images of drag performers. Through training a Machine Learning algorithm used to generate fake images on this augmented dataset, what it produced are an amalgam of drag faces, monsters, and things.<mark> Inserting the drag images</mark> – what Jake refers to as disrupting or even corrupting the dataset or<mark> injecting queerness</mark> into it –<mark> resulted in</mark> these <mark>monstrous, alluring, and queerly moving images</mark>, where the images move queerly and are queerly moving or affecting (see Image 1). 13 This notion of corruption as a tactic or strategy in Jake’s practice is particular to this project, where the aim is <mark>to reveal the limitations in normative datasets and </mark>in so doing <mark>propose </mark>fabulous and monstrous <mark>new formations of human-ness</mark>. It might be assumed that <mark>this </mark>sense of corruption might <mark>connect to </mark>queer negativity or queer anti-social politics, however, I link this practice to José Muñoz’s articulation of <mark>queer utopias </mark>where any articulation of a queer utopia contains within it <mark>an active critique and</mark> even <mark>refusal of a straight present</mark>. Positions of queer negativity and queer futurity are not, then, binary oppositions but instead complexly inter-related, and here this notion of corruption indicates a negative relationship to normative datasets that produces a future-oriented practice which I unpack further below. 15

Queering the Dataset has been shown internationally in artistic and festival settings, as well as on Instagram (see Image 2). 16 The impulse to actively inject queerness into the normative framework of these datasets means the work remains unfixed but also speaks to the playful engagement with the technologies that characterises Jake’s work elsewhere, with pieces such as dada da ta (2016) and Machine Learning Porn (2016) offering purposefully silly yet provocative deployments of AI algorithms in artistic work.

In my interview with Jake, they describe how the project began from playing with the idea of the representation (or lack thereof) of queerness within the datasets used to train facial recognition software and to produce deepfake technologies: Image 2.

What was interesting was <mark>subverting </mark>this idea of creating the most ‘realistic’ face, which often means <mark>the </mark>most <mark>‘normative’ face. </mark>What I love are the mistakes, the failures, the times where you can see this is obviously a construction, a digital process that is making these faces, which sometimes completely breaks down. This is not thinking about building a deepfake body but instead about <mark>injecting queer bodies </mark>and images into an existing dataset. 17 18

Here, I reflect on ‘real-ness’ as connected to certain drag histories, and in particular the ballroom scene popularised by the documentary Paris is Burning (1990) and more recently the television show Pose (2018– 2021). In ballroom practices, striving for ‘realness’ indicates the performers’ ability to pass or be seen as legitimate within a category (for example to achieve ‘executive realness’ a performer is expected to pass within office settings). As Judith Butler notes in Bodies That Matter, ‘what determines the effect of realness is the ability to compel belief, to produce the naturalised effect. This effect is itself the result of an embodiment of norms’. 19 For Butler, ‘realness’ relates to dominant modes of gendered (and racialised) performativity, where drag and ballroom cultures are understood to engage in a ‘performance’ deeply rooted in striving for the accomplishment of normativity. Notably, both Butler and bell hooks (amongst many others) articulate the limits of gendered and racialised notions of ‘realness’ which both enable and contain minoritarian subjects within injurious representational systems. 20 This is connected to Lauren Berlant’s notion of ‘cruel optimism’, understood as that which is both sustaining and damaging. ‘Cruel optimism’ recognises those things to which subjects are attached, such as the promise of the good life or normative modes of being in the world, can both sustain individuals and communities and injure them. 21

Performance and performativity, however, are distinct but connected terms. In their earlier thinking in Gender Trouble, Butler draws on drag as a key exemplar of the construction and performativity of gender, noting, ‘The effect of gender is produced through the stylization of the body and, hence, must be understood as the mundane ways in which bodily gestures, movements, and styles of various kinds constitute the illusion of an abiding gendered self’. 22 Importantly, gender is not being described as a performance (or a costume which can be put on and taken off at will) as is often still misunderstood by critics of this work, but instead as a temporally and spatially contingent set of acts and actions that cohere into normative understandings of gender. Regardless, this notion of performativity has permeated the fields of queer studies, gender studies, and performance studies in countless ways. Whilst many theorists (including Butler themself) have critiqued and developed this early position, these ideas remain. Drag performance still comes to represent the idea of gender as ‘constructed’ by drag performers, and those who study and watch it. Through these debates, drag as a performance has been central to understanding gender in the Global North since the 1990s (if not before). However, what is often missing in these discussions is that drag might offer not a re-performance of gendered norms, but instead a space in which to play, experiment with and expose the very limits of these gendered ideals – something which The Zizi Project does. Furthermore, the use of drag as an exemplar of the performativity of gender often fails to account for drag as a performance form with a distinct and complex set of histories and politics connected to and beyond the politics of gender, as Stephen Farrier notes. 23 However, returning to these notions opens up pertinent areas for discussion surrounding how these emergent AI technologies can be used to both subvert and uphold the status quo in much the same way Butler articulated of drag in relation to gendered norms. 24

Importantly, I am not setting out to draw an overly simplified link between ballroom cultures primarily connected to Black and Latinx queer and trans subcultures in the USA in the 1980s and 1990s and this contemporary AI project. Instead, my focus is the link between ‘real’ and ‘fake’ as connecting tissue that offers interesting modes of comparison between historic and contemporary drag forms and standards and contemporary languages used to describe the successes and failures of AI and deepfake systems. 25 This connection between realness and normativity is reflected in Jake’s assertion that the desire for ‘realness’ in deepfake technology is often synonymous with a desire for normativity. The languages of real and fake, then, offer inroads into considering how technologies can be mobilised in service of and in resistance to gendered (and other) norms.

In an informal conversation with Jake, when discussing these complex ideas of realness and deepfake technology, they made a link between notions of realness and passing in relation to gender and notions of computers or AI systems passing the Turing test (a test to show if a computer has a ‘mind’, which is achieved when a human speaks to both a computer and another human and cannot tell which responder is the human). 26 Jake cites Yuval Noah Harari’s Homo Deus: a brief history of tomorrow and their assertion that ‘[t]he Turing Test is simply a replication of a mundane test every gay man had to undergo in 1950 Britain: can you pass for a straight man?’. 27 This connection between passing the Turing test to pass as human and passing in relation to codified and hegemonic forms of gender and sexuality places The Zizi Project within wider technological and philosophical debates surrounding how norms and forms are reproduced and resisted by marginalised subjects and communities. However, I am resistant to too neatly finding confluence between these diverse areas of gender and technology and caution against any simple readings of how technology might both resonate with and challenge norms of gender and sexuality. Instead of getting lost in this (admittedly pleasurable) theorisation around what 138 Queering the Dataset might represent, I return to the work in question to consider its effects and affects, or what it does.

What initially intrigued me about Queering the Dataset was how the ever-morphing drag faces slowly picked up on recognisable images or forms. In looking at the various versions of the artwork on Instagram or in a gallery, fleeting visual references to recognisable drag performers can be picked out, only to move on to another less recognisable (and less recognisably human) face in the next moment. <mark>Drag </mark>monsters and things<mark> populate the work as</mark> the <mark>AI</mark> moves through the potential faces it has learned to<mark> generate from</mark> Jake’s <mark>queered dataset,</mark> <mark>navigating </mark>the latent space or ‘the <mark>limitations, </mark>boundaries and space<mark> containing everything a neural network has learnt’</mark>. 28 For Jake, this concept of the latent space is at the centre of their thinking around the potential of AI as well as its limitations. In a blog post for Digital Democracies, they state:

In many ways, I believe that there is already an inherent queerness to latent space. While an algorithm may have been given the task to distinguish between male and female faces – due to the labels inputted by humans – the neural network in fact reduces input faces to numbers or coordinates in a latent space. If we remove our human classifications and binaries from this system, it doesn’t read images of faces as male or female. Instead, everything falls into spectrums or points in this fluid and continuous space. Binaries and labels can be read back into these systems depending on where in this space an image is recognised to be. But we can also explore the unmediated mathematical latent space. Zizi – Queering the Dataset takes an unsupervised journey through this space. In the video, you can watch as it continuously and effortlessly moves through different identities and personas. 29

As such, in<mark> Queering the Dataset utopian possibilities</mark> might well be<mark> seen. </mark>There are glimpses of what José Muñoz might describe as ‘a kernel of political possibility within a stultifying straight present’. 30 However, beyond a political possibility, I also locate a potentiality within this work. As Muñoz theorises, 31

Possibilities exist, or more nearly, they exist within a logical real, the possible, which is within the present and is linked to presence. Potentialities are different in that although they are present, they do not exist in present things. Thus, potentialities have a temporality that is not in the present but, more nearly, in the horizon, which we can understand as futurity.

Queering the Dataset,<mark> with </mark>its <mark>shifting, morphing</mark>, and unceasing litany of <mark>drag faces, offers glimpses of</mark> potentiality and<mark> futurity beyond </mark>the boundaries of <mark>what AI understands as human</mark> and beyond the boundaries of what can be readily understood as drag. Whilst there are clear references to specific drag performers and performance forms, there are also <mark>these monstrous moments</mark> that <mark>defy human logics </mark>(and, indeed, biology) to speak to the future of drag whilst still being connected to drag histories and presents. Ultimately, whilst body-less, the images created refer back to drag bodies that <mark>queered the normative dataset </mark>used to generate the work <mark>and </mark>simultaneously<mark> make utopian gestures towards technological </mark>and embodied <mark>forms </mark>which move<mark> beyond binarized recognitions of gender </mark>and drag forms.

Blair Attard-Frost, [PhD candidate @ University of Toronto, research focuses on AI systems] published in Queer Reflections On AI, 2024, Queering intelligence A theory of intelligence as performance and a critique of individual and artificial intelligence, DOA 3/6/2025, https://library.oapen.org/bitstream/id/4b2a502a-4da8-4b21-b7b1-a4000aabf1e3/9781000923575.pdf //cy

Queering as exploratory method “<mark>Queering</mark>” has been employed<mark> as a verb</mark> by many queer people and communities, usually<mark> to describe a process</mark> through <mark>which </mark>the dominant values of <mark>heteronormative and cisnormative cultures are exposed and challenged by queer cultural practices</mark>. In her approach to queer interaction design, HCI scholar Ann Light describes queering more generally as “a space-making exercise” that challenges the dominant perspectives of a particular social context by exposing the ontological and normative assumptions implicit to those perspectives (2011, 433). Light explains that when applied to practices of design or analysis, <mark>queering functions</mark> as an exploratory method that aims 30 to <mark>produce </mark>“an absence of dogma and a mutability that allows <mark>new truths, perspectives and engagements </mark>to emerge through a refusal to accept definition” (2011, 433). As a method, queering can be applied to critique the cognitive, normative, and performative dimensions implicated in a domain of intelligence. Queering can also enable exploration of alternative perspectives on the boundaries between intelligent activity and unintelligent activity within that domain.

When combined with a theory of intelligence as value-dependent cognitive performance, queering allows for more than simply describing the cognitive, normative, and performative assumptions at play in a specified domain of intelligence.<mark> Queering enables critique of </mark>those <mark>assumptions</mark> by refusing their underlying values by default, <mark>questioning </mark>what <mark>alternative values </mark>might exist,<mark> and exploring discursive spaces </mark>in <mark>which those values could be given voice.</mark> Collectively, these theories and methods provide <mark>a </mark>critical <mark>framework for queering intelligence. </mark>

The following section will queer intelligence by applying that framework to a brief analysis of two primary texts and a discussion of secondary sources that are critical of the assumptions made by those texts. To demonstrate the applicability of the framework to multiple domains of intelligence, the primary texts were selected to be representative of two distinct domains of intelligence: individual human intelligence and AI. The primary texts represent the main cognitive, normative, and performative assumptions implicit in two influential, domain-specific theories of intelligence: John Carroll’s three-stratum model of individual human intelligence, and Alan Turing’s ontology of AI. Key findings from the analyses will then be mapped onto each of the two domains and three dimensions of intelligence and compiled into a set of exploratory questions.

Queer violence should be a central question - omnicide and violence stems from a desire to wipe the world of queerness
<mark>Sedgwick 90</mark> (Eve, Professor of English at Duke University, Epistemology of the Closet, 1990, California at Berkeley Press, p. 127-130, https://moodle2.units.it/pluginfile.php/579988/course/section/135870/Epistemology%20of%20the%20Closet.pdf)
From at least the biblical story of Sodom and Gomorrah, scenarios of same-sex desire would seem to have had a privileged, though by no means an exclusive, relation in Western culture to scenarios of both genocide and omnicide. That sodomy, the name by which homosexual acts are known even today to the law of half of the United States and to the Supreme Court of all of them, should already be inscribed with the name of a site of mass extermination is the appropriate trace of a double history.
