1. Digital Divide and Educational Inequality
GenAI may exacerbate existing inequalities within education:
Unequal Access: High costs of advanced GenAI tools along with infrastructure requirements limit accessibility for underprivileged students and institutions.
Widening the Gap: Those with access to premium GenAI services may gain advantages that increase the disparity between wealthy and disadvantaged learners.
Global Inequities: Developing countries may lack the infrastructure to support GenAI, hindering educational progress.
Critical Insight: While GenAI has the potential to transform education, it risks deepening the digital divide and exacerbating inequalities. Unequal access to AI-powered tools is a major concern, as advanced GenAI technologies are often costly and inaccessible to underprivileged students, particularly in low-income or rural areas. This economic barrier leaves many without access to GenAI’s personalized learning and academic support, further disadvantaging them. As <mark>well-resourced institutions adopt premium</mark>, rather than freemium, GenAI <mark>services</mark>, their <mark>students gain a competitive edge, deepening social</mark> and educational <mark>hierarchies.</mark> In contrast, <mark>underfunded schools rely on outdated resources, widening the gap between wealthy and disadvantaged learners.</mark> This disparity undermines education’s role in promoting equal opportunity. Global inequities in AI accessibility are also pressing, as many developing nations lack the infrastructure to support GenAI integration. In resource-constrained settings, where access to GenAI tools often comes at the cost of increased surveillance, marginalized communities may face difficult trade-offs, such as sacrificing privacy for technological access. This cost-benefit balance varies widely across institutions and regions, impacting the extent to which GenAI can be integrated in an equitable and autonomous manner. Without addressing these access and ethical concerns, GenAI risks reinforcing, rather than bridging, existing educational disparities.

## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case presents distinct impact scenarios (inequality, moral hazard, democratic governance) but contains no pre-written weighing mechanism comparing them. There is no "probability > magnitude" or "structural violence first" framing card, and no tagline such as "Inequality outweighs — structural impact precedes individual learning loss (Bradley 2022)." A weighing card would need to explicitly rank the case's own impacts and pre-empt Pro's likely magnitude claims (e.g., personalized learning gains).

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No card explicitly pre-empts common Pro positions such as "AI personalizes learning for marginalized students," "AI closes tutor-access gaps," or "teacher efficiency gains outweigh costs." The Williamson evidence critiques corporate incentives generally but does not name and neutralize a specific Pro contention. An offensive spike would carry a tag like "AI tutoring widens gaps — high-dose human tutoring is the only proven equalizer (Kraft 2021)" and be placed in Contention 1 to clash directly with Pro's access narrative.

### Defensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case lacks embedded frontlines against the responses it will face: "regulation solves," "digital divide is shrinking," "GPT-4o mini is free," or "hallucination rates are dropping." Williamson 2024 asserts regulation is politically unlikely but does not convert this into a spike tag such as "Regulation fails — 50 years of ed-tech deregulation proves Congress won't act (Williamson 2024)." Each contention needs a one-card pre-emption of the strongest Pro rebuttal.

### Evidence quality
**ABSENT:** Several cards fall short of elite evidence-comparison standards. Bastani 2024 is a working paper (SSRN pre-print) without peer-review confirmation; Paoli 2025 cites a secondary media summary of an unnamed study rather than the primary research; Bozkurt et al. 2024 is a collective manifesto, not an empirical study; Bradley 2022 relies on 2019 data and a Ballard Brief (undergraduate outlet). A top-tier case would replace these with peer-reviewed 2023-2024 studies (e.g., Nature Human Behaviour, AERA Open) showing large-N causal estimates.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case is effectively pinned to two linear link chains: (1) cost → resource shift → inequality and (2) ease-of-use → skill atrophy → moral hazard. There is no third independent scenario (e.g., surveillance/civil-liberties, teacher deprofessionalization, epistemic fragmentation) that could be collapsed to if Pro wins a link turn on cost or a mitigation on skill atrophy. A flexible case would include a Contention 3 card tagged "Surveillance infrastructure — AI embeds biometric tracking in LMS (Williamson 2024; Selwyn 2023)" to guarantee a clean 2NR option.

### Warrant depth
**Williamson 2024 (first card)** — "The weight of the available evidence suggests that the current wholesale adoption of unregulated AI applications in schools poses a grave danger to democratic civil society…" — The highlighted text lists five specific causal mechanisms (teacher-student degradation, mechanistic instruction, misinformation, vendor lock-in, privacy/surveillance) rather than merely asserting the conclusion, giving each tagline in Contention 1 and 2 a traceable warrant.  
**Bastani 2024 (third card)** — "When access is subsequently taken away, students actually perform worse than those who never had access (17% reduction for GPT Base)" — Provides a concrete experimental warrant (within-subjects removal design) for the moral-hazard claim, not just a correlational assertion.

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** Contention 1 argues AI widens inequality partly because "running AI is costly… schools will pay costly fees" (Williamson 2024 cost card), while the moral-hazard contention relies on Bastani 2024's finding that *free* GPT-4 Base access harms learning. If Pro argues "districts will use free tiers," the cost link collapses but the moral-hazard link remains — yet the case never acknowledges or reconciles this tension. A consistency card would flag: "Free-tier harms persist — Bastani 2024 GPT Base was free-to-user and still caused 17% post-removal decrement."

### Overall construction score
**Score: 4/10** — The case assembles relevant, high-theme evidence and two cards supply genuine warrants, but it lacks embedded weighing, offensive/defensive spikes, collapse options, and evidence-tier discipline, leaving it strategically rigid against prepared opponents.
