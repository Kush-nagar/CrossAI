Yang and Lee 20 [Yang, Hee-Yong, and Seungho Lee. "China's Engagement with Latin America and Its Implications for Soft Balancing against the United States." Asian Perspective 44, no. 4 (2020): 587-615. https://dx.doi.org/10.1353/apr.2020.0026, accessed: 8-8-2024] OA
 
Soft balancing is a useful concept to capture geopolitical realities and implications of China’s increasing activism in Latin America. Without a sensible analytical framework, China’s deepening ties with the region is merely treated as a part of wealth acquisition driven purely by economic motives and usual diplomatic efforts. Applying the concept of soft balancing allows us to understand China’s increasing economic and political foothold as a result of strategic planning, designed to link its geo-economic endowments with national policy objectives. This approach helps uncover the evident linkage between economic issues— deepening economic ties—and geopolitical issues—growing political influence—rather than treating them separately. Secondly, soft balancing underlines the systemic conditions that preclude traditional hard balancing strategies. Wohlforth (2004) suggests that second-tier powers are incapable of balancing US power in regional systems largely because they cannot find allies to join. This study attributes such argument not only to the lopsided attention to traditional hard balancing, but also to the lack of attention to the structural constraints that unipolarity imposes on second-tier powers. Under unipolarity, active and intense balancing will be revisionist, with the risk of being portrayed as a threat to the international order. This does not mean that secondary powers fully accommodate US hegemony. Soft balancing, in this regard, uncovers different strategic options that can be employed to constrain and undermine the hegemonic power. Lastly, the perspective of soft balancing can shed light to the longterm implications of China’s rise, which is power transition between the United States and China. According to Gilpin (1983, 1988), <mark>growth in</mark> the <mark>economic</mark> and military <mark>capabilities of a rising power triggers</mark> intense <mark>competition for resources and markets</mark>, political influence, and prestige, <mark>foreshadowing</mark> serious international <mark>crises and hegemonic wars</mark>. While <mark>China’s rise in Latin America</mark> has the potential to <mark>change the</mark> regional <mark>balance of power</mark>, China may now be in the stage of the “deconcentration” and “delegitimization” of America’s hegemonic power, prior to full-fledged balancing behavior (Schweller and Pu 2011, 44). China’s Rise in Latin America In order to understand China’s soft balancing strategy, it is necessary to examine the structural conditions that have given to it. The end of the Cold War brought about significant changes in the regional power   dynamics. The evaporation of a major security threat diluted the unifying force between the United States and Latin America. The 9/11 attacks and ensuing US military interventions in the Middle East diverted America’s attention and resources further away from the region. In response, several countries in the region began to reevaluate their relations with the regional hegemon and seek for their own ways to ensure economic development and political control. Eventually, China with its vast resources and market power began to fill the gap created by America’s waning influence in the region. Given the economic and military superiority of the United States in the Western Hemisphere, Latin America has been widely considered for centuries an area under the preponderant influence of the United States. The 1823 Monroe Doctrine and the 1904 Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine helped establish the US position as a regional hegemon. The United States not only shaped the regional system through a variety of US-led international institutions or treaties such as the Organization of American States (OAS), formed in 1948, the Rio Treaty signed in 1947, and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) established in 1959, but also intervened in the domestic affairs of a number of Latin American countries (Covarrubias and Dominguez 2015).The US possessed a capacity to realize its preferred political, economic, and diplomatic outcomes in the region. The end of the Cold War, however, brought about a major change in US foreign policy toward Latin America, liberating it from its Cold War, anti-communist logic. At first, the successful democratic transition of most Latin American countries and their enhanced market economies seemed to offer a positive vision for the post–Cold War Western Hemisphere (Hakim 2006). The United States was expected to solidify these developments, preserve its preeminent regional position, and foster regional prosperity (Williams 2015). Indeed, the early 1990s did see the United States exerting considerable influence in regional affairs. The Brady Plan, which was designed to restructure and reduce the debt of those countries in the region who agreed to implement structural adjustments; the North American Free Trade Agreement; restoring to power Haiti’s ousted President Aristide; the joint US-International Monetary Fund aid package in response to the Mexican currency crisis; and antinarcotics programs, such as the Andean Initiative and Plan Colombia, were the outcomes of US leadership (Lowenthal and Baron 2015). However, it is hard to deny that Washington’s regional influence has been in constant decline in the past three decades. Hakim (2006) argues that the erosion of communist threat not only reduced the incentives for   the United States to engage in regional affairs but also increased the anticipated costs of intervention. Both sides lost a reliable framework for the regional order following the end of the Cold War, making it difficult for them to define a path for US-Latin American relations (Smith 2012). The declining influence of the United States became even more apparent after the attacks of 9/11. It caused the focus of US foreign policy to drastically shift to anti-terrorism and the Middle East. A new foreign policy rationale of the Bush administration based on anti-terrorism and homeland security put Latin American affairs on the back burner. Since 2001, the United States has been attentive to the region only when there is a particularly troubling or urgent situation. The waning influence of the United States in the region provided Latin American countries with an unprecedented opportunity to pursue greater autonomy from what has been considered a client-patron relationship by the Latin American side. There were several incidents in which the United States failed to achieve its desired policy outcome in the region since the beginning of the new millennium. For instance, the majority of Latin American countries opposed its post-9/11 security agenda. Only Costa Rica, Colombia, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama supported a resolution endorsing the invasion of Iraq in the United Nations Security Council in 2003. The inattention given in US foreign policy toward the region was confirmed and reinforced during the Obama administration. Preoccupied by the global economic crisis and the continuing security challenges posed by Afghanistan, Iraq, and elsewhere in the Middle East, it mostly accepted the changed geopolitical landscape in the region (Whitehead and Nolte 2012). There were few instances which could be interpreted as the US attempts to establish a new foundation for cooperation in the region. The statement made by US Secretary of State John Kerry at the OAS in 2013 confirmed that the era of the Monroe Doctrine was over. In 2014, US president Barack Obama and Cuban president Raúl Castro announced the beginning of the process of normalizing US-Cuba relations. Nevertheless, the Latin American side did not consider these actions as a major shift in US foreign policy in Latin America. They were largely considered improvised, rather than products of a consistent strategic initiative. Given the leadership vacuum, Latin American countries acquired more space for maneuver. They continued to diversify economic and diplomatic ties with countries outside the Americas. At the same time, they established regional institutions that exclude the United States, such as the aforementioned CELAC, as well as the Union of South American Nations (UNASUR), and the Bolivarian Alliance   for the Peoples of Our America (ALBA), although the UNASUR and ALBA had effectively collapsed by 2018 and 2019 respectively. China arrived at a perfect moment, one in which the regional hegemon was preoccupied elsewhere and Latin American countries had begun to search for greater autonomy from the hegemon. The acceptance of China into the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001 marked the beginning of the expansion of its trade with the region. Later, investment and operations by Chinese policy banks and companies in the region began to take off, providing China with a growing stake in the domestic affairs of the region. Although US trade and OFDI flows to the region still tower over those between China and the region, as pointed out by Wise and Ching (2017), China has clearly become a major trading partner, investor, and lender in the region. Recent trends in trade between China and Latin America have attracted considerable scholarly attention (Li 2007; Jenkins 2012). Data from United Nations Comtrade Database (2019) reveal that the volume of bilateral trade between China and 18 Latin American countries has increased substantially over the past decades. The Latin American side exported only about $2.5 billion to China in 1995, but their exports amounted to about $19 billion in 2005 and $94 billion in 2017. Its imports from China were approximately $2.5 billion in 1995, but they recorded $34 billion in 2005 and $169 billion in 2017. The five economies of the region with the largest volume of bilateral trade with China in 2017 were Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, and Peru. The Chinese share in total exports and imports of Latin American countries has also increased considerably. In 1995, Chinese share in total exports and imports of 18 Latin American countries was only about 1 percent. In 2017, exports to China captured 10 percent share in the region’s total exports and 18 percent in its total imports. In the same year, exports to the United States accounted for 45 percent of the region’s total exports and imports from the United States occupied 32 percent of its total imports. At the country level, the share of China in total exports was 27 percent for Chile, 26 percent for Peru, 21 percent for Brazil, 18 percent for Uruguay, and 7 percent for Argentina in 2017. However, China has not become a major export destination for Central American countries. Imports from China grew at a faster rate than exports to China for the majority of the countries in the region. In 2017, Chinese share in total imports exceeded 20 percent in a number of countries in South America and 10 percent in most of Central American countries. Although trade between the two sides took off in the early 2000s, investments and loans to the region began to increase dramatically in the   later part of the decade. There are two main forms of capital outflows: outward foreign direct investment (OFDI) and lending by Chinese policy banks. Chinese government agencies and companies have invested in various projects in the region, ranging from natural resources production and extraction to road construction and hydro-electrical projects. Table 1 summarizes data on Chinese OFDI directed to the region from 2000 to 2018. Between 2000 and 2005, Chinese OFDI in Latin America recorded only about $4.4 billion, but reached $33 billion from 2006 to 2010 and $45 billion between 2015 and 2018. The top four recipient countries of Chinese OFDI since 2000 are Brazil, Peru, Argentina, and Chile. Nevertheless, the region’s OFDI inflows are still dominated by the United States. According to Council on Foreign Relations (2015), the United States accounted for 20 percent of the region’s OFDI inflows in 2014, compared with China’s 6 percent share in the same year.  As can be seen in Table 2, China has acted as a major provider of loans to Latin American countries since Chinese lending to the region took off in the mid-2000s. Data from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) reveal that the amount of Chinese loans to the region has continuously increased. Only nine countries in the region received Chinese loans from 2005 to 2018. One half of total Chinese lending to the region was directed to Venezuela, amounting to more than $67 billion. Brazil was ranked second with 21 percent. Ecuador and Argentina received 14 percent and 12 percent each.  Chinese policy banks have emerged as an important alternative to traditional lenders for the countries that have had difficulties in borrowing in global capital markets due to high risk (Dollar 2017). This means that they have provided financing to a significantly different set of countries in the region from the conventional sources of capital such as the World Bank, IDB, and Western banks (Urdinez et al. 2016). According to Kaplan (2016), Chinese lenders are characterized by risk tolerance, avoiding harsh policy conditions or credit being contingent on a country’s macroeconomic performance. However, it is pointed out that they often require oil sales agreements or equipment purchases from the borrowing governments. Several Latin American countries have been leveraging their future oil assets and profitability to attract Chinese lending. Policy Loans as an Instrument of Soft Balancing Keohane (1984) links control over capital to hegemony—in politics, money can buy friendship and loyalty. According to this view, the emergence   of China as a major lender in Latin America should pose a threat to US hegemony in the region. However, are these recent dynamics a reflection of China’s soft balancing against the regional hegemon? It is pivotal to ask whether its lending behavior across the region is motivated by its geopolitical goals or a consequence of independent economic motives for profit. We argue that China’s outbound loans feature geopolitical reasons rather than profit maximization goals. China’s outbound loans, mainly issued through its policy banks, China Export-Import Bank and China Development Bank, are almost exclusively official lending, signifying that the Chinese state plays a critical role in its policy banks’ lending decisions (Zhang and Heyang 2019). Indeed, these policy banks operate under the direct ownership and supervision of the Ministry of Finance and the Chinese State Council (Horn et al. 2019). The government ownership of policy banks allows Beijing to strategically employ state-driven lending to enter new markets, secure natural resources, and further China’s global ambitions. Chinese policy loans have always had a strategic element. In our empirical analysis covering 18 countries in the region from 2005 to 2018, we specifically estimate the effects of maintaining a strategic partnership with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions. We also inquire how a host Latin American country’s ties with the United States affect the likelihood of Chinese policy loans. One may point out that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are driven not by Beijing’s soft balancing strategy but rather by demands from Latin American governments. It could be that the countries that are marginalized by the United States or non-beneficiaries of the traditional Western lending agencies are pursuing a strategy of diversification and turning to China as an alternative source of financing (Urdinez et al. 2016). This argument gives agency to Latin American countries and points to the ideological affinities between China and a number of left-wing Latin American governments in the recent past (Kaplan 2019). In light of these considerations, we introduce two “control” dependent variables, an empirical strategy similar to the one employed in Urdinez et al. (2016), to support our hypothesis that Chinese lending decisions are supply-driven—i.e., strategically-driven—rather than demand-driven. We compare the determinants of Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions with those of two other forms of China’s capital exports, OFDI by Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and private-owned enterprises (POEs). Geopolitical underpinnings are expected to be most obvious in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks.   Investment decisions by SOEs are likely to be less conditioned by geopolitical interests of the Chinese government, as there is less room for state involvement in the decision making process. Political bias of this kind is expected to be much weaker when OFDI decisions are made by POEs, in which private agents play the main role in searching for yield. If we observe that the determinants of lending decisions and OFDI decisions by SOEs and POEs are significantly different from one another, it can be said that Beijing has a significant degree of agency in its engagement with Latin American countries—otherwise, the countries in the region would be almost equally receptive to the three forms of Chinese capital exports. We can then argue that Chinese policy loans are primarily a product of an underlying soft balancing strategy of the Chinese government, rather than an outcome of the will of a number of Latin American countries to approach Beijing for loans. It would be naive to say that all the outbound investment decisions are made at the behest of Beijing. However, it would be equally wrong to assume that the Chinese government does not exercise control over SOEs operating in industries and areas of strategic importance when they make OFDI decisions. Some point out that the extent to which the Chinese state drives SOEs’ decisions is overstated (Cogman, Gao, and Leung 2017). Indeed, a careful scrutiny of China’s SOEs reveals that their linkages with the state are becoming thinner and outbound investment decisions by SOEs, especially those involving acquisition deals, are increasingly driven by commercial logic rather than dictates of the state (Collier 2018). However, following a developmental state path, the Chinese government still subordinates its SOEs through several regulatory institutions such as the State Council, Ministry of Commerce, People’s Bank of China, State Administration of Foreign Exchange, and State Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, while relinquishing control of less important enterprises (Mattlin 2009; Luo, Xue, and Han 2010; Chen 2018). On the other hand, Chinese POEs are significantly less stifled by the institutional environment for their OFDI compared with SOEs, especially in terms of Chinese government influence that they have to deal with. For example, all SOEs are required to apply for OFDI approval from the Ministry of Commerce, whereas OFDI projects by POEs only require approval by provincial-level Foreign Economic Relation and Trade (Luo, Xue, and Han 2010). Studies on the determinants of Chinese lending across Latin America are rare. Urdinez et al. (2016) find that there is an inverse relationship between the amount of Chinese policy bank loans and US hegemonic influence in host countries in the region. However, their analysis is   centered on the determinants of the amount of lending, which is problematic as Chinese policy banks lend only to a selected set of countries in the region. Those who do not borrow at all from China can produce biased coefficients. Kaplan (2019) tests for supply and demand-side determinants of Chinese policy loans to Latin American governments. Similar to our work, a binary dependent variable is employed to observe if a Latin American government has a Chinese bilateral loan outstanding as a share of its external debt stock in a given year. However, the analysis is focused on the effect of host governments’ partisanship on the likelihood of Chinese policy loans and ultimately the influence of Chinese lending on fiscal policy choices of Latin American governments. We introduce three dependent variables in our empirical analysis. All of them are binary variables which are equal to 1 if a host Latin American country receives any lending from Chinese policy banks, OFDI by Chinese SOEs, and OFDI by Chinese POEs, respectively, and 0 otherwise. The time period we cover is from 2005 to 2018 for the regressions on lending decisions and 2000 to 2018 for the regressions on OFDI decisions. Data come from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) and Red ALC-China (2019). GDP, per capita GDP, inflation rate, external public debt as a percentage of GDP, rule of law, and US interest are included to test if Chinese loans and OFDI seek for bigger markets or better business environment, or are influenced by global financial conditions. One can argue that the nature of Chinese economic engagement in the region is resource-seeking. Host countries’ natural resource rents as a percentage of GDP are included to control for wealth in natural resources. Data for these variables come from World Development Indicators (2019), Worldwide Governance Indicators (2019), and United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (2019). As for our explanatory variables, two binary variables are included to estimate the effects of having a strategic partnership status with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese lending and OFDI decisions. We also include a set of variables to test whether they are sensitive to US influence over host Latin countries. A dummy variable to observe the presence of a free trade agreement (FTA) with the United States, and two variables that capture the share of the United States in their total exports and imports are introduced. Compared to Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions, Chinese SOEs are more market sensitive and less conditioned by the US posturing in its backyard. The presence of geopolitical underpinnings in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks and OFDI decisions by Chinese SOEs to a lesser extent, and their relative absence in OFDI decisions by Chinese POEs    suggest that China’s lending behavior in the region is shaped by strategic planning designed to link its economic endowments to national foreign policy objectives, which is, soft-balancing against the United States in the United States’ backyard. Institution-Building Efforts as an Instrument of Soft Balancing The growth of Sino-Latin American trade and Chinese investments and lending to the region has been complemented by the improving diplomatic relations between the two sides. In 1990, just five years after Premier Zhao Ziyang embarked on the first high-level visit to Latin America, President Yang Shangkun paid the first visit as the head of the state. China declared that its relations with Latin America should be based upon the strategy of non-interference. In the 1990s, China’s Going Out strategy, championed by President Jiang Zemin, identified the region as a target for strategic partnership. The beginning of the twenty-first century saw cooperation between China and Latin America deepen rapidly. President Jiang Zemin’s tour to the region in 2001 followed by that of President Hu Jintao in 2005, and subsequent exchange by senior Chinese and regional officials and business leaders, drew the two sides markedly closer to each other. Moreover, in 2004, China earned observer status in the OAS. The China-Chile FTA entered into force in 2006, China-Peru FTA in 2010, and China-Costa Rica FTA in 2011. In its first ever policy paper on the region in 2008,3  the Chinese government officially declared that it would adopt a more strategic approach to Latin America. The paper emphasized support for new regional institutions, including CELAC, which have the potential to counterbalance the US-led organizations, such as the OAS and IDB. Since then, China has demonstrated robust institution-building efforts, and it appears that such efforts are paying off. China has successfully immersed itself in a web of regional institutions and relationships, both bilateral and multilateral, through which it now pursues its strategic goals, such as initiating military engagements and sales, extending the reach of the BRI, garnering regional support for the One China Policy, internationalizing its currency, and securing investments in strategically important waterways. China has continued to strengthen bilateral strategic relationships in the region. China has established comprehensive strategic partnerships—the third highest level of bilateral ties in its diplomatic lexicon—  with Brazil (2012), Mexico (2013), Peru (2013), Venezuela (2014), Argentina (2014), Chile (2016), and Ecuador (2016) and strategic partnerships—the fourth level of bilateral ties after comprehensive strategic partnerships—with Costa Rica (2015), Uruguay (2016), and Bolivia (2018). The characterization of these partnerships mean more than diplomatic talking points. Their stated purpose is to coordinate more closely on regional and international affairs, even including military ones. In its 2015 National Defense Strategy White Paper, the Chinese government reaffirmed its interest in maintaining friendly military ties with Latin American counterparts (State Council Information Office of the People’s Republic of China 2015). After Xi Jinping took office in 2013, China’s diplomatic offensive to the region has been more active than ever. In 2014, Xi, during his trip to Brazil for the BRICs Summit, proposed to build a new “1+3+6” cooperation framework: “1” refers to the China-Latin American Countries and Caribbean States Cooperation Plan (2015–2019), which is expected to serve as a blueprint for economic growth and sustainable development; “3” denotes three engines—trade, investment, and financial cooperation—that will drive practical cooperation; and “6” means six cooperation priorities, such as energy and resources, infrastructure construction, agriculture, manufacturing, scientific and technological innovation, and information technology. China’s participation in CELAC and the establishment of the China-CELAC Forum opened a new chapter for China’s strategic engagement in the region. The first Ministerial Meeting of the China-CELAC Forum was held in 2015. According to Yu (2015), the meeting was a landmark in Sino–Latin American relations, signaling that cooperation between the two sides substantially expanded into the geopolitical and strategic realms. The Beijing Declaration issued by the Forum was a clear demonstration that neither China nor Latin America was satisfied with the US-dominated unipolar system. The second white paper on Latin America and the Caribbean released in 2016 was another forthright statement of China’s strategic approach to the region. It is widely believed that this effort was primarily aimed at addressing concerns over Chinese OFDI in the United States. The escalating US containment toward China both within and outside the region has not yet caused Beijing to look to more assertive or outright behavior in the region, strengthening our arguments about China’s intentions of soft balancing against the regional hegemon.  Conclusion Pursuing a multifaceted approach as a major trading partner, investor, lender, and diplomatic partner, China has established a remarkable presence in Latin America over a relatively short period of time. We argue that Beijing’s intensifying economic and political clout in the region is an outcome of its soft balancing strategy, designed to advance its geopolitical goal of increasing its influence in America’s backyard without directly confronting the regional hegemon. China has used its financial power—loans by state-owned policy banks—as a tool to draw the countries with strategic value to Beijing into its political orbit. In that regard, it can be argued China’s policy banks are the key institutions underpinning China’s soft balancing strategy in the region. Chinese soft balancing is also evident in its continuing activism in strengthening diplomatic arrangements with the countries in the region and engaging in the CELAC and the China-CELAC Forum. China’s expanding economic and political influence in the Western Hemisphere has interrelated implications for the intensifying US-China strategic competition. China’s growing leverage in the region will serve to shift the regional balance of power in favor of China if the Chinese economy continues to grow and Beijing remains committed to the region. Beijing’s activism in regional institutions will be useful in expanding its role <mark>as a major trading partner</mark>, investor and lender in the region, and translating its economic clout into geopolitical influence. China’s growing role in the region is not likely to immediately transform into more assertive behavior. Yet its institutional arrangements and economic interdependence between the two sides will allow <mark>China</mark> to gradually <mark>extract concessions from</mark> regional <mark>powers, chipping away at</mark> American <mark>dominance</mark> in the region. In the long run, it may lead to the emergence of a counterbalancing coalition against the US-led unilateralism.
