## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case does not contain pre-written impact calculus or weighing mechanisms embedded within the evidence itself. While the Case Overview asserts that "A vote for the Negative is a vote to preserve the improving status quo instead of furthering a faulty solution that erodes the human rights of migrants and Americans," this framing exists only as a rhetorical conclusion, not as extended card text with comparative warrants. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like **"Status quo legal pathways solve migration management without the lethal externalities of surveillance expansion"** and cite a recent MPI or CBP analysis quantifying the reduction in unlawful entries alongside the humanitarian costs of enforcement-only strategies, placed at the end of the Case Overview to function as a mini-framework shell.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** The case includes no cards that proactively attack anticipated Affirmative positions (e.g., deterrence, fentanyl interdiction, operational control, or "smart wall" efficacy). The Kan (2017) and Phippen (2021) evidence is deployed solely to support the Negative's own funnel-effect/solvency-deficit narrative, not to preempt specific Affirmative contentions with offensive link turns or impact defense. A spike card would carry a tag like **"Surveillance expansion increases fentanyl trafficking by incentivizing cartels to shift to harder-to-detect, higher-potency opioids"** citing a DEA or RAND study post-2020, placed as a standalone block after Contention 1 to be extended as offense against a likely Affirmative drug-interdiction advantage.

### Defensive spikes
**Soto (2018)** — *"A migration funnel effect has resulted from border security operations... Security forces deliberately amassed along established migration routes... in order to deter migration with the prospect of high risk crossings in more remote, rugged, wilderness areas... Over the years, the deterrence orientation of border security funneled undocumented migration into increasingly high-risk geography leading to ensuing discussions of the funnel effect centered on the exceptional and structural violence of U.S. border control."*  
**How it executes:** This card functions as an embedded frontline against the Affirmative's inevitable "technology reduces risk by detecting migrants earlier" rebuttal. By warranting that the funnel effect is a *structural consequence of deterrence-by-design* — not a failure of specific legacy technology — it preempts the "new tech solves" response: even perfect detection merely displaces crossing to more lethal terrain, because the policy logic remains deterrence-through-risk.

### Evidence quality
**Phippen (2021)** — Politico Magazine, December 10, 2021.  
**How it executes:** The evidence is high-quality for PF: a long-form investigative piece by a seasoned national-security journalist (Politico/The Atlantic) with on-the-record interviews from CBP's INVNT office, GAO reports, academic researchers (Boyce, Longo, Chaar López), and Anduril executives. It provides a 15-year dataset of programmatic failures (SBInet, Elbit IFT, Anduril) with specific cost figures, technical specifications, and institutional admissions that CBP "hasn't figured out how to measure what success looks like." The recency (2021) captures the Anduril contract award and Biden-era continuation, making it difficult for Affirmative evidence comparisons to dismiss as "outdated pre-AI systems."

**How it executes:** Author is a former INS Commissioner and senior fellow at MPI, the field's preeminent nonpartisan think tank. The card cites FY2023 CBP administrative data (160k fewer unauthorized crossings vs. 2022; encounters down from 252k/month peak) — a primary-government dataset that outperforms typical secondary-source citations in evidence comparison.

### Collapse flexibility
**ABSENT:** The case is structurally pinned to a single contention ("Protecting Human Rights") with two sub-points (funnel effect deaths + surveillance technological failure) that both collapse to the *same terminal impact*: migrant deaths in remote terrain. There is no independent impact scenario (e.g., constitutional rights violations for U.S. citizens in border zones, Fourth Amendment erosion, environmental damage to protected lands, or authoritarian mission creep) that would allow the Negative to jettison the funnel-effect link if the Affirmative convincingly mitigates it. A second contention tag would read: **"Expanded surveillance infrastructure militarizes the border zone, eroding Fourth Amendment protections for millions of U.S. citizens and legal residents living within the 100-mile enforcement perimeter"** citing ACLU or Cato Institute analyses of inland checkpoint expansion and warrantless surveillance authority.

### Warrant depth
**Kan (2017)** — *"Rule 1: A Cartel's Business is Business... Increased security along the border will not change demand for the goods and services that the cartels supply. In fact, as new barriers along the border increase risks for the cartels, they will push migrants to innovate smuggling operations, raise their prices to keep profits flowing, and stimulate new domestic markets... These adaptations occurred after 9/11, the last time the United States seriously tightened its border security."*  
**How it executes:** The tagline claims surveillance expansion fails to deter cartels; the card supplies a microeconomic warrant (cartels as "vicious firms" obeying supply/demand) and a historical analog (post-9/11 adaptations: tunnels, false docs, corrupted ports of entry) explaining *why* the mechanism holds. The warrant is internal to the evidence, not merely asserted.

**Soto (2018)** — See Defensive Spikes above. The card provides a 16-year empirical warrant (2001–2016 Pima County mortality data: 181 avg. deaths/year) and a theoretical warrant (deterrence-by-design logic) for the funnel effect, moving beyond a mere claim that "surveillance pushes migrants to dangerous areas."

**Phippen (2021)** — See Evidence Quality above. The card supplies layered warrants: technical (radar blind spots, thermal distortion, false-positive rates), institutional (GAO reports, CBP inability to define success metrics), and adaptive (smuggler countermeasures evolving faster than procurement cycles) — each tied to specific failed programs (SBInet, Elbit, Anduril).

### Internal consistency
**ABSENT:** The Case Overview cites Meissner (2023) to claim the status quo is *improving* ("policies are succeeding in shifting flows to ports of entry and reducing illegal crossings"), while Contention 1 argues that *any* surveillance expansion funnels migrants to death. These claims are in tension: if current surveillance (towers, sensors, agents) is already deployed and the Meissner evidence credits recent *policy* changes (legal pathways, safe mobility offices, Mexico cooperation) — not surveillance reduction — for the drop in crossings, then the Negative's own evidence suggests the funnel effect persists *alongside* the improving metrics. The case never reconciles whether the "improving status quo" includes the surveillance infrastructure the 1AC wants to expand, creating a vulnerability to Affirmative cross-examination: "You say the status quo is working *and* that surveillance kills — but the status quo *is* surveillance. Which is it?" A consistency card would explicitly distinguish *enforcement-only* surveillance (the resolution's mandate) from *complementary* legal-pathway policies (the Meissner mechanism), tagging: **"The Meissner-cited reductions stem from legal pathways and regional cooperation — not surveillance expansion — proving the Affirmative's mechanism is unnecessary for border management."**
