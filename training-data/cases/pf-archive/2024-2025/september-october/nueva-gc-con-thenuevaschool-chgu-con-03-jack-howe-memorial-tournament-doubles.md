What is new and unusual is not the officer’s act of stalking, but how he did it: by tracking his wife’s car through the use of ALPRs.
License plate readers are pitched as a way to identify “criminals” who might be driving around undetected, especially amid a supposed (often unsubstantiated) rise in car-related crimes. However, as police across the United States increasingly deploy ALPRs to scan license plates and track the movement of thousands of cars every day, we must question their use as a “safety” measure. In fact, we must recognize <mark>the broader expansion of surveillance</mark> technologies for what it <mark>is: a force</mark> that makes everyone less safe by <mark>upholding white supremacy</mark> and other systems of oppression.
Law enforcement agencies, including Immigration and Customs Enforcement, use ALPRS to identify cars on a “hot list” — a list of license plate numbers deemed to be tied to cars and people involved in criminal acts — which enables police to move faster and more deliberately to arrest the drivers of those vehicles. The adoption of ALPRs has seeded a huge private industry where companies like Motorola Solutions, ELSAG and Flock Safety earn millions by deploying these cameras and collecting billions of data points annually.
ALPRs are just the tip of the iceberg as surveillance cameras, facial recognition technologies, and other products increasingly monitor people 24/7. The officer in Wichita using the surveillance technology at his disposal toward a violent end is not an anomaly. There are numerous other incidents where <mark>police have used databases to illegally identify people, abused facial recognition technology</mark> when trying to identify a suspect and have requested a surveillance tech provider change data leading to someone being charged with murder, among other incidents.
Plain and simple, surveillance technologies are not designed to create safer communities. Creating safer communities would require increased public funding and resources to address the root causes of poverty and other reasons people commit acts of violence. Instead, <mark>surveillance</mark> technologies <mark>create a dragnet to monitor</mark> nearly everyone and collect data on <mark>all of our behaviors</mark>.
However, while everyone is being tracked, not everyone is being pursued and criminalized; police choose to use surveillance technologies like ALPRs against specific, marginalized sets of people. <mark>Police make the subjective decision as to whom to monitor and how. Police</mark> then <mark>use</mark> these <mark>datasets to justify the ongoing criminalization of Black, Brown</mark>, queer, trans,<mark> immigrant, disabled and poor communities</mark>. <mark>Police</mark> then use the crime data they produce to <mark>call for more surveillance. The cycle repeats itself</mark>. Surveillance technologies intensify and exacerbate the criminalization of already heavily criminalized people.
In reality, the use of surveillance technologies actually makes communities less safe. Instead of interrupting violence, these technologies invite it. First, surveillance tools increase the presence of police in communities which have been decimated by mass incarceration. Second, surveillance technologies increase the potential and threat of police contact, which always contains the possibility of physical violence. Finally, by enabling more interaction between residents and police, these technologies open the door for more residents to be subjected to abusive practices like stop and frisk, arrest, jailing and potentially prison time, simply because the technology initiated a police deployment, even if no violence was committed.
All of this must also be considered against the fact that <mark>there is no conclusive evidence that any of these technologies have a</mark> clear <mark>impact on reducing harm</mark> or violence. <mark>In criminalized communities, surveillance</mark> technologies <mark>create the conditions for more violence</mark> by facilitating the purpose of policing — punishment, not safety.
The ubiquity of digital surveillance technology is the latest example of how the state maintains a monopoly on defining what is the legitimate use of force and violence through law, policy and practice. Elected officials, judges and police create, define and enforce the legal boundaries which mark when force and violence can be used in acceptable ways under the law. With little public oversight or awareness, surveillance technologies have quickly been ushered into the realm of what are considered acceptable tools for use by police, schools, and other public agencies.
In the abstract, proponents only speak to how these tools will be used to monitor people performing behaviors and actions which are currently deemed to be criminal. The rise of computing technologies birthed new methods of surveillance which police and other entities have implemented with little understanding of the full scope of the potential for invasive violence each tool has. Elected officials and law enforcement claim that these surveillance technologies are urgently necessary because they will help to curb violence and promote community safety. But we must ask, whose lives are made safer by these tools?
The news from Wichita illustrates another layer of the state’s calculation regarding the legitimate use of force: Elected officials who approve surveillance technology contracts value order more than safety. Surveillance technologies inherently lack controls and preventative oversight is nearly impossible. The only accountability measure within these surveillance tools is an audit log of police use after the technology is used. Police can employ these tools for their own designs and then, if caught, ask for forgiveness later. This only becomes an issue for officers if they overstep the line on what the state defines as acceptable and are caught doing so, as in the Wichita case. These tools are violent, no matter if the tools are used properly or improperly.

His captors wanted more money, the stepson said, and were pounding Mr. Ferrera's hands repeatedly with a hammer, vowing to continue until his family sent it. When federal agents and sheriff’s deputies descended on the house, they discovered that Mr. Ferrara was not the sole victim. Organized crime cartels, he said, had “brought this terror across the border.” But if it was one of the first such cases, it was not the last. Only Costa Rica, Colombia, Dominican Republic, El Salvador, Honduras, Nicaragua, and Panama supported a resolution endorsing the invasion of Iraq in the United Nations Security Council in 2003. The inattention given in US foreign policy toward the region was confirmed and reinforced during the Obama administration. Preoccupied by the global economic crisis and the continuing security challenges posed by Afghanistan, Iraq, and elsewhere in the Middle East, it mostly accepted the changed geopolitical landscape in the region (Whitehead and Nolte 2012). There were few instances which could be interpreted as the US attempts to establish a new foundation for cooperation in the region. The statement made by US Secretary of State John Kerry at the OAS in 2013 confirmed that the era of the Monroe Doctrine was over. In 2014, US president Barack Obama and Cuban president Raúl Castro announced the beginning of the process of normalizing US-Cuba relations. Nevertheless, the Latin American side did not consider these actions as a major shift in US foreign policy in Latin America. They were largely considered improvised, rather than products of a consistent strategic initiative. Given the leadership vacuum, Latin American countries acquired more space for maneuver. They continued to diversify economic and diplomatic ties with countries outside the Americas. At the same time, they established regional institutions that exclude the United States, such as the aforementioned CELAC, as well as the Union of South American Nations (UNASUR), and the Bolivarian Alliance   for the Peoples of Our America (ALBA), although the UNASUR and ALBA had effectively collapsed by 2018 and 2019 respectively. China arrived at a perfect moment, one in which the regional hegemon was preoccupied elsewhere and Latin American countries had begun to search for greater autonomy from the hegemon. The acceptance of China into the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001 marked the beginning of the expansion of its trade with the region. Later, investment and operations by Chinese policy banks and companies in the region began to take off, providing China with a growing stake in the domestic affairs of the region. Although US trade and OFDI flows to the region still tower over those between China and the region, as pointed out by Wise and Ching (2017), China has clearly become a major trading partner, investor, and lender in the region. Recent trends in trade between China and Latin America have attracted considerable scholarly attention (Li 2007; Jenkins 2012). Data from United Nations Comtrade Database (2019) reveal that the volume of bilateral trade between China and 18 Latin American countries has increased substantially over the past decades. The Latin American side exported only about $2.5 billion to China in 1995, but their exports amounted to about $19 billion in 2005 and $94 billion in 2017. Its imports from China were approximately $2.5 billion in 1995, but they recorded $34 billion in 2005 and $169 billion in 2017. The five economies of the region with the largest volume of bilateral trade with China in 2017 were Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, and Peru. The Chinese share in total exports and imports of Latin American countries has also increased considerably. In 1995, Chinese share in total exports and imports of 18 Latin American countries was only about 1 percent. In 2017, exports to China captured 10 percent share in the region’s total exports and 18 percent in its total imports. In the same year, exports to the United States accounted for 45 percent of the region’s total exports and imports from the United States occupied 32 percent of its total imports. At the country level, the share of China in total exports was 27 percent for Chile, 26 percent for Peru, 21 percent for Brazil, 18 percent for Uruguay, and 7 percent for Argentina in 2017. However, China has not become a major export destination for Central American countries. Imports from China grew at a faster rate than exports to China for the majority of the countries in the region. In 2017, Chinese share in total imports exceeded 20 percent in a number of countries in South America and 10 percent in most of Central American countries. Although trade between the two sides took off in the early 2000s, investments and loans to the region began to increase dramatically in the   later part of the decade. There are two main forms of capital outflows: outward foreign direct investment (OFDI) and lending by Chinese policy banks. Chinese government agencies and companies have invested in various projects in the region, ranging from natural resources production and extraction to road construction and hydro-electrical projects. Table 1 summarizes data on Chinese OFDI directed to the region from 2000 to 2018. Between 2000 and 2005, Chinese OFDI in Latin America recorded only about $4.4 billion, but reached $33 billion from 2006 to 2010 and $45 billion between 2015 and 2018. The top four recipient countries of Chinese OFDI since 2000 are Brazil, Peru, Argentina, and Chile. Nevertheless, the region’s OFDI inflows are still dominated by the United States. According to Council on Foreign Relations (2015), the United States accounted for 20 percent of the region’s OFDI inflows in 2014, compared with China’s 6 percent share in the same year.  As can be seen in Table 2, China has acted as a major provider of loans to Latin American countries since Chinese lending to the region took off in the mid-2000s. Data from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) reveal that the amount of Chinese loans to the region has continuously increased. Only nine countries in the region received Chinese loans from 2005 to 2018. One half of total Chinese lending to the region was directed to Venezuela, amounting to more than $67 billion. Brazil was ranked second with 21 percent. Ecuador and Argentina received 14 percent and 12 percent each.  Chinese policy banks have emerged as an important alternative to traditional lenders for the countries that have had difficulties in borrowing in global capital markets due to high risk (Dollar 2017). This means that they have provided financing to a significantly different set of countries in the region from the conventional sources of capital such as the World Bank, IDB, and Western banks (Urdinez et al. 2016). According to Kaplan (2016), Chinese lenders are characterized by risk tolerance, avoiding harsh policy conditions or credit being contingent on a country’s macroeconomic performance. However, it is pointed out that they often require oil sales agreements or equipment purchases from the borrowing governments. Several Latin American countries have been leveraging their future oil assets and profitability to attract Chinese lending. Policy Loans as an Instrument of Soft Balancing Keohane (1984) links control over capital to hegemony—in politics, money can buy friendship and loyalty. According to this view, the emergence   of China as a major lender in Latin America should pose a threat to US hegemony in the region. However, are these recent dynamics a reflection of China’s soft balancing against the regional hegemon? It is pivotal to ask whether its lending behavior across the region is motivated by its geopolitical goals or a consequence of independent economic motives for profit. We argue that China’s outbound loans feature geopolitical reasons rather than profit maximization goals. China’s outbound loans, mainly issued through its policy banks, China Export-Import Bank and China Development Bank, are almost exclusively official lending, signifying that the Chinese state plays a critical role in its policy banks’ lending decisions (Zhang and Heyang 2019). Indeed, these policy banks operate under the direct ownership and supervision of the Ministry of Finance and the Chinese State Council (Horn et al. 2019). The government ownership of policy banks allows Beijing to strategically employ state-driven lending to enter new markets, secure natural resources, and further China’s global ambitions. Chinese policy loans have always had a strategic element. In our empirical analysis covering 18 countries in the region from 2005 to 2018, we specifically estimate the effects of maintaining a strategic partnership with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions. We also inquire how a host Latin American country’s ties with the United States affect the likelihood of Chinese policy loans. One may point out that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are driven not by Beijing’s soft balancing strategy but rather by demands from Latin American governments. It could be that the countries that are marginalized by the United States or non-beneficiaries of the traditional Western lending agencies are pursuing a strategy of diversification and turning to China as an alternative source of financing (Urdinez et al. 2016). This argument gives agency to Latin American countries and points to the ideological affinities between China and a number of left-wing Latin American governments in the recent past (Kaplan 2019). In light of these considerations, we introduce two “control” dependent variables, an empirical strategy similar to the one employed in Urdinez et al. (2016), to support our hypothesis that Chinese lending decisions are supply-driven—i.e., strategically-driven—rather than demand-driven. We compare the determinants of Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions with those of two other forms of China’s capital exports, OFDI by Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and private-owned enterprises (POEs). Geopolitical underpinnings are expected to be most obvious in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks.   Investment decisions by SOEs are likely to be less conditioned by geopolitical interests of the Chinese government, as there is less room for state involvement in the decision making process. Political bias of this kind is expected to be much weaker when OFDI decisions are made by POEs, in which private agents play the main role in searching for yield. If we observe that the determinants of lending decisions and OFDI decisions by SOEs and POEs are significantly different from one another, it can be said that Beijing has a significant degree of agency in its engagement with Latin American countries—otherwise, the countries in the region would be almost equally receptive to the three forms of Chinese capital exports. We can then argue that Chinese policy loans are primarily a product of an underlying soft balancing strategy of the Chinese government, rather than an outcome of the will of a number of Latin American countries to approach Beijing for loans. It would be naive to say that all the outbound investment decisions are made at the behest of Beijing. However, it would be equally wrong to assume that the Chinese government does not exercise control over SOEs operating in industries and areas of strategic importance when they make OFDI decisions. Some point out that the extent to which the Chinese state drives SOEs’ decisions is overstated (Cogman, Gao, and Leung 2017). Indeed, a careful scrutiny of China’s SOEs reveals that their linkages with the state are becoming thinner and outbound investment decisions by SOEs, especially those involving acquisition deals, are increasingly driven by commercial logic rather than dictates of the state (Collier 2018). However, following a developmental state path, the Chinese government still subordinates its SOEs through several regulatory institutions such as the State Council, Ministry of Commerce, People’s Bank of China, State Administration of Foreign Exchange, and State Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, while relinquishing control of less important enterprises (Mattlin 2009; Luo, Xue, and Han 2010; Chen 2018). On the other hand, Chinese POEs are significantly less stifled by the institutional environment for their OFDI compared with SOEs, especially in terms of Chinese government influence that they have to deal with. For example, all SOEs are required to apply for OFDI approval from the Ministry of Commerce, whereas OFDI projects by POEs only require approval by provincial-level Foreign Economic Relation and Trade (Luo, Xue, and Han 2010). Studies on the determinants of Chinese lending across Latin America are rare. Urdinez et al. (2016) find that there is an inverse relationship between the amount of Chinese policy bank loans and US hegemonic influence in host countries in the region. However, their analysis is   centered on the determinants of the amount of lending, which is problematic as Chinese policy banks lend only to a selected set of countries in the region. Those who do not borrow at all from China can produce biased coefficients. Kaplan (2019) tests for supply and demand-side determinants of Chinese policy loans to Latin American governments. Similar to our work, a binary dependent variable is employed to observe if a Latin American government has a Chinese bilateral loan outstanding as a share of its external debt stock in a given year. However, the analysis is focused on the effect of host governments’ partisanship on the likelihood of Chinese policy loans and ultimately the influence of Chinese lending on fiscal policy choices of Latin American governments. We introduce three dependent variables in our empirical analysis. All of them are binary variables which are equal to 1 if a host Latin American country receives any lending from Chinese policy banks, OFDI by Chinese SOEs, and OFDI by Chinese POEs, respectively, and 0 otherwise. The time period we cover is from 2005 to 2018 for the regressions on lending decisions and 2000 to 2018 for the regressions on OFDI decisions. Data come from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) and Red ALC-China (2019). In order to isolate the political determinants that are of interest to us from the economic ones, we introduce a number of control variables that can account for Chinese policy banks’ choices to issue loans or SOEs and POEs’ decisions to invest in a Latin American country. GDP, per capita GDP, inflation rate, external public debt as a percentage of GDP, rule of law, and US interest are included to test if Chinese loans and OFDI seek for bigger markets or better business environment, or are influenced by global financial conditions. One can argue that the nature of Chinese economic engagement in the region is resource-seeking. Host countries’ natural resource rents as a percentage of GDP are included to control for wealth in natural resources. Data for these variables come from World Development Indicators (2019), Worldwide Governance Indicators (2019), and United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (2019). As for our explanatory variables, two binary variables are included to estimate the effects of having a strategic partnership status with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese lending and OFDI decisions. We also include a set of variables to test whether they are sensitive to US influence over host Latin countries. A dummy variable to observe the presence of a free trade agreement (FTA) with the United States, and two variables that capture the share of the United States in their total exports and imports are introduced. Trade data come from United Nations Comtrade Database (2019).  Our probit regression results are presented in Table 3. Regressions (1), (2), and (3) suggest that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are positively associated with a host Latin American country’s market size. However, they seem tolerant of its business environment or stability, in contrast to traditional Western lending institutions. A rise in the US interest rate makes it more difficult for Latin American governments to borrow from Chinese policy banks, suggesting that global financial conditions do influence their lending decisions. With regard to our explanatory variables, although China rewards the countries in the region with a strategic partnership status, it piles pressure on those that recognize Taiwan, as it does in Africa (Dreher et al. 2018). The effect of the One China Policy on the likelihood of policy loans is not estimated in regressions (1), (2), and (3), as the Taiwan variable is perfectly collinear with the dependent variable. In all the cases in which Chinese lending was issued, the host country did not have formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan. Furthermore, Chinese policy banks tend to avoid those maintaining close economic ties with the United States when they make a lending decision. The three variables that measure US economic influence over host countries are all negatively related to the dependent variable. These results confirm that the Chinese government employs loans through its policy banks as a robust instrument of soft balancing against the United States in the region. Compared to Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions, Chinese SOEs are more market sensitive and less conditioned by the US posturing in its backyard. Regressions (4), (5), and (6) show that Chinese SOEs are more attracted to a Latin American country with a bigger market size and lower indebtedness. The coefficient on US interest rate is found to be negative. In the geopolitical realm, a strategic partnership status with China and the stance on the One China Policy are tied to the likelihood of OFDI by Chinese SOEs, as it was the case for lending decisions by Chinese policy banks. Among the three variables that measure US economic influence within host countries, only one variable that measures US share in imports is statistically significant. Regressions (7), (8), and (9) show that OFDI decisions by Chinese POEs are sensitive to market conditions and resource-seeking. What is markedly different about Chinese POEs is that they are almost independent of Beijing’s geopolitical considerations across the region, being sensitive only to a host Latin American country’s strategic partnership status, but not to its alignment with the One China Policy nor US influence within it. The presence of geopolitical underpinnings in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks and OFDI decisions by Chinese SOEs to a lesser extent, and their relative absence in OFDI decisions by Chinese POEs    suggest that China’s lending behavior in the region is shaped by strategic planning designed to link its economic endowments to national foreign policy objectives, which is, soft-balancing against the United States in the United States’ backyard. Institution-Building Efforts as an Instrument of Soft Balancing The growth of Sino-Latin American trade and Chinese investments and lending to the region has been complemented by the improving diplomatic relations between the two sides. In 1990, just five years after Premier Zhao Ziyang embarked on the first high-level visit to Latin America, President Yang Shangkun paid the first visit as the head of the state. China declared that its relations with Latin America should be based upon the strategy of non-interference. In the 1990s, China’s Going Out strategy, championed by President Jiang Zemin, identified the region as a target for strategic partnership. The beginning of the twenty-first century saw cooperation between China and Latin America deepen rapidly. President Jiang Zemin’s tour to the region in 2001 followed by that of President Hu Jintao in 2005, and subsequent exchange by senior Chinese and regional officials and business leaders, drew the two sides markedly closer to each other. Moreover, in 2004, China earned observer status in the OAS. The China-Chile FTA entered into force in 2006, China-Peru FTA in 2010, and China-Costa Rica FTA in 2011. In its first ever policy paper on the region in 2008,3  the Chinese government officially declared that it would adopt a more strategic approach to Latin America. The paper emphasized support for new regional institutions, including CELAC, which have the potential to counterbalance the US-led organizations, such as the OAS and IDB. Since then, China has demonstrated robust institution-building efforts, and it appears that such efforts are paying off. China has successfully immersed itself in a web of regional institutions and relationships, both bilateral and multilateral, through which it now pursues its strategic goals, such as initiating military engagements and sales, extending the reach of the BRI, garnering regional support for the One China Policy, internationalizing its currency, and securing investments in strategically important waterways. China has continued to strengthen bilateral strategic relationships in the region. China has established comprehensive strategic partnerships—the third highest level of bilateral ties in its diplomatic lexicon—  with Brazil (2012), Mexico (2013), Peru (2013), Venezuela (2014), Argentina (2014), Chile (2016), and Ecuador (2016) and strategic partnerships—the fourth level of bilateral ties after comprehensive strategic partnerships—with Costa Rica (2015), Uruguay (2016), and Bolivia (2018). The characterization of these partnerships mean more than diplomatic talking points. Their stated purpose is to coordinate more closely on regional and international affairs, even including military ones. In its 2015 National Defense Strategy White Paper, the Chinese government reaffirmed its interest in maintaining friendly military ties with Latin American counterparts (State Council Information Office of the People’s Republic of China 2015). After Xi Jinping took office in 2013, China’s diplomatic offensive to the region has been more active than ever. In 2014, Xi, during his trip to Brazil for the BRICs Summit, proposed to build a new “1+3+6” cooperation framework: “1” refers to the China-Latin American Countries and Caribbean States Cooperation Plan (2015–2019), which is expected to serve as a blueprint for economic growth and sustainable development; “3” denotes three engines—trade, investment, and financial cooperation—that will drive practical cooperation; and “6” means six cooperation priorities, such as energy and resources, infrastructure construction, agriculture, manufacturing, scientific and technological innovation, and information technology. China’s participation in CELAC and the establishment of the China-CELAC Forum opened a new chapter for China’s strategic engagement in the region. The first Ministerial Meeting of the China-CELAC Forum was held in 2015. According to Yu (2015), the meeting was a landmark in Sino–Latin American relations, signaling that cooperation between the two sides substantially expanded into the geopolitical and strategic realms. The Beijing Declaration issued by the Forum was a clear demonstration that neither China nor Latin America was satisfied with the US-dominated unipolar system. The second white paper on Latin America and the Caribbean released in 2016 was another forthright statement of China’s strategic approach to the region. While reaffirming its commitments to the “1+3+6” cooperation framework, the Chinese government vowed to explore a “3x3” model for capacity cooperation. First, three passages—logistics, power, and information technology—would be constructed to boost domestic demand in Latin America. Second, three financing channels— funds, loans, and insurance—would be expanded to provide financing to bilateral projects. In support of the implementation of the model, China pledged financing of $30 billion in total. When materialized, the   two projects will ultimately transform the existing Sino-Latin American relations. The “1+3+6” and “3x3” frameworks signaled a subtle yet significant shift in China’s policy focus, from trade to more diversified engagement. While China under the leadership of Xi was embracing Latin American countries in a more active manner, US-Latin American relations deteriorated further since President Donald Trump took office. The growing US disengagement in the region under the Trump administration was marked by such actions as withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership, reduction of US foreign aid, and hostility toward Mexico and Latino immigrants in general. The Trump administration also rolled back the previous administration’s Cuba policy. The “America First” doctrine has in effect once again created a strategic void in the region, giving China an opportunity to fill the void and promote its interests. In 2017, China renewed its efforts to diplomatically isolate Taiwan in Latin America. Central American countries have constituted the biggest bloc of Taiwan’s few remaining world allies. Panama shifted its diplomatic alignment from Taiwan to China in 2017, followed by the Dominican Republic and El Salvador in 2018. In exchange, China promised investments and loans to these countries. Beijing’s renewed activism in promoting its One China Policy resulted in US concerns. For instance, immediately after El Salvador’s switchover, the White House announced that it would reevaluate its diplomatic relationship with El Salvador, warning that the “short-term economic benefits” offered by China would lead to “dependency and domination.” At the same time, the Chinese leadership, taking advantage of bilateral strategic partnerships and its position in the CELAC, has endeavored to extend the BRI to the region with noticeable success. Beijing issued a formal invitation to the BRI at the Second Ministerial Meeting of China-CELAC Forum in 2018. It was an effort to gain CELAC’s endorsement on the “natural extension” of the BRI, Xi’s signature foreign policy objective. Panama was the first country in the region to sign a cooperation agreement under the BRI banner, and many others including Bolivia, Chile, Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama, Peru, Uruguay, Venezuela, and several other Caribbean countries have followed suit, with the purpose of gaining access to infrastructure financing. However, one should note that some of the largest economies in the region such as Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico have not yet signed any BRI agreements. This shows that Beijing’s charm offensive in the region has its limits.  Another recent piece of evidence of China’s soft balancing against American power in the region is the expansion of its currency swap program with Argentina. In 2018, China and Argentina agreed to nearly double their existing currency swap deal, bringing the total to $18.7 billion. They first agreed to a currency swap program to boost Argentina’s falling reserves in 2009. The recession-struck country once again turned to China as a source of financing and used some of the funds to cover its emergency deficits. Though its effect is marginal, the currency swap echoes China’s intensifying efforts to gradually promote the renminbi into a global currency. In parallel, the extension of the BRI to Latin America is expected to bring about the more widespread use of the renminbi. While China’s soft balancing strategy relies on a low-key approach for the purpose of avoiding direct confrontation with the United States, China’s increasing presence in the areas where America’s vital interests are at stake is likely to trigger strong pushback from the United States. Panama is a case in point. After establishing formal ties with China in 2017, Panama officially pledged its support for the BRI. In less than two years, the two states have signed nearly 20 bilateral agreements in trade and infrastructure. Chinese state-owned enterprises represented by China Ocean Shipping Companies Group are actively involved in investments around the Panama Canal. In response, US Secretary of State Mike Pompeo, while visiting Panama in October 2018, warned Panama against accepting the BRI loans (Churchill 2018). Furthermore, the US Southern Command’s 2019 posture statement associated China’s infrastructure investments on the Panama Canal with US security interests. The Panama Canal could once again turn into a flashpoint for great-power conflict. Wary of China’s increasing ambition, Washington’s view on US-China relations in the Western Hemisphere seems to be gradually moving from win-win to zero-sum. The trilateral relationship in the region has been further complicated by the deepening US-China tension. The ongoing trade conflict between the two major powers, which began in 2018, froze their bilateral relations. Both sides have been setting titfor-tat tariffs and other trade barriers on each other. Although they have recently agreed to discuss the rolling back of tariffs in phases, it seems uncertain when this all-out trade war will come to an end. In addition, along with the increasing trade deficit with China, concerns regarding the dramatic increase in Chinese OFDI have been growing in the United States. The 2017 US National Security Strategy warned that the “national innovation base” should be protected from Beijing’s attempts to    vacuum up core US companies and technologies. In response, the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act was legislated in 2018. It is widely believed that this effort was primarily aimed at addressing concerns over Chinese OFDI in the United States. Despite the growing suspicion of the United States toward the expanded role played by China in Latin America and the deteriorating US-China relations, Beijing does not seem to have practiced any strategy of hard balancing in the region. The escalating US containment toward China both within and outside the region has not yet caused Beijing to look to more assertive or outright behavior in the region, strengthening our arguments about China’s intentions of soft balancing against the regional hegemon.  Conclusion Pursuing a multifaceted approach as a major trading partner, investor, lender, and diplomatic partner, China has established a remarkable presence in Latin America over a relatively short period of time. We argue that Beijing’s intensifying economic and political clout in the region is an outcome of its soft balancing strategy, designed to advance its geopolitical goal of increasing its influence in America’s backyard without directly confronting the regional hegemon. China has used its financial power—loans by state-owned policy banks—as a tool to draw the countries with strategic value to Beijing into its political orbit. In that regard, it can be argued China’s policy banks are the key institutions underpinning China’s soft balancing strategy in the region. Chinese soft balancing is also evident in its continuing activism in strengthening diplomatic arrangements with the countries in the region and engaging in the CELAC and the China-CELAC Forum. China’s expanding economic and political influence in the Western Hemisphere has interrelated implications for the intensifying US-China strategic competition. China’s growing leverage in the region will serve to shift the regional balance of power in favor of China if the Chinese economy continues to grow and Beijing remains committed to the region. Beijing’s activism in regional institutions will be useful in expanding its role as a major trading partner, investor and lender in the region, and translating its economic clout into geopolitical influence. China’s growing role in the region is not likely to immediately transform into more assertive behavior. Yet its institutional arrangements and economic interdependence between the two sides will allow China to gradually extract concessions from regional powers, chipping away at American dominance in the region. In the short run, China’s rallying of like-minded countries, or countries adversarial to the United States, can serve as a tool to advance narrow strategic interests such as the One China Policy and a hedge against the excesses of US hegemony. In the long run, it may lead to the emergence of a counterbalancing coalition against the US-led unilateralism. Ultimately, if China sustains the momentum, China’s growing influence in Latin America can seriously impair the ability of the United States to exercise effective leadership, not only in the Western Hemisphere but also in other areas of strategic interest. For one thing, if China consolidates its position as an indispensable partner to the region, more countries will begin to look to Beijing not only as an economic but also as a political alternative that can replace the hegemonic position of the United States. For another, if America’s backyard turns into China’s playground, the United States will then be, as realists argue, less free to exert its power outside the Western Hemisphere.

Although this risk is not high in absolute terms, its consequences for the region and the world would be devastating. As long as the United States and China continue to pursue their current grand strategies, the risk is likely to endure.
