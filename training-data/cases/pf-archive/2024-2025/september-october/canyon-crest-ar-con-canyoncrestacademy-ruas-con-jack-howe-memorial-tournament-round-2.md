## Construction-criteria audit

### Embedded weighing
**ABSENT:** The case contains no explicit weighing mechanisms — no magnitude, probability, or timeframe comparisons between contentions, no "even if" framing, and no pre-written impact calculus cards. Each contention (indigenous rights, deterrence failure, humanitarian harms) operates as a siloed narrative without a card that explains why its impacts outweigh the Pro's likely economic/security impacts. A card filling this gap would carry a tag like "Weighing — Structural violence of surveillance outweighs marginal deterrence gains: indigenous sovereignty violations are ongoing and irreversible vs. speculative border security improvements" and cite a comparative framework source (e.g., a human rights tribunal ruling or IR scholarship on securitization tradeoffs), placed as a standalone subpoint after C3 or as a cross-contention overview.

### Offensive spikes
**ABSENT:** No cards proactively dismantle core Pro positions such as "surveillance reduces illegal crossings," "technology is more humane than walls," or "border security prevents fentanyl trafficking." The case cites evidence that surveillance *fails* (Del Valle 22, Tyler 22, AIC 21) but never frames these as direct refutations of Pro's offensive warrants — e.g., no card tagged "Spike: Surveillance expansion does not reduce crossings — empirical data shows apprehensions rise regardless of tech investment (Del Valle 22)" or "Spike: Fentanyl enters through legal ports, not surveillance gaps (CBP data)." An offensive spike card would explicitly name the Pro argument it neuters, cite contrary evidence, and live in the contention most relevant to that clash (e.g., a fentanyl spike in C1 where enforcement spending is discussed).

### Collapse flexibility
**C1 (Enforcement-adjudication mismatch + AI bias + detention harms) — Three distinct terminal impacts from one contention.** The AIC 24 cards establish a structural funding imbalance ($24:$1) that makes *any* surveillance expansion futile without court reform (systemic solvency deficit). Morley 24 adds an independent civil rights impact (racialized AI harm) that triggers even if surveillance "works." Hansen 19 adds a humanitarian impact (detention deaths/mental health) that scales with enforcement volume. The 2AR can collapse to any one: solvency (AIC), rights (Morley), or humanitarian (Hansen) — no single link is indispensable.

**C2 (Indigenous rights) — Two non-redundant impact pathways.** Felbab-Brown 19 establishes environmental/cultural severance (26 nations affected, sacred sites destroyed) — a structural violence impact. Parrish 19 adds active surveillance harms (persistent monitoring, harassment, "apartheid" conditions) — an ongoing rights violation distinct from the wall-specific harms in Felbab-Brown. Collapse to either survives a "no wall in resolution" Pro block.

**C3 (Deterrence failure) — Only one clean collapse route (empirical denial).** Galvez 24 (crossings at 3-year low *without* new surveillance) and AIC 21 (historical spikes post-deterrence) both serve the same "surveillance doesn't reduce migration" link. Tyler 22 (funnel effect → deaths) and Crane 24 (cartel adaptation) are defensive/impact amplification, not alternative offense. If Pro concedes deterrence fails but argues "surveillance manages flow," C3 has no independent offense.

**Hansen 19 (C1) — Tag: "Detention centers cause physical/mental health collapse."** Warrant delivered: specific morbidity stats (80% dissatisfied with medical care, 86% depression/77% anxiety/50% PTSD in asylum seekers), mortality data (155 reported deaths 2003-2016, estimated 3-10x undercount), and causal mechanisms (segregation as punishment for seeking mental health care, lack of sunlight/outdoor access). Multi-study synthesis with quantified outcomes.

### Internal consistency
**C1 and C3 are tension-free but not synergistic.** C1 argues surveillance expansion is fiscally irrational (AIC 24: $409B spent, backlog exploding) and technologically discriminatory (Morley 24). C3 argues surveillance expansion is empirically unnecessary (Galvez 24: crossings at 3-year low without it) and counterproductive (Tyler 22: funnel effect). Both indict *expansion* — no contradiction. However, C1's "enforcement-adjudication mismatch" framing implicitly accepts enforcement *exists* (just underfunded courts), while C3's "deterrence never works" framing rejects enforcement efficacy entirely. A Pro "surveillance helps manage *existing* flow" argument could exploit this: C1 says "fund courts instead," C3 says "flow is unmanageable." No card reconciles or sequences these.

**C2 (Indigenous rights) stands fully independent** — no shared premises with C1/C3, no cross-contention contamination. Felbab-Brown 19 and Parrish 19 form a tight indigenous-sovereignty module that survives any Pro framework (util, rights, security). The case's framing uniformity is high *within* contentions but low *across* them: no overarching value criterion (e.g., "structural violence," "human security," "fiscal responsibility") binds C1/C2/C3 into a single ballot story.
