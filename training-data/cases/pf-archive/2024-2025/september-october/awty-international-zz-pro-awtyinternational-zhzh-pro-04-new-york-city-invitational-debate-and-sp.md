Produced by particle interactions in the upper atmosphere, cosmic ray muons arrive at the surface of the earth with a spread of energies up to 1 TeV and beyond [1], [2], [3]. The highest energy muons produced in this way <mark>can penetrate</mark> to depths of <mark>several km</mark> [2]. Lower energy muons lose all of their initial kinetic energy in the intervening material, stop and decay. The flux of muons therefore decreases from the surface to several km, falling 6 orders of magnitude over this distance [2]. For reasons of loss of signal, the practical limit of muon tomography is for depths of less than 1 km. For similar reasons, the flux of muons also falls with increasing zenith angle with respect to vertical [2]. The energy loss of muons to matter, called stopping power, is nearly linearly dependent on the density of the matter [4]. An increase in measured muon flux from a particular direction, compared to what was expected, would indicate a lower integral of density times distance in that direction compared with expectations. Multiple detectors allow density variations to be mapped out in 3D. Muon tomography has applications to a wide range of fields, including volcanology, geology, archaeology and engineering [1]. The first well-known application of muon tomography was the exploration of the interior of Chephren’s pyramid in Giza, Egypt by the Nobel Prize winning physicist Luis W. Alvarez in 1970 [1]. While no hidden chambers were detected in Chephren’s pyramid the technique has successfully detected chambers in other pyramids.
