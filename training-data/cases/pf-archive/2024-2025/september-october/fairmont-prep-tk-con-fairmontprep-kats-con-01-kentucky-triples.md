Remittances are money transfers
Ratha nd [Dilip Ratha, scholar of international migration and its relationship with global development, “What Are Remittances?”, International Monetary Fund, https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/basics/pdf/ratha-remittances.pdf //akang]
<mark>WHEN MIGRANTS send</mark> home<mark> part of their earnings</mark> in the form of either cash or goods to support their families, <mark>these transfers are </mark>known as workers’ or migrant <mark>remittances</mark>. They have been growing rapidly in the past few years and now represent <mark>the largest source of foreign income </mark>for many developing economies. It is hard to estimate the exact size of remittance flows because many take place through unofficial channels. Worldwide, officially recorded international migrant remittances are projected to reach $596 billion in 2017, with $450 billion flowing to developing economies. These are recorded in the balance of payments; exactly how to record them is being reviewed by an international technical group. Unrecorded flows through informal channels are believed to be at least 50 percent larger than recorded flows. Not only are remittances large but they are also more evenly distributed among developing economies than capital flows, including foreign direct investment. Remittances are especially important <mark>for low-income countries</mark> and account for nearly 4 percent of their GDP, compared with about 1.5 percent of GDP for middle-income countries.

The campaign to fight U.S. inflation by upping interest rates has been going on for a year and a half — and its impacts are being felt around the world. On July 26, 2023, the Federal Reserve announced another quarter-point hike. That means U.S. rates have gone up 5.25 percentage points over the past 18 months.
