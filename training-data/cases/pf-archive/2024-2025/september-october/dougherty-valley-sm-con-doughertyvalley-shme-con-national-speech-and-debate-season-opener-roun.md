In this paper we have used geospatial analysis to model the difficulty of crossing the U.S- Mexico border
on foot through a particular high-traffic corridor in southern Arizona. Difficulty, in this case, was
calculated as a measure of caloric expenditure based on vari- ables of terrain, distance, slope, change in
slope, and average human weight, load and walking speed. We then measured the impacts of the U.S.
Border Patrol’s SBInet surveil- lance system on potential migrant routes, and compared these potential
routes against the location of actual human remains processed by the Pima County, Arizona Office of the
Medical Examiner. We find a statistically meaningful difference in the distribution of human remains preand post-SBInet, an outcome that corresponds to the patterns of migration predicted in our model. In
short, our findings affirm that surveillance programs like SBInet work not only to increase rates of

detection or interdiction, but also operate in concert with the rugged desert climate and terrain to
maximize the hardship and suffering encountered by unauthorized migrants. A logical and inevitable
outcome is death, and an increase in the rate of mortality among unauthorized border crossers over
time. Our use of geospatial analysis ultimately provides geographic verification of the “funnel effect”
that has been suggested through statistical analysis of the relationship between death records and policy
changes along the U.S.-Mexico border (Rubio-Goldsmith et al. 2006). This relationship, until now, had not
been mapped on a local scale nor connected in any robust way to landscape conditions and human
physiology; and neither had there been, until now, a statistical verification of death locations connected
to the deployment of border surveillance infrastructure.
