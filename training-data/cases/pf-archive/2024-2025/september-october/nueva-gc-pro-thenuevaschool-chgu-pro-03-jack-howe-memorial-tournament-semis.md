The <mark>profitability of human trafficking</mark> and drug trafficking makes them a desirable pairing for transnational criminal organizations (TCOs) seeking to <mark>increase profits and market control</mark>. The International Labour Organization estimated that human trafficking was a $236 billion-a-year business in 2021. Actual illegal drug trade profits are largely unknown due to the trade’s illicit nature. However, a 2014 report from Global Financial Integrity, a Washington, DC-based think tank focusing on illicit financial flows, corruption, illicit trade, and money laundering, has estimated the global illegal drug market between $426 and $652 billion. Human trafficking and smuggling are complex crimes that affect people differently. While sometimes related, these are separate crimes. Emerging research suggests that disasters, like the border crisis, increase the risk of human trafficking. Numerous factors drive migration, including natural disasters, regional conflicts, poverty, political instability, human rights violations, and fluctuating immigration policies. Although the responsibility for immigration policy and enforcement lies with the federal government, state and local governments shoulder the burden for the stabilization of migrants remaining in the U.S. as they await immigration proceedings. State and local government efforts will be chiefly coordinated by emergency management personnel utilizing tactics similar to those for natural and human-caused disasters. They will assist social services agencies and their non-profit partners to quickly mobilize emergency migrant-serving facilities, programs, and services. Aside from the humanitarian lens, the policing lens has agencies shifting their “safeguarding” focus of respective communities to combat spillover violence associated with the border crisis: According to Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) Director Wray at a March 2024 congressional hearing, “I would say this from an FBI perspective: we are seeing a wide array of very dangerous threats that emanate from the border, and that includes drug trafficking. The FBI alone seized enough fentanyl in the last two years to kill 270 million people.” In March 2024, U.S. Border Patrol chief Jason Owens stated that the extraordinary flow of people into the U.S. is mainly driven by cartels, and they were setting “the rules of engagement” at the southern border. Aside from illicit drug trafficking, Mexican TCOs are aggressively increasing their involvement in corresponding crimes like extortion, human smuggling, human trafficking, arms trafficking, and oil theft, according to the Congressional Research Service. Additionally, the level of <mark>violence</mark> associated with territorial control, trafficking routes, and criminal influence <mark>is increasing</mark>. According to the Global Risk Map, Mexico remains at “high risk,” crediting the enduring narco-war that threatens the balance of power and fuels upticks in violence. Over the past few decades, Mexican TCOs have fragmented the seven original Mexican cartels into twelve. In addition to large TCOs, approximately 200-400 smaller criminal organizations operate in Mexico. The fragmentation and evolution of criminal enterprises did not happen instantaneously. In 2018, instead of directly combating TCOs, Mexican President Andrés Manuel López Obrador adopted a policy addressing social conditions within the country, allowing these organizations to flourish. Unlike Colombia in the 1980s and 1990s, where dismantling the Medellin and Cali cartels led to less violence overall, dismantling the Mexican TCOs has fragmented criminal groups and encouraged widespread violence, largely due to Mexico’s governmental corruption and lack of enforcement. TCOs continue to be the greatest ongoing drug threat to the U.S., according to the DEA. Mexican TCOs control a majority of the U.S. illicit drug market, have well-established transportation routes, possess advanced technological and communication capabilities, and have strong affiliations with criminal groups inside the U.S. Their routine participation and involvement with affiliated crimes such as human smuggling, human trafficking, arms trafficking, public corruption, and violence pose a serious threat to homeland security and further jeopardize public safety. The TCOs’ production and trafficking of fentanyl to the U.S. has fueled a continuing opioid crisis. The DEA reported that fentanyl was responsible for the deaths of 74,225 Americans in 2022 and almost 38,000 Americans in the first six months of 2023, making it the deadliest U.S. drug threat. TCOs currently control transportation routes inside Mexico, smuggling corridors, and distribution hubs in every U.S. state, as well as synthetic drug manufacturing facilities. Encrypted messaging applications and social media platforms facilitate these drug sales, making detection more challenging for law enforcement. TCOs can advertise drugs and cross-border human-smuggling services over social media platforms, communicate with co-conspirators and drug purchasers over encrypted applications, and receive payment via cash apps and cryptocurrency platforms on a single electronic mobile device. Transnational human trafficking The overwhelming number of undocumented, illegal migrants entering the U.S. is alarming. In 2023, Customs Border Patrol reported over 2.4 million encounters at the Southwest border and 3.2 million encounters nationwide. TCO violence and corruption, coupled with inadequate U.S. immigration policies and enforcement, have increased the number of asylum seekers entering the country. According to a 2022 report by the United Nations’ International Organization for Migration, the Mexico to U.S. corridor is the largest in the world, with nearly 11 million people fleeing violence, extortion, armed clashes, or organized crime groups. Conflicts and competition for control between TCOs in violent states and battlegrounds – expanding into formerly peaceful areas – fuel a large exodus of people. Smugglers are capitalizing on opportunities for diversification and exploitation. Recruiting And transport Common human trafficking recruitment methods include familial relationships, smuggling, and migration, specifically <mark>targeting migrants </mark>who originate in or pass through Mexico en route to the U.S. Migrants often have preexisting vulnerabilities and are <mark>unlikely to report</mark> trafficking <mark>crimes</mark> to law enforcement for reasons including the lack of self-identification, TCO retribution, deportation, and arrest. TCOs control the 2,000-mile U.S. Mexico-U.S. border which has escalated violent competition between groups over illicit markets and trafficking routes. The smugglers, often known as “foot guides,” “coyotes,” or “polleros,” recruit victims with promises of safe passage. Sometimes, victims are kidnapped en route to their destination and controlled with debt bondage, physical violence, and threats to family members. Smuggling endeavors are highly sophisticated. In 2021, TCOs earned an estimated $13 billion from human smuggling. Migrants agree to pay TCOs to travel from Southern or Central America to the U.S. Before the journey occurs, TCOs do the equivalent of a background check to learn about the migrants’ family members and friends and where they live. The family members or friends become “collateral” for the migrants. When the journey begins, TCOs require migrants to wear color-coded and serial-numbered wristbands that reflect how much money is outstanding and who they may have to work for once inside the U.S. During the trip, TCOs often increase the price or “piso” (tax), making it difficult for migrants to back out of deals or negotiate lower fees. Most migrants have some money, but not always enough to pay for the entire journey, with smuggling rates ranging from $3,000 to $60,000. At the border, TCOs remove the migrants’ wristbands and collect any remaining piso and smuggling fees. Migrants refusing to pay risk having someone kill their family members or friends. Any migrant who owes money must go to a work location in the U.S., where the migrant stays enslaved in debt bondage to the trafficker until the full amount is paid. Forced labor in the U.S. Once in the U.S., large percentages of undocumented illegal migrants smuggled into the U.S. from TCOs fall prey to labor trafficking, which expands beyond laboring on a farm, cleaning hotels, doing construction, or working in factories for little or no wages. According to research conducted by one of the nation’s leading anti-trafficking organizations, Polaris, the National Human Trafficking Hotline identified that, between 2015-2018, 50% of persons trafficked for labor were from Latin America. Agriculture has the distinction of being the U.S. industry with the greatest number of reported immigrant victims of human trafficking. Latin American migrants are not the only victims of the gateway from smuggling to trafficking. Labor trafficking also occurs at legal and illegal marijuana farms, which may be operated by TCOs or Asian Criminal Organizations with ties to the Chinese Communist Party, who also function as money launderers. According to the U.S. Customs and Border Patrol, encounters with Chinese nationals have significantly increased over the past four years: 450 in FY2021, 2,176 in FY2022, 24,314 in FY2023, and 27,700 in the first 7 months of FY2024. Expanding beyond drug trafficking Capitalizing on their existing drug routes, TCOs have expanded and diversified into human smuggling and, subsequently, human trafficking. Targeting migrants <mark>desperate to</mark> enter the U.S. and <mark>escape violence and</mark> economic <mark>poverty</mark> in their own countries, TCOs offer struggling migrants paid unauthorized transportation. Many migrants cannot pay the transportation cost <mark>and become</mark> the TCOs’ <mark>indentured servants</mark>. Repayment for this service takes many forms, primarily sex trafficking, labor trafficking, drug smuggling, and other associated criminal activity. <mark>Refusal</mark> to comply with TCO demands for trade-based repayment often <mark>means violence</mark> to migrants or their family members who remained in the country. Same routes, more commodities Unlike the Colombian cartels of the 1980s and 1990s, disrupting and dismantling Mexican TCOs have proved difficult because of corruption in the Mexican government and because arresting TCO leaders results in further fragmentation, cartel expansion, and extensive violence in geographic turf battles. TCOs control the areas along the Southwest border and exploit that control and long-established transportation routes. Capitalizing on their already prosperous business model for drug cargo, TCOs diversified into human smuggling using the same routes and procedures to transport human commodities. As an added benefit, desperate migrants rarely have the total funds required for their transportation, so TCOs accommodate them by forcing them into sex work, forced labor, drug trafficking, drug production, and associated criminal activities. <mark>Exploiting migrants provides an endless supply of</mark> TCO personnel and <mark>income</mark> not dependent on the manufacture and transportation of illicit narcotics. The diversification into human smuggling and trafficking is a simple <mark>business</mark> decision for TCOs who <mark>have no regard for human life</mark> and make no distinction between drugs and human commodities. <mark>Profits</mark> effectively <mark>triple with minimal effort</mark> while providing an endless supply of personnel and diversified illicit business opportunities should the narcotics trade become disrupted. With money as a prime motivation for these illegal activities, transnational crime is likely to increase until authorities challenge the high-profit, low-risk paradigm. Enacted or amended legislation is needed to address the immigration crisis, as well as training for law enforcement to recognize human trafficking when encountering migrants.

The acceptance of China into the World Trade Organization (WTO) in 2001 marked the beginning of the expansion of its trade with the region. Later, investment and operations by Chinese policy banks and companies in the region began to take off, providing China with a growing stake in the domestic affairs of the region. Although US trade and OFDI flows to the region still tower over those between China and the region, as pointed out by Wise and Ching (2017), China has clearly become a major trading partner, investor, and lender in the region. Recent trends in trade between China and Latin America have attracted considerable scholarly attention (Li 2007; Jenkins 2012). Data from United Nations Comtrade Database (2019) reveal that the volume of bilateral trade between China and 18 Latin American countries has increased substantially over the past decades. The Latin American side exported only about $2.5 billion to China in 1995, but their exports amounted to about $19 billion in 2005 and $94 billion in 2017. Its imports from China were approximately $2.5 billion in 1995, but they recorded $34 billion in 2005 and $169 billion in 2017. The five economies of the region with the largest volume of bilateral trade with China in 2017 were Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Mexico, and Peru. The Chinese share in total exports and imports of Latin American countries has also increased considerably. In 1995, Chinese share in total exports and imports of 18 Latin American countries was only about 1 percent. In 2017, exports to China captured 10 percent share in the region’s total exports and 18 percent in its total imports. In the same year, exports to the United States accounted for 45 percent of the region’s total exports and imports from the United States occupied 32 percent of its total imports. At the country level, the share of China in total exports was 27 percent for Chile, 26 percent for Peru, 21 percent for Brazil, 18 percent for Uruguay, and 7 percent for Argentina in 2017. However, China has not become a major export destination for Central American countries. Imports from China grew at a faster rate than exports to China for the majority of the countries in the region. In 2017, Chinese share in total imports exceeded 20 percent in a number of countries in South America and 10 percent in most of Central American countries. Although trade between the two sides took off in the early 2000s, investments and loans to the region began to increase dramatically in the   later part of the decade. There are two main forms of capital outflows: outward foreign direct investment (OFDI) and lending by Chinese policy banks. Chinese government agencies and companies have invested in various projects in the region, ranging from natural resources production and extraction to road construction and hydro-electrical projects. Table 1 summarizes data on Chinese OFDI directed to the region from 2000 to 2018. Between 2000 and 2005, Chinese OFDI in Latin America recorded only about $4.4 billion, but reached $33 billion from 2006 to 2010 and $45 billion between 2015 and 2018. The top four recipient countries of Chinese OFDI since 2000 are Brazil, Peru, Argentina, and Chile. Nevertheless, the region’s OFDI inflows are still dominated by the United States. According to Council on Foreign Relations (2015), the United States accounted for 20 percent of the region’s OFDI inflows in 2014, compared with China’s 6 percent share in the same year.  As can be seen in Table 2, China has acted as a major provider of loans to Latin American countries since Chinese lending to the region took off in the mid-2000s. Data from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) reveal that the amount of Chinese loans to the region has continuously increased. Only nine countries in the region received Chinese loans from 2005 to 2018. One half of total Chinese lending to the region was directed to Venezuela, amounting to more than $67 billion. Brazil was ranked second with 21 percent. Ecuador and Argentina received 14 percent and 12 percent each.  Chinese policy banks have emerged as an important alternative to traditional lenders for the countries that have had difficulties in borrowing in global capital markets due to high risk (Dollar 2017). This means that they have provided financing to a significantly different set of countries in the region from the conventional sources of capital such as the World Bank, IDB, and Western banks (Urdinez et al. 2016). According to Kaplan (2016), Chinese lenders are characterized by risk tolerance, avoiding harsh policy conditions or credit being contingent on a country’s macroeconomic performance. However, it is pointed out that they often require oil sales agreements or equipment purchases from the borrowing governments. Several Latin American countries have been leveraging their future oil assets and profitability to attract Chinese lending. Policy Loans as an Instrument of Soft Balancing Keohane (1984) links control over capital to hegemony—in politics, money can buy friendship and loyalty. According to this view, the emergence   of China as a major lender in Latin America should pose a threat to US hegemony in the region. However, are these recent dynamics a reflection of China’s soft balancing against the regional hegemon? It is pivotal to ask whether its lending behavior across the region is motivated by its geopolitical goals or a consequence of independent economic motives for profit. We argue that China’s outbound loans feature geopolitical reasons rather than profit maximization goals. China’s outbound loans, mainly issued through its policy banks, China Export-Import Bank and China Development Bank, are almost exclusively official lending, signifying that the Chinese state plays a critical role in its policy banks’ lending decisions (Zhang and Heyang 2019). Indeed, these policy banks operate under the direct ownership and supervision of the Ministry of Finance and the Chinese State Council (Horn et al. 2019). The government ownership of policy banks allows Beijing to strategically employ state-driven lending to enter new markets, secure natural resources, and further China’s global ambitions. Chinese policy loans have always had a strategic element. In our empirical analysis covering 18 countries in the region from 2005 to 2018, we specifically estimate the effects of maintaining a strategic partnership with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions. We also inquire how a host Latin American country’s ties with the United States affect the likelihood of Chinese policy loans. One may point out that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are driven not by Beijing’s soft balancing strategy but rather by demands from Latin American governments. It could be that the countries that are marginalized by the United States or non-beneficiaries of the traditional Western lending agencies are pursuing a strategy of diversification and turning to China as an alternative source of financing (Urdinez et al. 2016). This argument gives agency to Latin American countries and points to the ideological affinities between China and a number of left-wing Latin American governments in the recent past (Kaplan 2019). In light of these considerations, we introduce two “control” dependent variables, an empirical strategy similar to the one employed in Urdinez et al. (2016), to support our hypothesis that Chinese lending decisions are supply-driven—i.e., strategically-driven—rather than demand-driven. We compare the determinants of Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions with those of two other forms of China’s capital exports, OFDI by Chinese state-owned enterprises (SOEs) and private-owned enterprises (POEs). Geopolitical underpinnings are expected to be most obvious in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks.   Investment decisions by SOEs are likely to be less conditioned by geopolitical interests of the Chinese government, as there is less room for state involvement in the decision making process. Political bias of this kind is expected to be much weaker when OFDI decisions are made by POEs, in which private agents play the main role in searching for yield. If we observe that the determinants of lending decisions and OFDI decisions by SOEs and POEs are significantly different from one another, it can be said that Beijing has a significant degree of agency in its engagement with Latin American countries—otherwise, the countries in the region would be almost equally receptive to the three forms of Chinese capital exports. We can then argue that Chinese policy loans are primarily a product of an underlying soft balancing strategy of the Chinese government, rather than an outcome of the will of a number of Latin American countries to approach Beijing for loans. It would be naive to say that all the outbound investment decisions are made at the behest of Beijing. However, it would be equally wrong to assume that the Chinese government does not exercise control over SOEs operating in industries and areas of strategic importance when they make OFDI decisions. Some point out that the extent to which the Chinese state drives SOEs’ decisions is overstated (Cogman, Gao, and Leung 2017). Indeed, a careful scrutiny of China’s SOEs reveals that their linkages with the state are becoming thinner and outbound investment decisions by SOEs, especially those involving acquisition deals, are increasingly driven by commercial logic rather than dictates of the state (Collier 2018). However, following a developmental state path, the Chinese government still subordinates its SOEs through several regulatory institutions such as the State Council, Ministry of Commerce, People’s Bank of China, State Administration of Foreign Exchange, and State Assets Supervision and Administration Commission, while relinquishing control of less important enterprises (Mattlin 2009; Luo, Xue, and Han 2010; Chen 2018). On the other hand, Chinese POEs are significantly less stifled by the institutional environment for their OFDI compared with SOEs, especially in terms of Chinese government influence that they have to deal with. For example, all SOEs are required to apply for OFDI approval from the Ministry of Commerce, whereas OFDI projects by POEs only require approval by provincial-level Foreign Economic Relation and Trade (Luo, Xue, and Han 2010). Studies on the determinants of Chinese lending across Latin America are rare. Urdinez et al. (2016) find that there is an inverse relationship between the amount of Chinese policy bank loans and US hegemonic influence in host countries in the region. However, their analysis is   centered on the determinants of the amount of lending, which is problematic as Chinese policy banks lend only to a selected set of countries in the region. Those who do not borrow at all from China can produce biased coefficients. Kaplan (2019) tests for supply and demand-side determinants of Chinese policy loans to Latin American governments. Similar to our work, a binary dependent variable is employed to observe if a Latin American government has a Chinese bilateral loan outstanding as a share of its external debt stock in a given year. However, the analysis is focused on the effect of host governments’ partisanship on the likelihood of Chinese policy loans and ultimately the influence of Chinese lending on fiscal policy choices of Latin American governments. We introduce three dependent variables in our empirical analysis. All of them are binary variables which are equal to 1 if a host Latin American country receives any lending from Chinese policy banks, OFDI by Chinese SOEs, and OFDI by Chinese POEs, respectively, and 0 otherwise. The time period we cover is from 2005 to 2018 for the regressions on lending decisions and 2000 to 2018 for the regressions on OFDI decisions. Data come from China-Latin America Finance Database (2019) and Red ALC-China (2019). In order to isolate the political determinants that are of interest to us from the economic ones, we introduce a number of control variables that can account for Chinese policy banks’ choices to issue loans or SOEs and POEs’ decisions to invest in a Latin American country. GDP, per capita GDP, inflation rate, external public debt as a percentage of GDP, rule of law, and US interest are included to test if Chinese loans and OFDI seek for bigger markets or better business environment, or are influenced by global financial conditions. One can argue that the nature of Chinese economic engagement in the region is resource-seeking. Host countries’ natural resource rents as a percentage of GDP are included to control for wealth in natural resources. Data for these variables come from World Development Indicators (2019), Worldwide Governance Indicators (2019), and United Nations Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (2019). As for our explanatory variables, two binary variables are included to estimate the effects of having a strategic partnership status with China and diplomatic relations with Taiwan on Chinese lending and OFDI decisions. We also include a set of variables to test whether they are sensitive to US influence over host Latin countries. A dummy variable to observe the presence of a free trade agreement (FTA) with the United States, and two variables that capture the share of the United States in their total exports and imports are introduced. Trade data come from United Nations Comtrade Database (2019).  Our probit regression results are presented in Table 3. Regressions (1), (2), and (3) suggest that Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions are positively associated with a host Latin American country’s market size. However, they seem tolerant of its business environment or stability, in contrast to traditional Western lending institutions. A rise in the US interest rate makes it more difficult for Latin American governments to borrow from Chinese policy banks, suggesting that global financial conditions do influence their lending decisions. With regard to our explanatory variables, although China rewards the countries in the region with a strategic partnership status, it piles pressure on those that recognize Taiwan, as it does in Africa (Dreher et al. 2018). The effect of the One China Policy on the likelihood of policy loans is not estimated in regressions (1), (2), and (3), as the Taiwan variable is perfectly collinear with the dependent variable. In all the cases in which Chinese lending was issued, the host country did not have formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan. Furthermore, Chinese policy banks tend to avoid those maintaining close economic ties with the United States when they make a lending decision. The three variables that measure US economic influence over host countries are all negatively related to the dependent variable. These results confirm that the Chinese government employs loans through its policy banks as a robust instrument of soft balancing against the United States in the region. Compared to Chinese policy banks’ lending decisions, Chinese SOEs are more market sensitive and less conditioned by the US posturing in its backyard. Regressions (4), (5), and (6) show that Chinese SOEs are more attracted to a Latin American country with a bigger market size and lower indebtedness. The coefficient on US interest rate is found to be negative. In the geopolitical realm, a strategic partnership status with China and the stance on the One China Policy are tied to the likelihood of OFDI by Chinese SOEs, as it was the case for lending decisions by Chinese policy banks. Among the three variables that measure US economic influence within host countries, only one variable that measures US share in imports is statistically significant. Regressions (7), (8), and (9) show that OFDI decisions by Chinese POEs are sensitive to market conditions and resource-seeking. What is markedly different about Chinese POEs is that they are almost independent of Beijing’s geopolitical considerations across the region, being sensitive only to a host Latin American country’s strategic partnership status, but not to its alignment with the One China Policy nor US influence within it. The presence of geopolitical underpinnings in lending decisions by Chinese policy banks and OFDI decisions by Chinese SOEs to a lesser extent, and their relative absence in OFDI decisions by Chinese POEs    suggest that China’s lending behavior in the region is shaped by strategic planning designed to link its economic endowments to national foreign policy objectives, which is, soft-balancing against the United States in the United States’ backyard. Institution-Building Efforts as an Instrument of Soft Balancing The growth of Sino-Latin American trade and Chinese investments and lending to the region has been complemented by the improving diplomatic relations between the two sides. In 1990, just five years after Premier Zhao Ziyang embarked on the first high-level visit to Latin America, President Yang Shangkun paid the first visit as the head of the state. China declared that its relations with Latin America should be based upon the strategy of non-interference. In the 1990s, China’s Going Out strategy, championed by President Jiang Zemin, identified the region as a target for strategic partnership. The beginning of the twenty-first century saw cooperation between China and Latin America deepen rapidly. President Jiang Zemin’s tour to the region in 2001 followed by that of President Hu Jintao in 2005, and subsequent exchange by senior Chinese and regional officials and business leaders, drew the two sides markedly closer to each other. Moreover, in 2004, China earned observer status in the OAS. The China-Chile FTA entered into force in 2006, China-Peru FTA in 2010, and China-Costa Rica FTA in 2011. In its first ever policy paper on the region in 2008,3  the Chinese government officially declared that it would adopt a more strategic approach to Latin America. The paper emphasized support for new regional institutions, including CELAC, which have the potential to counterbalance the US-led organizations, such as the OAS and IDB. Since then, China has demonstrated robust institution-building efforts, and it appears that such efforts are paying off. China has successfully immersed itself in a web of regional institutions and relationships, both bilateral and multilateral, through which it now pursues its strategic goals, such as initiating military engagements and sales, extending the reach of the BRI, garnering regional support for the One China Policy, internationalizing its currency, and securing investments in strategically important waterways. China has continued to strengthen bilateral strategic relationships in the region. China has established comprehensive strategic partnerships—the third highest level of bilateral ties in its diplomatic lexicon—  with Brazil (2012), Mexico (2013), Peru (2013), Venezuela (2014), Argentina (2014), Chile (2016), and Ecuador (2016) and strategic partnerships—the fourth level of bilateral ties after comprehensive strategic partnerships—with Costa Rica (2015), Uruguay (2016), and Bolivia (2018). The characterization of these partnerships mean more than diplomatic talking points. Their stated purpose is to coordinate more closely on regional and international affairs, even including military ones. In its 2015 National Defense Strategy White Paper, the Chinese government reaffirmed its interest in maintaining friendly military ties with Latin American counterparts (State Council Information Office of the People’s Republic of China 2015). After Xi Jinping took office in 2013, China’s diplomatic offensive to the region has been more active than ever. In 2014, Xi, during his trip to Brazil for the BRICs Summit, proposed to build a new “1+3+6” cooperation framework: “1” refers to the China-Latin American Countries and Caribbean States Cooperation Plan (2015–2019), which is expected to serve as a blueprint for economic growth and sustainable development; “3” denotes three engines—trade, investment, and financial cooperation—that will drive practical cooperation; and “6” means six cooperation priorities, such as energy and resources, infrastructure construction, agriculture, manufacturing, scientific and technological innovation, and information technology. China’s participation in CELAC and the establishment of the China-CELAC Forum opened a new chapter for China’s strategic engagement in the region. The first Ministerial Meeting of the China-CELAC Forum was held in 2015. According to Yu (2015), the meeting was a landmark in Sino–Latin American relations, signaling that cooperation between the two sides substantially expanded into the geopolitical and strategic realms. The Beijing Declaration issued by the Forum was a clear demonstration that neither China nor Latin America was satisfied with the US-dominated unipolar system. The second white paper on Latin America and the Caribbean released in 2016 was another forthright statement of China’s strategic approach to the region. While reaffirming its commitments to the “1+3+6” cooperation framework, the Chinese government vowed to explore a “3x3” model for capacity cooperation. First, three passages—logistics, power, and information technology—would be constructed to boost domestic demand in Latin America. Second, three financing channels— funds, loans, and insurance—would be expanded to provide financing to bilateral projects. In support of the implementation of the model, China pledged financing of $30 billion in total. When materialized, the   two projects will ultimately transform the existing Sino-Latin American relations. The “1+3+6” and “3x3” frameworks signaled a subtle yet significant shift in China’s policy focus, from trade to more diversified engagement. While China under the leadership of Xi was embracing Latin American countries in a more active manner, US-Latin American relations deteriorated further since President Donald Trump took office. The growing US disengagement in the region under the Trump administration was marked by such actions as withdrawal from the Trans-Pacific Partnership, reduction of US foreign aid, and hostility toward Mexico and Latino immigrants in general. The Trump administration also rolled back the previous administration’s Cuba policy. The “America First” doctrine has in effect once again created a strategic void in the region, giving China an opportunity to fill the void and promote its interests. In 2017, China renewed its efforts to diplomatically isolate Taiwan in Latin America. Central American countries have constituted the biggest bloc of Taiwan’s few remaining world allies. Panama shifted its diplomatic alignment from Taiwan to China in 2017, followed by the Dominican Republic and El Salvador in 2018. In exchange, China promised investments and loans to these countries. Beijing’s renewed activism in promoting its One China Policy resulted in US concerns. For instance, immediately after El Salvador’s switchover, the White House announced that it would reevaluate its diplomatic relationship with El Salvador, warning that the “short-term economic benefits” offered by China would lead to “dependency and domination.” At the same time, the Chinese leadership, taking advantage of bilateral strategic partnerships and its position in the CELAC, has endeavored to extend the BRI to the region with noticeable success. Beijing issued a formal invitation to the BRI at the Second Ministerial Meeting of China-CELAC Forum in 2018. It was an effort to gain CELAC’s endorsement on the “natural extension” of the BRI, Xi’s signature foreign policy objective. Panama was the first country in the region to sign a cooperation agreement under the BRI banner, and many others including Bolivia, Chile, Costa Rica, Cuba, Dominican Republic, Ecuador, El Salvador, Panama, Peru, Uruguay, Venezuela, and several other Caribbean countries have followed suit, with the purpose of gaining access to infrastructure financing. However, one should note that some of the largest economies in the region such as Argentina, Brazil, Colombia, and Mexico have not yet signed any BRI agreements. This shows that Beijing’s charm offensive in the region has its limits.  Another recent piece of evidence of China’s soft balancing against American power in the region is the expansion of its currency swap program with Argentina. In 2018, China and Argentina agreed to nearly double their existing currency swap deal, bringing the total to $18.7 billion. They first agreed to a currency swap program to boost Argentina’s falling reserves in 2009. The recession-struck country once again turned to China as a source of financing and used some of the funds to cover its emergency deficits. Though its effect is marginal, the currency swap echoes China’s intensifying efforts to gradually promote the renminbi into a global currency. In parallel, the extension of the BRI to Latin America is expected to bring about the more widespread use of the renminbi. While China’s soft balancing strategy relies on a low-key approach for the purpose of avoiding direct confrontation with the United States, China’s increasing presence in the areas where America’s vital interests are at stake is likely to trigger strong pushback from the United States. Panama is a case in point. After establishing formal ties with China in 2017, Panama officially pledged its support for the BRI. In less than two years, the two states have signed nearly 20 bilateral agreements in trade and infrastructure. Chinese state-owned enterprises represented by China Ocean Shipping Companies Group are actively involved in investments around the Panama Canal. In response, US Secretary of State Mike Pompeo, while visiting Panama in October 2018, warned Panama against accepting the BRI loans (Churchill 2018). Furthermore, the US Southern Command’s 2019 posture statement associated China’s infrastructure investments on the Panama Canal with US security interests. The Panama Canal could once again turn into a flashpoint for great-power conflict. Wary of China’s increasing ambition, Washington’s view on US-China relations in the Western Hemisphere seems to be gradually moving from win-win to zero-sum. The trilateral relationship in the region has been further complicated by the deepening US-China tension. The ongoing trade conflict between the two major powers, which began in 2018, froze their bilateral relations. Both sides have been setting titfor-tat tariffs and other trade barriers on each other. Although they have recently agreed to discuss the rolling back of tariffs in phases, it seems uncertain when this all-out trade war will come to an end. In addition, along with the increasing trade deficit with China, concerns regarding the dramatic increase in Chinese OFDI have been growing in the United States. The 2017 US National Security Strategy warned that the “national innovation base” should be protected from Beijing’s attempts to    vacuum up core US companies and technologies. In response, the Foreign Investment Risk Review Modernization Act was legislated in 2018. It is widely believed that this effort was primarily aimed at addressing concerns over Chinese OFDI in the United States. Despite the growing suspicion of the United States toward the expanded role played by China in Latin America and the deteriorating US-China relations, Beijing does not seem to have practiced any strategy of hard balancing in the region. The escalating US containment toward China both within and outside the region has not yet caused Beijing to look to more assertive or outright behavior in the region, strengthening our arguments about China’s intentions of soft balancing against the regional hegemon.  Conclusion Pursuing a multifaceted approach as a major trading partner, investor, lender, and diplomatic partner, China has established a remarkable presence in Latin America over a relatively short period of time. We argue that Beijing’s intensifying economic and political clout in the region is an outcome of its soft balancing strategy, designed to advance its geopolitical goal of increasing its influence in America’s backyard without directly confronting the regional hegemon. China has used its financial power—loans by state-owned policy banks—as a tool to draw the countries with strategic value to Beijing into its political orbit. In that regard, it can be argued China’s policy banks are the key institutions underpinning China’s soft balancing strategy in the region. Chinese soft balancing is also evident in its continuing activism in strengthening diplomatic arrangements with the countries in the region and engaging in the CELAC and the China-CELAC Forum. China’s expanding economic and political influence in the Western Hemisphere has interrelated implications for the intensifying US-China strategic competition. China’s growing leverage in the region will serve to shift the regional balance of power in favor of China if the Chinese economy continues to grow and Beijing remains committed to the region. Beijing’s activism in regional institutions will be useful in expanding its role <mark>as a major trading partner</mark>, investor and lender in the region, and translating its economic clout into geopolitical influence. China’s growing role in the region is not likely to immediately transform into more assertive behavior. Yet its institutional arrangements and economic interdependence between the two sides will allow <mark>China</mark> to gradually <mark>extract concessions from</mark> regional <mark>powers, chipping away at</mark> American <mark>dominance</mark> in the region. In the short run, China’s rallying of like-minded countries, or countries adversarial to the United States, can serve as a tool to advance narrow strategic interests such as the One China Policy and a hedge against the excesses of US hegemony. In the long run, it may lead to the emergence of a counterbalancing coalition against the US-led unilateralism. Ultimately, if China sustains the momentum, China’s growing influence in Latin America can seriously <mark>impair</mark> the <mark>ability of the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>to exercise</mark> effective <mark>leadership</mark>, not only in the Western Hemisphere but also in other areas of strategic interest. For one thing, if China consolidates its position as an indispensable partner to the region, more countries will begin to look to Beijing not only as an economic but also as a political alternative that can replace the hegemonic position of the United States. For another, if America’s backyard turns into China’s playground, the United States will then be, as realists argue, less free to exert its power outside the Western Hemisphere.

As China’s power has grown in recent years, so, too, has the risk of war with the United States. Under President Xi Jinping, China has increased its political and economic pressure on Taiwan and built military installations on coral reefs in the South China Sea, fueling Washington’s fears that Chinese expansionism will threaten U.S. allies and influence in the region. U.S. destroyers have transited the Taiwan Strait, to loud protests from Beijing. American policymakers have wondered aloud whether they should send an aircraft carrier through the strait as well. Chinese fighter jets have intercepted U.S. aircraft in the skies above the South China Sea. Meanwhile, U.S. President Donald Trump has brought long-simmering economic disputes to a rolling boil. A war between the two countries remains unlikely, but the prospect of a military confrontation—resulting, for example, from a Chinese campaign against Taiwan—no longer seems as implausible as it once did. And the <mark>odds of</mark> such a <mark>confrontation going nuclear are higher than</mark> most policymakers and <mark>analysts think</mark>. Members of China’s strategic com­munity tend to dismiss such concerns. Likewise, U.S. studies of a potential war with China often exclude nuclear weapons from the analysis entirely, treating them as basically irrelevant to the course of a conflict. Asked about the issue in 2015, Dennis Blair, the former commander of U.S. forces in the Indo-Pacific, estimated the likelihood of a U.S.-Chinese nuclear crisis as “somewhere between nil and zero.” This assurance is misguided. If deployed against China, the Pentagon’s preferred style of conventional warfare would be a potential recipe for nuclear escalation. Since the end of the Cold War, the United States’ signature approach to war has been simple: punch deep into enemy territory in order to rapidly knock out the opponent’s key military assets at minimal cost. But the Pentagon developed this formula in wars against Afghanistan, Iraq, Libya, and Serbia, none of which was a nuclear power. <mark>China</mark>, by contrast, not only <mark>has nuclear weapons</mark>; it has also <mark>intermingled</mark> them <mark>with</mark> its <mark>conventional</mark> military forces, making it difficult to attack one without attacking the other. This means that <mark>a </mark>major U.S. military <mark>campaign</mark> targeting China’s conventional forces <mark>would</mark> likely also <mark>threaten its nuclear arsenal</mark>. Faced with such a threat, Chinese <mark>leaders</mark> could decide to <mark>use</mark> their nuclear <mark>weapons while they were</mark> still <mark>able</mark> to. As U.S. and Chinese leaders navigate a relationship fraught with mutual suspicion, they must come to grips with the fact that a conventional war could skid into a nuclear confrontation. Although this risk is not high in absolute terms, its consequences for the region and the world would be devastating. As long as the United States and China continue to pursue their current grand strategies, the risk is likely to endure. This means that leaders on both sides should dispense with the illusion that they can easily fight a limited war. They should focus instead on managing or resolving the political, economic, and military tensions that might lead to a conflict in the first place.
