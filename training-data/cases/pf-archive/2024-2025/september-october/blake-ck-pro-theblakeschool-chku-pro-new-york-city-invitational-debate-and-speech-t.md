In recent years, the United States has witnessed a surge in the number of migrants crossing the
country’s southern border, exposing the Democrats’ long-standing frailty on the issue, and heightening
the significance of immigration issues in the upcoming Presidential Election. Between October 2022 and September 2023 alone,
Customs and Border Protection officers encountered 2.47 million migrants crossing the southern border with Mexico, a huge increase from
the pre-pandemic annual average of 500,000. Moreover, of these encounters, fewer than a quarter were refused entry to the United States, a
reversal of the trend over the previous nine years where more than half of the 8.7 million migrants apprehended at the southern border
were not admitted to the United States. Although the number of encounters between border patrol agents and migrants has dropped
sharply in recent months, the official figure of 104,116 migrant encounters in July 2024 is still over 20,000 more than the number of
encounters recorded in July 2019 (81,777), the last comparable year before the pandemic. With immigration one of the dominant issues of
the presidential campaign, we at Redfield and Wilton Strategies recently asked American voters their views on immigration, the Biden
Administration’s border policies, and how concerns about immigration will impact their vote in November. For starters, 11% of voters
rate immigration as the single issue that will be most important in determining their vote, placing it behind
the economy (38%) and abortion (16%) as the third most commonly cited issue. Older voters are more likely than younger voters to say
immigration is an issue that will determine their vote in November. 17% of those aged 65 or older select immigration as the issue most
important in determining their vote (making it the second most commonly selected issue, behind the economy) compared to just 9% of 18-24
year olds, among whom it is the fourth most commonly selected issue behind the economy, abortion, and healthcare. Specifically, a
majority of voters (53%) rate illegal immigration as ‘extremely important’ in determining their vote,
with a further 22% describing the issue as ‘moderately important’. 16% rate it as ‘somewhat important’ in determining how they will vote, while
only 6% describe the issue as ‘not at all important’ to determining their vote. 90% of likely Trump voters rate illegal

two-thirds (66%) of likely Harris voters also rate illegal immigration as ‘extremely’ or ‘moderately’ important in determining their vote. As it

What is Article 5 of the NATO Treaty? Article 5 really is the heart and soul of the NATO alliance. It is the part of
the treaty that says that if one member is attacked, then all of the other members will treat it as an
attack on them all. In effect, it calls for a collective response once requested by any of the current 30
members of NATO and invoked by the entire alliance. The NATO treaty was signed in April 1949 and Article 5 is central to
it. In the immediate aftermath of World War II, Western European countries sought a way to defend themselves in the event Germany again
arose as a security challenge. By the late 1940s, concerns shifted toward the threat posed by the Soviet Union, which stationed large military
forces across Eastern Europe, staged a coup in Czechoslovakia, and blockaded Berlin. Initially, the United States was skeptical of joining any kind
of post-war alliance in Europe, but Soviet actions convinced American leaders to sign on as a way of maintaining Western Europe as free and

US hegemony over the last seven decades or so has recently been challenged by China’s rise. Since the economic reforms launched in December 1978, China has achieved a remarkable economic development. As a matter of fact, competing with the USA in many areas, China has quickly strengthened its economic, technological and military power and as a consequence has started to threaten US global hegemony. Accordingly, many scholars now often talk about a declining US hegemony and a forthcoming Chinese hegemony (see, for instance, Layne 2018, 2012). Some of them even predict a hegemonic war between the USA and China during the hegemonic
transition period (see, e.g., Liff and Ikenberry, 2014; Mearsheimer, 2014; Friedberg, 2005). Underlying these arguments for an inevitable war between the two superpowers is PTT. PTT originally formulated by Organski (1958) posits that war is likely when the power of the dominant state in the international system (i.e. hegemon) is declining and that a dissatisfied rising challenger substantially reduces the power gap between the hegemon and itself. Unlike
balance of power theory, PTT argues that the war is most likely when there is near power parity between a dominant state and a rising and dissatisfied challenger (Organski and Kugler, 1980, pp. 19-20)[5]. A rising power here is generally dissatisfied with the existing international order and initiates war against a declining hegemon in order to impose orders that are more favorable to itself (Organski 1958, pp. 364-367). Layne (2018, p. 110) put these power
transition dynamics quite succinctly as follows: “Over time, however, the relative power of states changes, and eventually the international order no longer reflects the actual distribution of power between or among the leading Great Powers. When that happens, the legitimacy of the prevailing order is called into question, and it will be challenged by the rising power(s).” And when the balance of power between a dominant state and a rising challenger changes
sufficiently, a new order replaces an old one typically by a hegemonic war (2018, p. 104). Paying close attention to the growing Sino–US competition over hegemony in the twenty-first century, therefore, Shirk (2007, p. 4), China specialist, argues that “History teaches us that rising powers are likely to provoke war.” On the other hand, scholars like Gilpin (1981) contend that the power transition war between great powers is likely to
occur when a hegemonic state whose power is declining due to imperial overstretch[6] views “preventive war as the most attractive means of eliminating the threat posed by challengers” (Ned Lebow and Valentino, 2009, p. 391), although they do acknowledge that there might be some “ways to prolong the period of its power preponderance vis-à-vis the rising challenger, so that the rapidly rising power will not dare
to challenge the hegemonic leadership” (Kim and Gates, 2015, p. 221). In this case, the initiator of war is a declining hegemon, rather than a rising challenger. The declining hegemon who fears a rising challenger’s overtaking its power in the near future sees war as a better option than other options of maintaining its hegemony such as reducing its commitments abroad and appeasing a
rising challenger.
