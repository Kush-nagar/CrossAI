Gebhart 23 [L. Gebhart, 8-2023, Muon tomography for detection of dynamic border tunnels, Science Direct, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S016890022300373X, 8-11-2024] // tristanM
Using <mark>muon tomography</mark> to detect tunnels has been suggested and studied with promising results. This work revisits the idea in the context of border tunnels with applications to drug interdiction and military intrusions. Unlike previous studies, this work considers the detection of border tunnels as they are being built with the goal of detecting tunnels before they cross the border. An updated physics model and numerical analysis is presented and applied to the problem with the following results. For a single borehole the optimal <mark>detector depth is</mark> 40–60 m with a reach of almost <mark>60 m</mark> for drug tunnels. A more useful configuration is an array of detectors spaced evenly along the border. This picket fence arrangement has been suggested but not studied. With detectors spaced at 130 m separation this work shows that drug <mark>tunnels</mark> could be <mark>detected 18 days before crossing the border</mark> with only 10% variation in detection time. Relaxing the uniformity requirement allows the separation to increase to 300 m. For larger, military tunnels the distance between the detectors could be increased further to 450 m. Introduction Muon <mark>tomography</mark> is an imaging technique that uses naturally occurring cosmic ray muons to map out density variations as far underground as a km. Produced by particle interactions in the upper atmosphere, cosmic ray muons arrive at the surface of the earth with a spread of energies up to 1 TeV and beyond [1], [2], [3]. While muons lose energy penetrating the earth [2] their enormous energies allow them to penetrate deeply. The highest energy muons produced in this way <mark>can penetrate</mark> to depths of <mark>several km</mark> [2]. Lower energy muons lose all of their initial kinetic energy in the intervening material, stop and decay. The flux of muons therefore decreases from the surface to several km, falling 6 orders of magnitude over this distance [2]. For reasons of loss of signal, the practical limit of muon tomography is for depths of less than 1 km. For similar reasons, the flux of muons also falls with increasing zenith angle with respect to vertical [2]. The energy loss of muons to matter, called stopping power, is nearly linearly dependent on the density of the matter [4]. An increase in measured muon flux from a particular direction, compared to what was expected, would indicate a lower integral of density times distance in that direction compared with expectations. Multiple detectors allow density variations to be mapped out in 3D. Muon tomography has applications to a wide range of fields, including volcanology, geology, archaeology and engineering [1]. The first well-known application of muon tomography was the exploration of the interior of Chephren’s pyramid in Giza, Egypt by the Nobel Prize winning physicist Luis W. Alvarez in 1970 [1]. While no hidden chambers were detected in Chephren’s pyramid the technique has successfully detected chambers in other pyramids. Many <mark>current tunnel detection</mark> technologies are <mark>limited </mark>in their ability to detect tunnels before the completion of construction, or are heavily constrained in the depths and sizes of tunnels they can detect. With many drug smuggling and trafficking organizations increasingly using tunnels to enter the United States, evidenced by a large increase in the number of tunnels discovered from 1990–2011, the timely detection of cross border tunnels is imperative [5]. Beyond contraband smuggling operations, border tunnels also pose risks to national infrastructure, the transportation of weapons as a result of military activity, and ground collapse [6].
