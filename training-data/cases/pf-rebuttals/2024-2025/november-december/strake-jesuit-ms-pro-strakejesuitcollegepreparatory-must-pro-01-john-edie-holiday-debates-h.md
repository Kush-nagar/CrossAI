NQ. Historical trends prove secessionism is inevitable.
Marche '21 [Stephen; The author of “The Next Civil War”; 12-31-2021; Washington Post; "Perspective," https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/2021/12/31/secession-civil-war-stephen-marche/; accessed: 12-7-2024] tristan
It’s not that secession can’t work. The rest of the world is busily at it all the time. <mark>Separatism is a global</mark> political <mark>trend</mark>. <mark>The number of nations</mark> in the world has <mark>tripled since</mark> 19<mark>45</mark>. And there will soon be more. “Right now, there are about 60 secessionist movements worldwide. Sixty independence movements is a pretty large number by historical standards,” says Ryan Griffiths, a professor at Syracuse University who focuses on the dynamics of secession and the study of sovereignty. “<mark>In the long run, there will be another</mark> secessionist movement in the United States. <mark>It will just happen. No country is permanent</mark>. It will change. It <mark>will break apart</mark> in some way.”
LT. Recognition diffuses secessionism---sets a high bar.
Meservey '21 [Joshua; Senior Fellow @ Hudson Institute, Former Research Fellow @ the Heritage Foundation, Masters in Law & Diplomacy from Tufts University, BA in History from the Templeton Honors College @ Eastern University; 10-19-2021; Heritage Foundation; "The U.S. Should Recognize Somaliland," https://www.heritage.org/global-politics/report/the-us-should-recognize-somaliland, accessed: 11-30-24] tristan
<mark>A common objection</mark> to recognizing Somaliland’s statehood <mark>is</mark> that it would set off a brushfire of <mark>secession</mark> in Africa. <mark>Yet</mark> <mark>Eritrean and South Sudanese independence did not.</mark> <mark>Somaliland is </mark>also <mark>unique</mark> in Africa because it <mark>has</mark> successfully <mark>operated autonomously for 30 years</mark>, <mark>has</mark> a critical mass of the <mark>attributes of statehood</mark>, <mark>was once independent</mark>, and wishes to revert to that status within colonial-era borders, the standard the African Union uses to determine statehood.1
<mark>Recognition</mark> of its independence <mark>would delegitimize other secessionist movements’</mark> claims <mark>by</mark> <mark>establishing a difficult standard for</mark> achieving <mark>sovereignty</mark>.
Independence would free Somaliland from the drag of association with southern Somalia. It would also deliver the justice of honoring the strongly and consistently held aspirations for independence of millions of Somalilanders.
N/!. Movements don’t escalate---empirics.
Kingsbury '17 [Damien; Emeritus Professor @ Deakin University, Supervises PhD candidates; 10-4-2017; Conversation; "Passion and pain: why secessionist movements rarely succeed," https://theconversation.com/passion-and-pain-why-secessionist-movements-rarely-succeed-85097; accessed: 12-15-2024] tristan
<mark>There are</mark> currently well <mark>over 100 secessionist movements</mark>, including four in the <mark>Philippines</mark>, dozens in <mark>India</mark>, around eight in <mark>Myanmar</mark>, and several dozen in <mark>Africa</mark>. Many of these have produced bloodshed and trauma well in excess of possible practical gains. Yet, <mark>despite</mark> their <mark>numbers</mark>, <mark>very few</mark> secessionist movements <mark>are</mark> ultimately <mark>successful</mark>, while the costs for governments imposing a nominal unity can be high for all involved.
With high risks and limited chances of success, secessionist movements are rarely about pragmatism and more about fervour. <mark>Even with popular support</mark>, these <mark>movements rarely have the political or military capacity to</mark> impose their will on the state from which they intend to <mark>secede</mark>.

Isaak sparks conflict absent the AFF
ICG 24 [Staging A 11-13-2024, "Somaliland’s Peaceful Handover Withstands Neighbourhood Strains", No Publication, https://www.crisisgroup.org/africa/horn-africa/somaliland/somalilands-peaceful-handover-withstands-neighbourhood-strains] mac
Secondly, the conflict in Sool between the Somaliland government and Dhulbahante clan militias dented Somaliland’s reputation for internal stability. <mark>Members of Somaliland’s Isaaq clan led the agitation for independence from Somalia following years of insurgency</mark> against the country’s strongman ruler Siad Barre, who held power in Mogadishu from 1969 to 1991. But the majority of Sool’s population are Dhulbahante, a community that belongs to a non-Isaaq family, the Darod. The desire for independence <mark>is</mark> not shared by all communities in the territory claimed by Somaliland, and the outbreak of violence demonstrated this. In early 2023, Dhulbahante elders and elites formed the Sool, Sanaag and Cayn-Khatumo (SSC-K) administration in Sool region, representing the clan, along with Fiqishine and Madiban clans in the area. The administration led the campaign to expel Somaliland forces in August 2023, and has since declared itself part of Somalia rather than Somaliland.
