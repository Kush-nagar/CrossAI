<mark>You’re</mark> being <mark>deceived by a</mark> very powerful <mark>evil demon</mark> right now. This demon has the ability to manipulate <mark>your sensory impressions</mark> such that it will seem to you that things are some way when they are not that way at all. Accordingly, things are actually nothing like P. For example, suppose it seems to you as though you are in a room with a table and chair in it and that you are reading from a computer screen, etc. If (1) is true, then you actually are in a room with a table and chair in it and you are reading from a computer screen, etc. If (2) is true, then you are not in a room with a table and chair in it and you are not reading from a computer screen, etc. If (2) is true, <mark>things are very different from how</mark> they <mark>seem to you</mark> to be.1

Philosophers call (2) a skeptical scenario. In skeptical scenarios, you are radically misled, deceived, or bamboozled by your evidence in such a way that how things seem to you is different from how things actually are. Perhaps the most famous propounder of skeptical scenarios in the history of philosophy is René Descartes (1596-1650) in his Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). In the Meditations, Descartes considers that he might be dreaming or that he might be being deceived by the evil demon from our scenario (2) above. Hollywood has made much of skeptical scenarios in movies like Total Recall, The Matrix, and Inception. So back to our original question: Which of (1) or (2) is best supported or best justified by its seeming to you that P? If you’re being honest with yourself, you’ll conclude that how things seem equally well supports (1) and (2). From your internal, first-personal perspective, either of (1) or (2) could be true given how things seem to you. And if that weren’t bad enough, here comes the kicker: If both (1) and (2) are equally well supported by your evidence, <mark>how can you</mark> ever possibly <mark>know</mark> anything about <mark>the world</mark> <mark>outside</mark> <mark>your</mark> own <mark>skin</mark>? This is the problem of external world skepticism, perhaps the central problem of modern epistemology.
BOLTZMAN BRAIN – if induction works then you don’t exist – its statistically more likely.
Higgs Centre for Theoretical Physics 2024, 2024 "The Boltzmann Brain", https://higgs.ph.ed.ac.uk/outreach/higgshalloween-2021/boltzmann-brain CHO
<mark>You are not real.</mark>
Look around you. What do you see? Your computer? A desk? Maybe you see a loved one: a family member or a close friend. Think about the last time you did something with that person. Maybe you went to the shops, or the cinema, or out for dinner. I’ll bet you had a good time, that it’s a happy <mark>memory.</mark> Now what if I told you that none of it is real. It never happened. You don’t exist. They <mark>don’t exist.</mark> Your desk, your computer? They don’t exist either. You are a <mark>mere statistical fluctuation</mark> in a cold, dark, unending spacetime. You are <mark>a Boltzmann Brain.</mark>
Or, well, <mark>statistically speaking</mark> you are. The idea of a Boltzmann brain emerges from two observations. First, that even extremely <mark>unlikely events happen if</mark> you <mark>wait long enough</mark>, and second, any mechanism which produced the <mark>universe</mark> must be capable of <mark>producing a human </mark>observer since, clearly, we exist.
We’ll come back to the second point in a moment, but for now let’s tackle the first. Imagine you had a box of gas, and all the particles suddenly crammed themselves into one corner of the box. It’s not physically impossible, but it’s very, very unlikely. If we imagine the universe as a similar, if far larger, box of particles, then we can see how the <mark>Big Bang</mark> looks a lot like all the particles in the universe were jammed into the same spot and allowed to expand.
Of course, this is yet <mark>more unlikely to happen</mark> for the universe than in our box of gas, but if you leave the system for long enough—say an infinite period of time—such unlikely fluctuations not only become likely but guaranteed. And, clearly, the <mark>smaller</mark> the <mark>fluctuation,</mark> the <mark>more likely</mark> it is <mark>to occur</mark>. It is far more likely for only half of the gas in the box to find itself in one corner of the box than all the gas, for instance.
Ok, lets add in our second ingredient. Let’s say what we observe as our universe emerged from a low probability fluctuation. What’s the smallest—and therefore <mark>most likely—fluctuation</mark> <mark>which could result in a human </mark>observation of our universe? Well, <mark>it’s a brain</mark>! A brain <mark>whose neurons </mark>are so arranged that it <mark>thinks it</mark>’s a living observer which <mark>can perceives</mark> a vast, endless universe teeming with stars and planets. A brain which thinks it’s sitting at a desk reading an article on a computer screen.
After all, what is our human experience but a collection of neurons connected in exactly the correct way? We normally think of those connections having been generated by years of life experience and eons of evolution. Professor Graeme Ackland, of the University of Edinburgh and Researcher at the Higgs Centre, gives some insight into the thermodynamic origins of the brain as we know it: “The brain evolved over time as a way of increasing the entropy production of living things. There's no problem with that in thermodynamics.”
For the Boltzmann brain, however, none of this is the case. It is a quirk of probability, a <mark>remarkable collection of particles</mark> which exists for a <mark>fraction of a nanosecond</mark> <mark>before returning to the void of an infinite universe.</mark>

The <mark>paradox of induction is the problem that</mark> in <mark>all scientific reasoning</mark> we form conclusions, called laws, that <mark>are of</mark> a general nature; however, the <mark>evidence</mark> we have for those laws is <mark>based upon particular experiences</mark>. For example, we form the conclusion that all <mark>rays of light will be bend</mark> as the pass <mark>from air into glass</mark>, <mark>but</mark> <mark>we</mark> have <mark>only</mark> ever <mark>observed a finite </mark>number of <mark>instances</mark> of this law. On further reflection we see that there is no necessary connection between something happening on one occasion and the same thing happening in like circumstances on another occasion. We are not directly acquainted with the “power” behind events that ensures the uniformity of nature throughout space and time. Another illustration of this might concern the uniformity of space. Imagine that a space mission is about to be sent to the nearest star, Alpha Centuri. People might be queuing up to volunteer to be the first people to witness life on a distant planet. On the other hand, there might be anxious reluctant passengers, desperate not to be dragged on the fool-hardy mission. Why? Because there is no guarantee that the laws of nature operate in the same way in outer space as they do in our solar system. It is entirely conceivable that once the space ship passes beyond the perimeter of our solar system, that entirely different laws of physics will apply, and the space ship could be destroyed by chaotic forces that cannot be anticipated. We have no way at present of being sure that universe is uniform. We have only sampled physical nature in our own limited portion of the universe. We might regard the fear of the passengers as outlandish, but it is not an irrational fear. <mark>Just because things</mark> have <mark>happen</mark>ed <mark>at one point of space</mark> and at a given time in a certain way <mark>is no guarantee</mark> that <mark>they always will happen that way</mark>. This, then, is the paradox. Every day <mark>we reason from particular instances to generalities</mark>, and such inference is essential to our way of life; but there is no guarantee that such an inference is valid, and, indeed, very often such <mark>inferences prove</mark> to be <mark>fallacious</mark> — as in the case of the chicken that reasoned that its master would always feed it just because its master always has! A schematic representation of the inductive inference is as follows. The general law encompasses a potentially infinite number of instances that <mark>no amount of observation could possibly affirm</mark>. The problem is usually expressed as a problem of inference from past to future, but strictly this is only an instance of the problem; <mark>unobserved past events</mark> <mark>are</mark> also <mark>subject to the paradox</mark> of induction — we can <mark>never</mark> be <mark>sure that any</mark> general <mark>law</mark> has <mark>applied uniformly</mark> even <mark>in the past</mark>. No general law can ever be certain. Yet <mark>inductive reasoning is</mark> (in the very broadest sense) <mark>the basis of science</mark>, which constructs laws governing all events. [We have to put this qualifying remark in because science in practice hardly ever employs direct inductive arguments. It proceeds by forming hypotheses and testing these. Nonetheless, the evidence for any scientific theory is based on particular experimental observation, so in the broadest sense science employs induction from particular to general laws. The question of how science actually operates is considered under the heading of the methodology of science. Philosophers of science generally agree that the actual practice of science does not follow an inductive pattern. Yet, the paradox of induction remains. Even so, this point is complicated by the fact that eminent philosophers, such as Karl Popper, have maintained that they have solved the paradox of induction by demonstrating that science does not employ induction. INFINITE UNIVERSE – that thumps “probability” and thumps utility.
Reilly 23 James Reilly, PhD student in philosophy, University of Notre Dame, 6-2-2023, "Ethics and Value in an Infinite Universe", De Vera Doctrina, https://deveradoctrina.substack.com/p/ethics-and-value-in-an-infinite-universe CHO
Many people think that standard forms of consequentialism face serious objections based on the possibility of an infinite universe. The basic idea is this: a fairly standard form of consequentialism says that actions are right or wrong just insofar as they positively or negatively affect the aggregate amount of goodness in the world. But if the universe is infinite, then plausibly it contains infinite positive and negative utility, and hence nothing we do can ever affect the aggregate amount of goodness in the world. This problem is worsened by the fact that <mark>if the universe is infinite</mark>, then <mark>each of our actions potentially has infinite positive and negative utility</mark>. So the possibility of an infinite universe would seem to play havoc with consequentialism in a number of ways.
Here’s an interesting question: does the deontologist have any parallel problems? That is, would the possibility of an infinite universe pose a problem for us, just like it does for the consequentialist?
I think not. For while it’s true that even deontologists typically approve of actions which increase aggregate welfare, our reason for doing so is very is different from the consequentialist’s. The consequentialist cares about increasing aggregate welfare for its own sake, regardless of how it affects individual persons. This is why utilitarians think you should feed yourself to the utility monster: all that matters is increasing aggregate wellbeing, and you, puny person, are merely a means to that end. If you being eaten by sharks makes the number on the big board go up by 0.00000001 utils relative to your not being eaten, then that’s what ought to happen.
Deontologists, by contrast, think that actions which increase aggregate welfare are usually (though not always) good because they involve helping lots of individual persons. For instance, donating to charity is good, not because it puts more beans in the imaginary jar, but because it makes things go better for this or that specific person, and this or that specific person have intrinsic value.
What this means is that even if the aggregate amount of total wellbeing in the universe is infinite, such that we cannot affect the aggregate, the deontologist can still say it’s good to increase people’s wellbeing, because what matters morally is helping that person, not just increasing the aggregate. Consequentialists, on the other hand, have a hard time with this.
Fortunately, <mark>consequentialism is obviously false.</mark>

UTILITY MONSTER – Black swan event with magnitudes greater than the sum total of human existence means that consequentialism is indeterminate.
Fisher 20 Richard Fisher, 12th November 2020, "The intelligent monster that you should let eat you," BBC, https://www.bbc.com/future/article/20201111-philosophy-of-utility-monsters-and-artificial-intelligence CHO
Imagine a <mark>monster</mark> with a set of words <mark>so powerful you have to let it eat you.</mark> It might sound fanciful, but we could be on a <mark>trajectory to inventing one right now</mark>, writes Richard Fisher.
One day, a philosopher was walking down the street, when a monster jumped out.
Despite its terrifying fangs, it was actually more polite and articulate than expected.
“I want to eat you, please,” the monster said.
“Sorry, but I’d prefer not to be your lunch,” the philosopher replied, and moved to keep walking.
“Wait,” said the monster, holding up a clawed finger. “What if I could present you with a sound argument?”
Armed with tenure and a TED talk, the philosophy professor very much doubted that any monster could be so persuasive, but was nonetheless intrigued.
“Proceed,” the philosopher said.
A few minutes later, the monster stomped away, with a dead professor in its belly.
This theoretical beast, called a “utility monster”, is a philosophical thought experiment, originally proposed in the 1970s. To reject its argument and stop it eating you, it asks you to discard a widely held and intuitive principle about how to weigh up right and wrong.
Long regarded as unlikely, if not impossible, the utility monster is usually considered the stuff of fanciful imagination. However, according to some researchers, we could be on a trajectory to building one – but it’ll be made of silicon, rather than flesh and claws.
If they are right, we could soon have some tough choices to make if we want to avoid being eaten.
To understand the monster’s deadly argument, you first have to understand the ethical theory it was proposed to challenge: a prominent school of thought in moral philosophy called utilitarianism.
The word utilitarianism is pretty dry – some have pointed out it sounds like doing the laundry – but it’s actually a profound way of thinking about happiness and the human quest to prioritise a good life for the most amount of people. Roughly sketched, a utilitarian is guided by the principle that well-being can be totted up and that we should aim to maximise it overall in the world. For many of its advocates, utilitarianism offers a simple rule for deciding how to live life, donate to charity and choose careers.
But while the principle of maximising wellbeing and happiness can seem intuitively correct, there are some extreme cases where it feels less so.
A utilitarian is guided by the principle that we should aim to maximise overall well-being in the world
One objection to utilitarianism’s most basic framing is that it would seem to permit acts that almost everybody would agree are wrong, like killing or supporting deliberate suffering. For example, in science-fiction writer Ursula Le Guin’s short story The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas, the reader is introduced to a thriving, joyful city whose prosperous existence totally depends on the extreme misery of a single child that lives in a dungeon. If one were to add up the total happiness of the city, then it would massively outweigh the child’s suffering. But as Le Guin writes, some residents cannot stomach the idea of the sacrificed child, no matter how much overall happiness it creates, and so “walk away” from the city.
There are various other challenges to utilitarianism, but the one that matters for us belongs to our hungry monster.
Back on the street, when the utilitarian philosopher asks to be presented with a case to be eaten, the monster explains that it has a special way of experiencing well-being.
“<mark>Your </mark>idea of <mark>happiness is </mark>only<mark> a mere fraction of what I am capable of feeling,</mark>” it says. “I am as different to you, a human, as you are to an ant. <mark>If I eat you, it will give me more well-being </mark>and satisfaction <mark>than all humans who have ever lived.”</mark>
The philosopher hesitates while trying to think of a counterargument. “Well, gosh, that’s certainly a valid...” But time’s up, the professor is lunch.
Of course, there are responses to the monster. <mark>A philosopher who believe</mark>d<mark> that there are certain moral codes that cannot be broken would have much less trouble.</mark> <mark>Killing people</mark> for food <mark>is wrong,</mark> she might say, so I <mark>don’t care how happy it will make you</mark> to eat me.

2. Direction of Fit: The Teleological Version
I will now offer a second version of the direction of fit argument. I will call this the Teleological Version. This argument, unlike the previous one, is tied closely to motivation. The argument is as follows:
1. If <mark>a</mark> state is a <mark>motivational state</mark>, then it <mark>embodies</mark> the having of <mark>a goal</mark>.
2. If a state embodies the having of a goal, <mark>then the content of the state specifies the goal</mark>.
3. To be in a state that embodies the having of a goal is to be in a state with a content to <mark>which the world must conform if the state is</mark> to be <mark>satisfied</mark>.
4. The content of intention, if intention is capable of motivating, specifies the goal (from 2).
5. <mark>Intention is</mark> a state with a <mark>content</mark> to which <mark>the world must conform if</mark> the intention is to be <mark>satisfied</mark> (from 3 & 4).
6. <mark>Foresight is</mark> essentially a <mark>conditional belief about how the world will be</mark>.
7. <mark>Belief</mark> is not a goal-directed state, but rather a state that <mark>has a content that must conform to the world if</mark> it is to be <mark>true</mark>.
8. Therefore, foresight is a state the content of which must conform to the world if it is to be true (from 6 & 7).
9. <mark>Thus</mark>, <mark>intention and foresight are distinct</mark> in principle (5 & 8).
This would appear to be a good argument against incorporating “foresight” qua belief into one’s intention. This is so because qua belief, foresight does not have a world-to-mind direction of fit, and, therefore, is not goal-directed.
Again it is open to PDI proponents to argue that the claim of PDI is that a rational agent must make that which is foreseen as likely to result from one’s action part of the content of one’s intention. This move, however, leaves advocates of PDI in the position of claiming that the death of the fetus is part of the doctor’s goal, motivationally speaking, in the original version of the therapeutic hysterectomy case. This is because the content of the motivational state specifies the goal. In the counterfactual version of the case, if the death of the fetus was really part of the doctor’s goal, then we should expect the doctor to take steps to ensure fetal death even if it should become evident that the fetus’ life could be saved.
On this teleological version of the direction of fit argument, then, the goal the doctor is to achieve would have to be, in part, the death of the fetus. This contradicts an obvious feature of the case, i.e., that the death of the fetus does not form part of the doctor’s goal in action. It is not part of what motivates him to do that which he does. It does not give him a reason in favor of his action. Indeed it is clear that in the original case of the therapeutic hysterectomy the doctor acts in spite of the fact that the likely fetal death gives him a prima facie reason not to act.
