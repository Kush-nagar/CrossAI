1. NL - The ICC is US-aligned --- incessant litigation is improbable.
Scharf 02 [Michael Scharf, professor of law @ New England School of Law and Director of the Center for International Law and Policy, The Case For Supporting the International Criminal Court, WashU Law, https://law.washu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/The-Case-For-Supporting-the-International-Criminal-Court.pdf] BZ **date retrieved from WashU Law publications**
Opponents of the ICC like to raise the specter of politicized indictments against American or Israeli officials drafted by prosecutors and confirmed by judges from countries that oppose our policies. A close examination of the list of the countries that have so far ratified the ICC, however, reveals that <mark>the ICC</mark> will be <mark>dominated</mark> not by our diplomatic opponents, but instead <mark>by our closest</mark> friends and <mark>allies.</mark> <mark>Of</mark> these <mark>46</mark> ratifying <mark>countries</mark>, nineteen are NATO or Western European allies, nine are Latin American and Caribbean countries with which the U.S. enjoys close relations, and four are U.S.-friendly Pacific island countries such as New Zealand and the Marshall Islands. With the possible exception of the Central African Republic, <mark>no country</mark> on the list would <mark>give a U.S</mark>. foreign <mark>policy-maker an</mark>y cause for <mark>concern</mark>.[25] On the other hand, the <mark>countries that</mark> most frequently <mark>oppose the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates in the United Nations (asian and middle-eastern countries such as China, Cuba, Iraq, Libya, North Korea, Syria, and the Sudan) are the countries <mark>least likely to ratify</mark> the ICC Statute, <mark>so they will not be able to participate</mark> in the ICC Assembly of Parties or nominate judges for the ICC's bench or select the Court's Prosecutor. Consequently, even if the U.S. does not ratify the Rome Treaty, the reality is that the ICC is going to be a very U.S.-friendly tribunal, unless, that is, the United States figuratively (and literally) wages war against the institution as suggested in Senator Helms' legislation.

2. NL - Checks solve.
Vindman 23 writes [Yevgeny Vindman; former deputy legal advisor on the White House National Security Council, Master of Laws from the Judge Advocate General's Legal Center and School; 04-11-2023; “It’s Time for the United States to Join the ICC”; Foreign Policy; https://foreignpolicy.com/2023/04/11/russia-putin-ukraine-war-icc-united-states-crimes-arrest-warrant/; accessed 02-04-2025] leon
The idea that unelected bureaucrats in a supranational body can question and impugn the actions of democratically elected national officials is unconvincing. Though international <mark>prosecutors</mark> have vast powers, they <mark>can be constrained by the U.N.</mark> system and are only effective when the actions at hand violate principles of international law either in the initiation or conduct of conflict. Any objectively just and appropriate use of force would be beyond the ICC’s reach. One would hope that any use of force by the United States would meet these simple criteria.
<mark>The greatest concern about cooperating</mark> with the ICC <mark>is that doing so would expose U.S.</mark> service <mark>members</mark> and leaders <mark>to politically motivated prosecution</mark> by foreign bureaucrats. <mark>But</mark> the court operates on the principle of complementarity, meaning that the ICC will not exercise jurisdiction when a state exercises its own prerogatives to investigate and prosecute potential war crimes. The ICC steps in only when a state fails to use its own national criminal justice apparatus to handle war crimes, as is currently the case in Russia. In the United States, however, the <mark>robust military justice systems ensure that crimes are investigated and prosecuted</mark> as a matter of maintaining order and discipline within the armed forces, <mark>making ICC jurisdiction against U.S. military personnel unlikely</mark>, so long as the United States continues to police its own behavior.
Because <mark>the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>is already compliant with</mark> core principles of <mark>international</mark> criminal <mark>law</mark>, supporting and even <mark>joining the ICC would have</mark> very <mark>little</mark> practical <mark>effect on U.S. operations</mark>. Support for the ICC would, however, eliminate the argument that the United States is hypocritical and send a clear message that the United States plays by the same rules that it expects of all other international actors.

3. NU - The US military is doomed---recruitment proves.
Filkins 2/3 [Dexter Filkins (), 2-3-2025, "The U.S. Military’s Recruiting Crisis", New Yorker, https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/2025/02/10/the-us-militarys-recruiting-crisis (accessed 2-7-2025)] ME
In 2022 and 2023, <mark>the Army missed</mark> its <mark>recruitment goal by</mark> nearly twenty-five per cent—about <mark>fifteen thousand </mark>troops<mark> a year</mark>. It hit the mark last year, but only by reducing the target by more than ten thousand. <mark>The Navy</mark> has also fared badly: it <mark>failed</mark> to reach its goals in 2023, then met them in 2024 by filling out the ranks with recruits of a lower standard; nearly <mark>half</mark> <mark>measured below average</mark> on an aptitude exam. The Army <mark>Reserve</mark> hasn’t met its benchmark since 2016, and the <mark>ranks</mark> <mark>are</mark> so <mark>depleted</mark> that active-duty officers have been put in charge of reserve units. Some experts worry that, if the country went to war, many reserve units might be <mark>unable to deploy</mark>. A U.S. official who works on these issues put it simply: “We can’t get enough people.” At the end of the Second World War, the American military had twelve million active-duty members. It now has 1.3 million—even though the population has more than doubled, and women are now eligible for armed service. “The U.S. <mark>military</mark> has been <mark>shrinking</mark> <mark>for thirty years</mark>,” Lawrence Wilkerson, a former senior State Department official who leads a task force on the challenges facing the armed services, said. “But its global commitments haven’t changed.” The military operates out of bases in more than fifty countries, and routinely deploys Special Operations forces to about eighty. Now, Wilkerson said, “it’s not clear that the military is large enough anymore for America to uphold its promises.”

1. T/NL - Restraint prevents provocation and fosters diplomacy --- forward posturing is unsustainable.
Walt 24 [Stephen M. Walt, Robert and Renée Belfer professor of international relations at Harvard University, 2-12-2024, It’s Not Too Late for Restrained U.S. Foreign Policy, Foreign Policy, https://foreignpolicy.com/2024/03/14/united-states-realism-restraint-great-power-strategy/, Willie T.] **edited for objectionable language**
The answer is no.¶ Let’s start by being clear on what the restraint movement wants. More than anything else, <mark>restrainers oppose</mark> fighting <mark>unnecessary “wars</mark> of choice,” where the vital interests of the United States are not involved. They are neither pacifists nor isolationists; they believe in a strong national defense; and they recognize that the United States should be willing to use force overseas in some circumstances. Instead of withdrawing from the world, restrainers believe the United States should trade and invest in other countries, encourage other states to do the same, and be open to managed immigration instead of building walls in a fit of xenophobia. Indeed, <mark>restrainers</mark> think the United States should be more actively and effectively engaged than it is today, putting diplomacy first and <mark>making</mark> the use of <mark>force Washington’s last resort rather than</mark> its first <mark>impulse</mark>. Why? Because restrainers also understand the limits of military power: It may be necessary on occasion, but it is a crude instrument that invariably produces lots of unintended consequences. It is also hard to sustain public support for wars when vital interests are not engaged and success is hard to define. In particular, restrainers oppose trying to spread liberal values through regime change and military occupation, because such efforts typically lead to costly quagmires or failed states instead.¶ In practical terms, most restrainers believe the United States should disengage militarily from the Middle East and have normal relations with all countries in that region, instead of “special” relations with a few states and no relations with others. They think Washington should encourage our NATO allies to take greater responsibility for their own defense. Unlike Trumpian wannabes such as Republican Sen. J.D. Vance, however, most restrainers support continued aid to Ukraine combined with a dedicated and flexible effort to reach a diplomatic settlement. Restrainers are divided on the best way to respond to China, with some favoring more vigorous efforts at containment while others emphasize the need to reduce tensions and pursue mutually beneficial compromises. But they all agree that <mark>the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>is</mark> still <mark>overcommitted overseas</mark>, as well as overly prone to relying on military solutions that cannot solve underlying political problems.¶ This view is as relevant today as it was a decade or more ago. Remember: Many of the <mark>problems the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>is</mark> now <mark>grappling with might</mark> have <mark>be</mark>en <mark>avoided</mark> entirely <mark>had</mark> the <mark>restrainers’</mark> earlier warnings <mark>been heeded. If the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>had not pushed</mark> hard <mark>for</mark> open-ended <mark>NATO enlargement</mark>—and to bring Ukraine into the Western orbit and eventually into NATO—<mark>Russia’s</mark> seizure of Crimea in 2014 and its illegal <mark>invasion</mark> in February 2022 <mark>would</mark> probably <mark>never have occurred</mark>. Indeed, had the Biden administration shown more flexibility in the months preceding the Russian attack, been more supportive of Turkish and Israeli mediation efforts in the spring of 2022, or pushed for a cease-fire when Ukraine had the upper hand that fall, the two states might never have fought at all or ended the conflict before Ukraine had suffered so much damage. It is impossible to know any of this for certain, of course, but U.S. officials did not do all that they might have done to avert the war that Ukraine is now losing.¶ Events in the Middle East offer a similar lesson. Both the Trump and Biden administrations mistakenly focused on trying to normalize relations between Israel and its Arab neighbors while completely ignoring the Palestinians, who were under growing pressure from increasingly right-wing Israeli governments. As restrainers warned, this shortsighted approach was bound to trigger an explosion, leading to the tragic results of Oct. 7, 2023.¶ Israel’s onslaught in Gaza—a campaign that has now killed more than 30,000 Palestinians, destroyed or damaged 50 percent to 60 percent of the buildings in Gaza, and badly tarnished Israel’s global image (along with America’s)—is also a telling reminder of the limits of military power. Israel has massive military superiority and is using it wholeheartedly, but force alone cannot resolve the political differences that keep its conflict with the Palestinians going. Hamas is not going to be destroyed by military action, and the fundamental issue—how to accommodate the legitimate aspirations of Israeli Jews and Palestinian Arabs and allow both populations to lead secure lives—will remain unresolved. Much the same could be said for U.S. efforts to halt Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping by dropping bombs and firing missiles at them, instead of using its considerable leverage to bring about a cease-fire in Gaza (the Houthis’ stated condition for halting their attacks). These examples illustrate the reflexive tendency to think that complex political issues can be addressed by blowing something up, an impulse that restrainers have long opposed.¶ Restrainers also recognize that <mark>American power</mark>, while considerable, <mark>is not unlimited</mark>. In an era of great-power competition, <mark>it is</mark> more <mark>important</mark> than ever <mark>to set</mark> clear <mark>priorities</mark> and to make sure our main objectives are not at odds with one another. Today, <mark>the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates is trying to <mark>help Ukraine</mark> defeat Russia in Ukraine, backing <mark>Israel’s</mark> efforts to wipe out Hamas no matter what the human consequences may be, <mark>and</mark> trying to permanently <mark>cripple</mark> [eliminate] <mark>China</mark>’s efforts to develop world-class semiconductor and other digital technologies. This is hardly a restrained agenda, and the contradictions within it are as obvious as they are self-defeating. Instead of playing the two main Eurasian powers off each other, we have spent several decades giving them additional reasons to work together. Instead of isolating Russia and limiting Chinese influence in the global south, our support for Israel’s campaign in Gaza has put the hypocrisy of the “rules-based order” in sharp relief and handed China a cheap propaganda victory. President Joe Biden’s failure to reenter the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action might be included here as well, insofar as it led Iran to restart its nuclear program, increase its support for its various regional proxies, and then give active military support to Russia’s efforts in Ukraine.¶ Lastly, advocates of restraint have long warned that <mark>overstretched military</mark> commitments and “forever wars” would <mark>have debilitating effects</mark> back home. To maintain support for an overly ambitious foreign policy, U.S. leaders have come to rely on the all-volunteer force, thereby insulating most of the electorate from the consequences of its decisions. <mark>The U.S.</mark> military <mark>now faces</mark> an <mark>acute recruitment</mark> crisis; according to West Point’s Modern War Institute, this is partly because Americans “witnessed a generation of servicemembers deploy again and again to a war without bounds.” U.S. presidents concealed the costs of these activities by borrowing the money instead of raising taxes, by inflating threats, and by concealing some of what they are doing from the American people. When some of these secret activities are eventually revealed, however, trust in public institutions erodes further. As the Founding Fathers understood, republics that are constantly at war put their republican character at risk. It is not hard to see the fragile condition of American democracy today as partly due to an unrealistic and unsuccessful foreign policy, one that restrainers are striving to correct.
