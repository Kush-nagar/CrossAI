However, whether as an approach to learning, innovation, persuasion or culture shift,<mark>policy simulations </mark>derive their power from two central features: their combination of simulation and gaming(Geurts et al. 2007). 1. The simulation element: the unique combination of simulation with role-playing. The unique simulation/role-play mix enables participants to<mark> create possible futures</mark> relevant to the topic being studied. This is diametrically opposed to the more traditional, teacher-centric approaches in which a future is produced for them. In policy simulations, possible futures are much more than an object of tabletop discussion and verbal speculation. ‘No other technique <mark>allows</mark> a group of participants to engage in collective<mark> action in a safe environment</mark> to create and analyse the futures they want to explore’ (Geurts et al. 2007: 536). 2. The game element: the interactive and tailor-made modelling and design of the policy game. The actual run of the policy simulation is only one step, though a most important and visible one, in a collective process of investigation, communication, and evaluation of performance. In the context of a post-graduate course in public policy development, for example, a policy simulation is a dedicated game constructed in collaboration with practitioners to achieve a high level of proficiency in relevant aspects of the policy development process. To drill down to a level of finer detail, policy development <mark>simulations</mark>—as forms of interactive or participatory modelling— are particularly effective in <mark>develop</mark>ing participant <mark>knowledge and skills in</mark> the five key areas of the <mark>policy</mark> <mark>development</mark> process(and success criteria), namely: Complexity, Communication, Creativity, Consensus, and Commitment to action (‘the five Cs’). The capacity to provide effective learning support in these five categories has proved to be particularly helpful in strategic decision-making (Geurts et al. 2007). Classical policy analysis, however absent from actual policy making, remains an important vehicle for teaching policy analysts the connections between their analysis and the policymaking world in which their recommendations would live. <mark>Even if it implies more power than analysts</mark> will ever have, classical policy <mark>analysis teaches that politics, law, implementation</mark>, social structures, organizational behavior, and other factors <mark>are critical to</mark> policy <mark>outcomes </mark>and must play key roles in thinking through possible ways to address policy problems. <mark>Bringing policy ideas to fruition</mark>, bridging the worlds of research and policy making, <mark>is a critical skill</mark> for analysts to develop. In addition, policy schools are instilling in prospective policy analysts the structure and habits of mind to engage successfully in the policy enterprise. 28 <mark>Teaching disciplined thinking</mark> for public service <mark>is important</mark>. Policy <mark>analysts</mark> not only <mark>have a problem-oriented, interdisciplinary approach</mark> to policy and <mark>the ability to synthesize and bring policy relevance to problems</mark> that social scientists are not trained for, <mark>but they understand the</mark> "rational lunacy of policy-making <mark>systems</mark>" (Weiss 2009). In the absence of written classical policy analyses, <mark>policy analysts</mark> become their human embodiment. Their training will provide a mental picture of how a classical policy analysis should be performed. They can derive elements of policy analysis from writing position papers, briefing policy makers, and controlling meetings. They <mark>anticipate counterarguments and frame</mark> their <mark>analyses recognizing alternative options</mark>. In short, <mark>the mental map of a policy analysis</mark> allows good policy analysts not only to be effective in their jobs but also to <mark>advance</mark> into the <mark>public debate</mark> the appropriate elements of a policy analysis. Further, <mark>the problem orientation</mark> of policy analysis <mark>focuses</mark> at least some <mark>attention on social problems, not just</mark> political <mark>expediency</mark>. The role of policy analysts is not merely to translate research for policy makers, but to use creative means to turn available knowledge about the implications of various policy options into actionable policy recommendations appropriate for their clients. This is a subtle skill requiring attention to both political realities and the best available research. Finally, prospective policy analysts are instructed repeatedly about the importance of their relationship to the client(s), yet far less attention is paid to the other part of the policy analyst's relationship: to the community of knowledge producers. Policy <mark>analysts play critical roles as intermediaries</mark> between "custodians of the knowable" and policy makers. Their training should include <mark>the ability to understand and interpret the</mark> academic <mark>lit</mark>erature <mark>on a topic at a far deeper level</mark> than most journalists have the time or, often, the analytic skill set to uncover.
