NL. Recognition is popular. 
Meservey '22 [Joshua; Senior Fellow @ Hudson Institute, Former Research Fellow @ the Heritage Foundation, Masters in Law & Diplomacy from Tufts University, BA in History from the Templeton Honors College @ Eastern University; 5-9-2022; Heritage Foundation; "Somalilanders’ Quest for Independence Isn’t “Neocolonial” Plot. It’s Self-Determination.," https://www.heritage.org/africa/commentary/somalilanders-quest-independence-isnt-neocolonial-plot-its-self-determination; accessed: 12-3-2024] tristan **brackets & elipses r og**
The sentiment is difficult to gauge, and the best data is 20 years old. Nonetheless, during Somaliland’s 2001 referendum—which the international observer mission reported was overwhelmingly perceived by Somalilanders as a vote on <mark>independence</mark>, and which <mark>passed with 97% of the vote</mark>—the highest percentage of “no” votes in any region was only 16%. Voters who were opposed may have abstained from the vote, and turnout was indeed significantly lower in opposition areas, but it still registered at 35%, even in the most ardently unionist area of Somaliland.
Furthermore, <mark>clan</mark> elder<mark>s</mark> in the contested regions have in the past reportedly <mark>contemplated switching</mark> their <mark>allegiance</mark> from Puntland <mark>to Somaliland</mark>, suggesting their affinity for their clan brethren in Puntland is not as ardent as some claim.
2AC---AT: Secession
NQ. Historical trends prove secessionism is inevitable.
Marche '21 [Stephen; The author of “The Next Civil War”; 12-31-2021; Washington Post; "Perspective," https://www.washingtonpost.com/outlook/2021/12/31/secession-civil-war-stephen-marche/; accessed: 12-7-2024] tristan
It’s not that secession can’t work. The rest of the world is busily at it all the time. <mark>Separatism is a global</mark> political <mark>trend</mark>. <mark>The number of nations</mark> in the world has <mark>tripled since</mark> 19<mark>45</mark>. And there will soon be more. “Right now, there are about 60 secessionist movements worldwide. Sixty independence movements is a pretty large number by historical standards,” says Ryan Griffiths, a professor at Syracuse University who focuses on the dynamics of secession and the study of sovereignty. “<mark>In the long run, there will be another</mark> secessionist movement in the United States. <mark>It will just happen. No country is permanent</mark>. It will change. It <mark>will break apart</mark> in some way.”

N/!. Movements don’t escalate---empirics.
Kingsbury '17 [Damien; Emeritus Professor @ Deakin University, Supervises PhD candidates; 10-4-2017; Conversation; "Passion and pain: why secessionist movements rarely succeed," https://theconversation.com/passion-and-pain-why-secessionist-movements-rarely-succeed-85097; accessed: 12-15-2024] tristan
<mark>There are</mark> currently well <mark>over 100 secessionist movements</mark>, including four in the <mark>Philippines</mark>, dozens in <mark>India</mark>, around eight in <mark>Myanmar</mark>, and several dozen in <mark>Africa</mark>. Many of these have produced bloodshed and trauma well in excess of possible practical gains. Yet, <mark>despite</mark> their <mark>numbers</mark>, <mark>very few</mark> secessionist movements <mark>are</mark> ultimately <mark>successful</mark>, while the costs for governments imposing a nominal unity can be high for all involved.
With high risks and limited chances of success, secessionist movements are rarely about pragmatism and more about fervour. <mark>Even with popular support</mark>, these <mark>movements rarely have the political or military capacity to</mark> impose their will on the state from which they intend to <mark>secede</mark>.
