1. COMMUNITY DA.
Debates about debate are good to figure out how we as debaters can produce change from our social locations. Our interp sustains those debates since teams clash over the merits of following topical norms.
Athanasopoulos ’23 [Charles, Corinne Sugino; 2023; Assistant Professor of African American and African Studies & English at The Ohio State University; Contemporary Argumentation & Debate, “Clash of the Uncivilized: An Alternative Approach to Policy Debate,” pp. 103-120]
Ironically, this <mark>community</mark> has <mark>become engulfed in a debate about debate</mark>. As a previous editor of Contemporary Argumentation & Debate (Louden, 2004) has noted, “the debate about debate simultaneously tears at the fabric of community and <mark>builds shared norms</mark>, an unending search for an ‘acceptable’ balance.” (p. 40). Taking the concern that critical debate presents a threat to the ability to produce adequate clash into consideration, we explore the potential for alternative approaches to policy debate that have heretofore been discounted. Put another way, this article seeks to articulate the nuanced debates and clash offered by the uncivilized. Building on scholars such as Amber Kelsie, Shanara Reid-Brinkley (2008), Tiffany Dillard-Knox (2014), Rashad Evans (2015), and others, we argue that these debates -- what we refer to as “revolutionary v. revolutionary debates” or “rev v. rev debates” -- <mark>produce nuanced</mark> and generative <mark>clash over</mark> the <mark>nature of resistance</mark> to structures of domination. We choose to use the term “revolutionary” in contrast to traditional approaches to top-down, U.S. governmental reform, to designate those approaches that seek to move beyond reformulation of particular institutions to a reorientation of societal foundations. We seek to <mark>displace</mark> the <mark>presumption</mark> that <mark>debates ought to solely entail consideration over</mark> reform and the desirability of <mark>governmental action</mark>. Consequently, the label “revolutionary” does not imply the purity of these alternative approaches. On the contrary, we suggest they can and should be critiqued, examined, and debated. In doing so, we highlight how centering Black and minoritarian knowledge produces thoughtful debates about the resolution concerning the analytical frameworks, methodologies, and representations deployed by opposing teams.

Lack of competitive equity is the status quo.
Zarychta ’21 [Emily and Holly Popenia; July; articling student and Community Law Program Lawyer; Community Legal Assistance Society, “Intro to JR: The Basics of Procedural Fairness,” https://clasbc.net/intro-to-judicial-review-the-basics-of-procedural-fairness/]
Other <mark>Procedural Fairness Issues</mark>: <mark>Bias</mark>
It is common to feel that a decision-maker was biased when you receive a decision that goes against you. Bias in administrative proceedings usually only refers to very serious situations. It is not biased of a decision-maker to believe the other party instead of you, without <mark>other factors influencing</mark> the decision-maker’s <mark>decision</mark>.
There are two major forms of bias:
<mark>Individual bias</mark>: includes situations <mark>where</mark> the <mark>decision-maker</mark> may be reasonably perceived to <mark>have</mark> a monetary or material <mark>interest</mark> in the outcome of the decision, or a personal relationship with those involved in the dispute. For example, if the decision-maker was friends with the opposing party.
<mark>Institutional bias</mark>: includes prior knowledge or information about the matter in dispute, or a <mark>predisposition toward an outcome</mark>. For example, if the decision-maker advocated for a certain group, and then later decided an issue involving that group.
2. NO PARADOX.
It belies the model minority, causing distress.
Lai ’24 [Tabitha Kok Chee; 2024; Masters candidate in Multicultural Education at California State University, Sacramento; Master’s Project presented to the Department of Graduate and Professional Studies in Education, “The Model Minority Myth and Mental Health Workshops for Adolescents,” https://www.proquest.com/docview/3116476540?pq-origsite=gscholar&fromopenview=true&sourcetype=Dissertations%20&%20Theses]
The study done by Atkin et al. focuses on two major components. The first is the <mark>internalization of</mark> the <mark>Model Minority Myth, and</mark> the second is the <mark>psychological distress it causes</mark>. Internalization of the MMM refers “to <mark>identification with</mark> its <mark>stereotyp</mark>ical characteristics - achievement orientation and unrestricted mobility” (Atkin et al., 2018, p. 109). The first component of internalization is the Model Minority Myth of achievement orientation, which centers on the idea that Asian Americans accentuate work ethic (perseverance and drive) more than other minority groups (Atkin et al., 2018, p. 109). The second component of internalization is the Model Minority Myth of unrestricted mobility, stating that compared to other minority groups, <mark>Asian</mark> American<mark>s value fairness and</mark> thus <mark>are not affected by racism</mark> or barriers in academic or professional settings (Atkin et al., 2018, p. 109). These types of <mark>internalization</mark> can <mark>lead to chronic stress</mark> and <mark>psychological distress</mark>, such as <mark>anxiety</mark>, <mark>depression</mark>, and <mark>difficulty in help-seeking behaviors</mark>.
Buddhists want proportional equality, not equal individualism. Overcorrect for structural unfairness.
Wang ’23 [Mei, Marc Rieger, Sebastian Reitz; 2023; Professor of Behavioral Finance at Otto Beisham School of Management; Review of Behavioral Economics, “Economic Individualism, Perceived Fairness, and Policy Preference: A Cross-Cultural Comparison,” vol. 10]
While talking about the impact of culture, the role of religion and philosophical background cannot be ignored. The possible influences of religion on development of the Western welfare state have already been the subject of the earlier research (Esping-Andersen, 1990). With <mark>egalitarianism</mark> embedded in New Testament Christianity, equality has been described as “an unshakable <mark>principle of</mark> the <mark>Christian tradition</mark>” (Todorov, 1999). However, on the development of the western welfare state, the influence of Catholicism and Protestantism is different. While Catholic social doctrine developed a strong positive legitimation for social policy intervention, reformed Protestantism due to its emphasis such as on self-help and individual asceticism is <mark>associated to</mark> economic <mark>individualism</mark> and had an anti-welfare state character (Feldman, 1983; Manow, 2002). <mark>In East Asian cultures</mark>, Confucianism, <mark>Buddhism</mark> and Taoism <mark>have</mark> a profound influence. In Confucianism, <mark>proportional equality</mark>, <mark>treating people differently according to their position</mark>, or “role-based equality”, is a fundamental principle (Li, 2012). Social and economic inequalities are accepted and tolerated, since they are viewed as helpful to encourage personal effort (Li, 2012). The brotherhood of monks and low emphasis on hierarchy in Buddhism, however, reflects an egalitarian ethic and principle of equality in this religion (Jayasuriya, 2008). On the other hand, Wu wei (noninterference or freedom) and the emergence of spontaneous order (harmony) when people are left alone to pursue their own happiness and prosperity, two fundamental principles of Taoism, adhered to the principle of freedom and economic liberalism (Dorn, 1998; Dorn, 2016; Irwin, 2016).
