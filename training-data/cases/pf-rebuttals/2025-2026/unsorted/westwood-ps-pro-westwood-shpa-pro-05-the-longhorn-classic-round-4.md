Fragmentation collapses the global economy.
Sullivan ’23 [Andrew Sullivan; President and CEO of the Internet Society; July 10; "A Fragmented Internet Is A Threat To The Future Of Global Business"; Forbes, https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbestechcouncil/2023/07/10/a-fragmented-internet-is-a-threat-to-the-future-of-global-business/] +
The Internet’s global scope, reach and redundancy—enabled by its decentralized nature—are part of what makes it so useful for business. The current drift towards fragmentation raises the specter of splinternets—a world in which the "open, globally connected Internet splinters into a collection of isolated networks controlled by governments," or corporations or both. It would be a loss to all humanity, and we must avoid it.
Numerous forces are busily creating this dystopian future. Governments around the world are wrongheadedly trying to fix social or societal issues by regulating the Internet. At the same time, some of the most successful and profitable corporations in the world are vying for ownership of so much of the Internet’s real estate that any hope of competing with these behemoths will be impossible.
And, in a techno-paradox, people are increasingly mistrustful of the Internet even as they are more dependent than ever upon it.
The Beauty Of The Internet
The Internet is made up of many independent networks that, through common technology, all work together without central authority. But that is where the problem lies. Increasingly, we see local legal decisions, legislative and regulatory proposals and company actions that in different ways tend to isolate those networks from one another.
The way parts of the Internet so easily “snap together” like building blocks reduces costs. Modularity allows companies to outsource parts of their operations to best-in-class operators. Even small companies can have a world-class network operation. Any enterprise can have a global and highly redundant infrastructure to protect against even the most unlikely disaster in any one place. Companies can work across international divisions and access the talents of people wherever in the world they happen to be.
According to Forbes Advisor, <mark>retail e-commerce</mark> increased by more than $1 trillion U.S. just from 2021-2023, and more than 20% of retail purchases are expected to be online in 2023. And consumer sales increases aren’t the only place where the Internet has transformed business. The availability of <mark>cheap</mark>, largely <mark>reliable comm</mark>unications <mark>enables better business-to-business communication</mark>, faster <mark>customer feedback</mark> cycles, better <mark>supply chain management</mark> and workplace flexibility in the face of things like a global pandemic.
<mark>All</mark> of this is <mark>at risk if the Internet turns into</mark> a series of partly-connected <mark>islands</mark> separated along the lines of geography or jurisdiction. So, not only does <mark>a fragmented Internet</mark> create problems for the technology industry, it <mark>presents a threat to the global economy</mark>.

<mark>The American economy is heading for</mark> trouble, and it may go into <mark>recession</mark> next year. If that happens, it would be bad news for the rest of us, including the UK. That old adage still applies: when the US sneezes, Europe catches a cold.
<mark>At first</mark> sight, that <mark>might seem</mark> an overly <mark>pessimistic</mark> outlook. US shares are still close to their all-time highs, with the S&P 500 index up 16 per cent this year. Unemployment, at 4.4 per cent, is the highest since 2021, but is only creeping upwards. And while consumer confidence fell sharply last month, that was probably associated with the government shutdown, which is now over.
<mark>But</mark> there are several powerful reasons to expect things to slow down sharply in the coming months. One is that the stock market boom has been driven by its technology giants, with the top 10 companies accounting for more than one-third of the total capitalisation, up from 18 per cent 10 years ago. Another is that <mark>US house prices have weakened</mark>. They are not facing an overall collapse, though in some regions, notably Florida, they are very soft. But the big rises of recent years have come to an end and <mark>prices</mark> overall <mark>are expected to run behind pay increases</mark>. The greatest concern, however, is what artificial intelligence will do to the job market.
In the long-run it is clear that <mark>AI</mark> will give a huge boost to the overall efficiency of the world economy, and since the US is in the forefront of its development, it should in theory at least be the principal beneficiary. But in the short-run it <mark>is killing jobs</mark>.
An analysis by a US firm, Challenger, Gray & Christmas, showed that AI was cited in US company reports and presentations in <mark>enabling</mark> a <mark>headcount reduction of</mark> nearly <mark>50,000</mark> jobs this year. Some 31,000 of those were cut in October alone. In an economy the size of the US that may not seem a lot, and it may suit employers to blame AI when they are trimming their workforce for other reasons. But since AI is cited as a reason for job losses in about one-fifth of instances, we are clearly in the early stages of something huge.
What should we look at to see whether the US will indeed plunge into recession next year, and what would that mean for us here in the UK? There is a problem. Because of the US Government shutdown we don’t have up-to-date official figures from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. We did, though, just get some estimates of what is happening in the private sector in the APD National Employment Report. It is not a good story, for this showed that 32,000 jobs had been lost in November. You should always be cautious about any one set of economic indicators, but if this trend is confirmed in the coming weeks then it looks likely that the US will indeed head into recession. A <mark>sharp rise in unemployment is</mark> one of <mark>the best indicator</mark>s that this is happening.
