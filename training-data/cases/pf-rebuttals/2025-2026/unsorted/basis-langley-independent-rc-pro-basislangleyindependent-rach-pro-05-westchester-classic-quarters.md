2AC --- Westchester Quarters

State inconsistencies are the root cause.
Andrea <mark>Ercolino</mark>, 11-11-20<mark>24</mark>, Managing Editor of Staff, "Regulatory Inconsistencies Regarding Sports Gambling – Journal of International Business & Law", Hofstra Journal of International Business and Law, https://www.hofstrajibl.org/2024/11/regulatory-inconsistencies-regarding-sports-gambling/ //CChun
I. Sports Gambling and Advertising in the United States Following the Supreme Court case of Murphy v. National Collegiate Athletic Association in 2018, there have been many developments in the realm of sports betting. In this case, New Jersey made claims that the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (“PASPA”) of 1992, which made it unlawful for states to “sponsor, operate, advertise, promote, license or authorize by law or compact… a lottery, sweepstakes, or other betting, gambling, or wagering scheme based… on competitive sporting events,” was unconstitutional under the anti-commandeering doctrine.[1] This doctrine prohibits the federal government from forcing state governments to enforce or implement federal laws.[2] New Jersey argued that the PASPA forced states to follow this federal law.[3] The Supreme Court ruled that the PASPA was unconstitutional under the anti-commandeering doctrine, which in turn allowed states to now legalize and regulate sports betting at their own discretion.[4] Since this decision, about 30 different states have decided to enact laws to legalize online sports gambling.[5] <mark>Each state has different rules and regulations</mark> regarding the legalization of sports gambling.[6] For example, <mark>some</mark> states <mark>only allow gambling in casinos or</mark> on a limited number of <mark>online</mark> sports betting apps, <mark>while other s</mark>tates might <mark>allow for a broad range </mark>of online betting apps and casino betting.[7] States such as Tennessee and Vermont have online only sports gambling. Only 9 states have in-person only sports gambling and 12 have not yet legalized sports betting, and it is therefore, still illegal in any capacity.[8] Each state also has different regulations for sports betting advertisements. New York recently has taken a tougher position on sports gambling advertisements.[9] New legislation now mandates all gambling-related advertisements, including online sports wagering, to include warning messages of the harmful and addictive effects of gambling.[10] In New Jersey, advertisements cannot target minors and must display a hotline message for potential gambling addition help.[11] There are also states like South Dakota and Washington who have no sports advertising policies.[12] II. User Protection Concerns These regulatory inconsistencies have led to concerns regarding user protection and privacy rights.[13] These issues become prominent not just in the user market but also for the sports betting corporations that operate in different states.[14] Due to these <mark>different rules </mark>in each state, this can <mark>encourage users to resort to illegal offshore betting sites.</mark>[15] This raises concern for users’ privacy rights.[16] These illegal sites require extensive financial and personal information to be utilized, causing users to face the risk of their information being exploited or leaked.[17] When utilizing legalized sports betting platforms, it is safe to provide your information as it will be protected.[18] These <mark>regulated sportsbooks have a duty to uphold user protection</mark> and privacy rights such as protecting the user’s funds and personal data.[19] When resorting to <mark>offshore</mark> books, these <mark>platforms do not have a duty</mark> to protect their user’s funds or personal data.[20] If your “bookie” fails to pay you, you have <mark>no means</mark> or method <mark>of enforcing payment.</mark>[21] <mark>These</mark> offshore books <mark>also provide no benefit to the</mark> United States <mark>economy because they do not contribute</mark> any <mark>taxes.</mark>[22] For users, different advertisements can lead to confusion and unfair or unethical targeting.[23] Since excessive gambling creates risk for addiction, corporations need to be careful with how they are advertising their services. The director of the Office of Responsible Gambling, Natalie Wright, stated “we are seeing sports betting advertising in almost every aspect of sports, which sends a message that sports betting is part and parcel of enjoying sports.”[24] Recently, Senator Richard Blumenthal proposed the SAFE Bet Act which would establish a nationwide ban on sports betting but also crack down on the advertising inconsistencies.[25] With the new Act, gambling operators would be prohibited from running advertisements between 8 a.m. and 10 p.m., during live sporting events and would prohibit incentives such as “bonus bets” or “no sweat bets.”[26] III. Proposed Solution To combat these risks, <mark>new laws should</mark> be adopted to <mark>unify these standards across states and countries. This would halt</mark> those living in states where online sports gambling is currently illegal, from <mark>resorting to offshore betting sites</mark> and therefore reduce the risks posed by them. Although it has been held unconstitutional under the anti-commandeering doctrine to force states to implement federal sports gambling regulations,[27] the federal government can provide tax incentives for states to legalize sports gambling. The Court has previously held in South Dakota v. Dole, that Congress may provide indirect encouragement of state action to reach uniformity in the States’ drinking ages.[28] There, the Court found that this was a valid use of Congress’ spending power.[29] Similarly here, Congress may provide a tax credit incentive to the states and legal sports gambling operators to encourage state action to reach uniformity in online sports gambling. Not only does sports gambling provide extensive revenue for each state and the federal government, but a more uniform standard will protect users engaging in online sports betting from having their private information at risk. One of the major appeals of offshore gambling is tax-free winnings for the user.[30] Offshore sites are also not subject to state or federal taxes.[31] So not only could a tax incentive encourage the states to legalize sports gambling, but also can be used to encourage offshore sites to become licensed as well. Addressing the newly proposed SAFE Bet Act, this would likely fail to promote responsible and safe gambling because if sports gambling is banned nationwide, people of all states will then resort to offshore gambling sites. If sports gambling was uniformly legalized in each state, the states must also adopt uniform advertising regulations to promote safe and regulated gambling. If a legalized standard for sports gambling is implemented, the states will need to adopt stricter advertising regulations to reduce the risk of potential addiction to sports gambling that can in turn lead to larger and broader gambling addictions. In this realm, the SAFE Bet Act can be a beneficial starting point to cracking down on advertising throughout the United States when it comes to sports gambling.[32] This is because the advertising section of the Act requires that states offering sports betting to meet uniform federal standards.[33] Specifically, prohibiting sports betting advertisements that are designed to induce users to gamble by showing how to or explaining how it works.[34]
