One of the <mark>major stressors</mark> <mark>affect</mark>ing the <mark>health of immigrants</mark> and refugees in the US, especially those from racial-ethnic minority backgrounds, is racism and discrimination (Ayon, 2015; Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009; Takeuchi, 2016; Williams, 2012). Racial discrimination, along with other forms of social disadvantage, has detrimental effects on health and contributes to existing health disparities (Colen, Ramey, Cooksey, & Williams, 2017; Smedley, Stith, & Nelson, 2003; Williams, 2012). At the societal level, racism and discrimination operate through residential segregation and when individuals are unequally treated in accessing jobs, education, healthcare, social services, and so on, due to their foreign-born status, ethnic origin, and/or race (Ayon, 2015; Williams, 2012). At the individual level, <mark>discrimination</mark> “gets under the skin” <mark>as an acute</mark> and chronic <mark>stressor</mark> that <mark>activates physiological responses</mark>, such as <mark>elevated blood pressure</mark>, <mark>heart rate</mark>, and <mark>cortisol secretions</mark>, that <mark>trigger declines in mental and physical health</mark> (Clark, Anderson, Clark, & Williams, 1999; Williams, 1999). Perceived discrimination, defined as a behavioral manifestation of a negative attitude, judgment, or unfair treatment toward members of a group (Williams, 1999), has been associated with poor mental and physical health (Paradies, 2006; Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009; Williams, 2012; Williams & Mohammed, 2009; Williams, Neighbors, & Jackson, 2003). <mark>Routine discrimination</mark>, in particular, may erode an individual’s protective resources and <mark>increase vulnerability to physical illness through</mark> over- or <mark>underactivity of allostatic systems</mark> (Seeman, Singer, Rowe, Horwitz, & McEwen, 1997).
