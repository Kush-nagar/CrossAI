Wolin decontextualizes and misinterprets Bataille.
Patrick Miller 22. A philosopher of twentieth-century Continental thought with an emphasis on French metaphysics and politics in response to Fascism “What is Fascism Without a State?: Countering Claims of Bataille’s Left Fascism” Critical Horizons: A Journal of Philosophy and Social Theory. DOI: 10.1080/14409917.2022.2104082
<mark>Wolin’s interpretation is</mark> heavily <mark>dependent on quotations from</mark> one of Bataille’s later works, <mark>The Accursed Share</mark>, written between 1946 and 1949. <mark>A</mark> supposed <mark>example of Bataille’s embrace of the aesthetic of violence</mark> cited by Wolin <mark>is “Glory</mark> … expresses a movement of senseless frenzy, of measureless expenditure of energy, which the fervour <mark>of combat</mark> presupposes. Combat is glorious in that it is always beyond calculation at some moment".14 <mark>This is not</mark> necessarily <mark>a</mark> glowing <mark>endorsement of war</mark>. <mark>Recognising that combat is beyond calculation is not </mark>the same as <mark>endorsing combat as a means to exceed bourgeois society</mark>. More importantly, <mark>this quote</mark> from Bataille <mark>is in the context of a discussion on rank in a potlatch</mark>. Potlatch is an Indigenous tradition from the Pacific Northwest. It is <mark>a ceremonial feast</mark> in which possessions are destroyed or given away to opposing tribes. The opposing tribes are then, by tradition or social pressure, expected to return with interest the possessions given away or destroyed. Hence, <mark>Wolin distorts</mark> <mark>Bataille's view by ignoring the context </mark>of this quotation. <mark>Bataille states that glory and warfare are misunderstood</mark> if one does not understand them through the acquisition of rank. The clearest form of obtaining rank and its relationship to expenditure is the potlatch. Likewise, communities often recognise glory and bestow accolades in reference to one’s willingness to confront death and the severity of chance in combat. What makes one glorious is the embrace of chance, and “Combat is glorious in that it is always beyond calculation at some moment".15 <mark>Bataille is not recognising combat as the highest form of useless expenditure</mark> but recognising it as a powerful form that is honoured through rank.

Africa enters 2026 carrying more weight than momentum. The continent is no longer operating on the margins of global disorder; it is inside it, shaped by it and, at times, exploited by it. The question of whether Africa will be affected by the fracturing of the global system is as distant as it is moot. If anything, the priority now is whether African states can convert disorder into room to maneuver or else risk a drift into managed decline.¶ First on the agenda is a harrowing continental security dynamic, which is perhaps the most unforgiving place to begin. <mark>Armed conflict</mark> now <mark>affects</mark> <mark>more African countries</mark> <mark>than</mark> at <mark>any point in</mark> <mark>the past two decades</mark>. <mark>Sudan</mark> alone has lost an estimated 400,000 lives since 2023, with nearly 13 million people displaced and the country <mark>fractured into rival zones</mark> of control that <mark>resemble war</mark> economies more than states. Sudan is no longer a single crisis — it has become an engine of <mark>instability</mark> <mark>for </mark>an <mark>entire </mark>sub<mark>region</mark>.¶ Adjacent to the Horn of Africa’s woes is the perennially unstable <mark>Sahel</mark>, which is now considered the <mark>most</mark> <mark>lethal zone of militant violence worldwide</mark>. More than <mark>half</mark> of all <mark>deaths</mark> <mark>linked to terror</mark> groups on the continent occur there, with civilians accounting for the majority. Since military takeovers in Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger, fatalities have surged rather than declined. In Burkina Faso, deaths linked to <mark>extremist violence</mark> have nearly <mark>tripled</mark> in three years, while the state now exercises effective control over a fraction of its territory. The promise that juntas would restore order has given way to siege warfare, mass displacement and deepening isolation.¶ Moreover, Africa’s conflicts are no longer insulated from one another. Fighters, weapons, tactics and financing now move across regions with frightening speed. 1NC Agee [Ryan Agee; senior US military officer with 25 years in national security and special operations, 10-2-2025, "China’s Closing Window: Strategic Compression and the Risk of Crisis", Foreign Policy Research Institute, https://www.fpri.org/article/2025/10/chinas-closing-window-strategic-compression-and-the-risk-of-crisis/] mac
Compression is the pressure Beijing places upon itself; friction is the pressure others apply upon Beijing. Strategic compression is best understood as the simultaneous narrowing of options and acceleration of timelines that confront states when long-term ambitions collide with structural constraints. The concept is related to the classic “windows of vulnerability” debate in strategic studies. Lebow argued that leaders exploit perceived openings when adversaries are temporarily weak. Later scholars extended the idea: Doeser and Eidenfalk posited that foreign policy change often requires <mark>leaders</mark> to <mark>perceive a fleeting</mark> “window of <mark>opportunity</mark>” as both real and urgent. More recently, Hal Brands has tied these windows directly to great-power competition, warning that compressed states <mark>may lunge</mark> an advantage <mark>before their window</mark> of opportunity <mark>closes</mark>.
China today is a textbook case. Its <mark>demographic collapse</mark> (fertility below 1.1, <mark>shrinking labor force</mark>, <mark>and surging pension obligations</mark>) <mark>erode</mark>s both <mark>economic and military potential</mark>. Its economic stagnation (<mark>youth unemployment</mark> above 20 percent, <mark>debt-to</mark>–gross domestic product (<mark>GDP</mark>) ratios beyond 270 percent, <mark>and growth rates</mark> far lower than official statistics claim) <mark>undermine</mark>s <mark>the CCP’s claim to technocratic competence</mark>. And its <mark>political rigidity</mark> (Xi Jinping’s consolidation of power, purges of technocrats, and reliance on loyalty over capability) <mark>reduces</mark> the very <mark>adaptability compression demands.</mark>
<mark>Compression is</mark> both structural and <mark>perceptual</mark>, but the latter is the primary force. <mark>Leaders act not on perfect information but</mark> on <mark>how they read</mark> the <mark>trajectory</mark> of time. <mark>In Beijing,</mark> the perception is that <mark>delay is fatal</mark>: the path to “rejuvenation” risks being blocked by demographics, debt, and dissent. The <mark>2025 white paper</mark>, China’s National Security in the New Era, <mark>makes this explicit</mark> by subordinating economic growth to security priorities and framing Taiwan’s annexation as essential to CCP survival.
Strategic compression thus creates a dilemma. <mark>Beijing</mark> can attempt reform, a path of delay, though uncertain and historically resisted. Or it <mark>can gamble that</mark> crisis, even <mark>conflict, offers the only way to break through</mark> the narrowing window. In either case, time itself has become China’s adversary.
CCP Attributes Under Compression
Demographic and Economic Decline
The <mark>CCP’s legitimacy</mark> has long <mark>rest</mark>ed <mark>on</mark> two pillars: <mark>rising prosperity and national rejuvenation</mark>. <mark>Both are faltering.</mark> <mark>China’s demographic decline is accelerating faster than</mark> even <mark>pessimistic forecasts predicted</mark>. Fertility rates have fallen below 1.1, population peaked in 2022, and the working-age cohort is shrinking by millions annually. The consequences ripple across the system: labor shortages, surging pension obligations, and a contracting pool for military recruitment.
Economic performance tells a similar story. While official figures continue to advertise ~5 percent GDP growth, independent estimates suggest real growth is half that. Youth unemployment (officially “suspended” from publication in 2023 after surpassing 20 percent) reflects a generation losing faith in the promise of upward mobility. Debt-to-GDP ratios above 180 percent, and likely closer to 300 percent by some estimates, reveal an economy built on fictitious capital and financial engineering. Foreign investment, once the backbone of China’s integration into global markets, has collapsed. These are not cyclical dips but structural stall points of a peaking great power.
