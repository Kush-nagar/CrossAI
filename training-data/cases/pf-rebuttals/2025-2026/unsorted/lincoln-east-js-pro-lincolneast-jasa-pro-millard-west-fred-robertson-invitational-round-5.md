I really wish the BBC — and others — would stop using the term “back door” when referring to what law enforcement or the security services
say they want in relation to access to data in an end-to-end, strongly encrypted (E2EE) environment. Apple’s ADP service is a contemporary case
in point.
We’re told in E2EE environments only the end users have the decryption keys. Not the platforms themselves. So even if they wanted to the
platforms which deploy E2EE could not comply with a data request howsoever or wheresoever it came from.
Before moving on, let us just remind ourselves that no international law or treaty of any kind, and no country’s national
laws anywhere in the world, has ever declared privacy to be an absolute right. It is always described as a qualified
right, often described as a “fundamental” right, but never an absolute right. E2EE threatens to make it one.
Who voted for that?
Nobody.
Ever.
OK. So, “back door” ?
It is misleading and unhelpful. I believe intentionally so.
Why? A number of civil society groups and commercial entities opposed to what law enforcement and the security agencies want have rightly
concluded the term “back door” conjures up dark and worrying scenarios.
Hidden forces. Undisclosed activity or agendas. That’s precisely why the BBC and other responsible outlets should not recycle it so readily and
uncritically. Because that is not what is going on here.
We saw something similar in the EU when the Pirate Party people came up with “Chat Control” to describe a range of measures proposed by the
European Commission. These were designed to target online paedophile behaviour and identify child sexual abuse material being distributed
over the internet.
“Chat Control” was baloney, but it trips off the tongue easily, it’s catchy, it sounds scary, it stuck and continues to do a great deal of harm. The
Commission’s proposals have been mired in bureaucratic delay for several years now.
Crooks creep in
Returning to the UK. Using similar extravagant language moves are afoot to box our politcians into a corner.
Crooks creep in through “back doors”. What the police and security agencies want is not a sneaky, underhanded
way to bypass systems without anybody noticing or being able to find out they had been there or what they got up to when they were.
They are asking for “lawful access” — a process that is tightly regulated, very specific, and accountable at the
highest levels. Check out the Investigatory Powers Act 2016.
If UK police or intelligence services want access to encrypted data now, and it would remain the same with Apple-esque E2EE stuff,
they must obtain a warrant. To get the warrant they have to jump through some of the toughest legal
and procedural hoops in the world. The request for the warrant must be:
Precisely targeted and based on compelling evidence;
Approved by the Home Secretary; and
Signed off by a senior judge as part of a “double lock” safeguard.

In some cases, there’s even an additional layer of oversight to ensure these powers aren’t misused or applied too broadly.
The entire system is subject to ongoing review by the Investigatory Powers Commissioner’s Office (IPCO), which audits how these powers are
used in practice.
Moreover, if anyone — an individual, a company, or a campaign group — thinks they’ve been unlawfully
targeted or subjected to surveillance, they can take their case to the Investigatory Powers Tribunal. This
fully independent judicial body is chaired by Lord Justice Singh, one of the country’s most senior judges. The Tribunal can investigate complaints,
hold open or closed hearings, and, where necessary, order remedies — including compensation or the destruction of wrongly obtained data.
Legal Aid is available.

urgent need for comprehensive legal and technological responses. In the twenty-first century, as the world becomes more digitally
interconnected, cyber terrorism has emerged as one of the most insidious threats to global security. Unlike conventional forms of terrorism that
rely on physical violence, cyber terrorism leverages the power of technology to disrupt critical infrastructure, steal sensitive information, and
instigate fear through digital channels (Choraś et al., Citation2016). Two key technological developments namely encryption and the dark web,

cyber terrorists with an anonymous platform to operate, trade illicit goods, and organize attacks without fear of detection (Liggett et al.,
Citation2019). Its decentralized nature and the use of encrypted communications make it particularly difficult for authorities to track criminal
activity, creating a significant gap in law enforcement’s ability to enforce the law. In this context, India faces unique legal challenges as it tries to
navigate the intersection of cyber security, privacy rights, and counterterrorism efforts.
