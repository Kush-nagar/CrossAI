James Holmes 24. Ph.D. in International Security Studies, Tufts University, J. C. Wylie Chair of Maritime Strategy at the Naval War College. May 2. “A Declining China Is a Dangerous China.” National Interest. https://nationalinterest.org/blog/buzz/declining-china-dangerous-china-210861. $P
Why not go for it now if now is as good as it gets?
And indeed, <mark>now-or-never logic</mark> is <mark>common</mark>place <mark>in</mark> the annals of military history, including East <mark>Asia</mark>n history. Imperial Japan was especially prone to it. This was an island state that was renowned for excellence at arms but lacked the natural-resource base to overcome a mobilized foe such as Russia or America. Accordingly, opportunism was a watchword for Japanese foreign policy and strategy. Tokyo acted when it held a temporary advantage.
Two cases stand out in modern Japanese history. The Imperial Japanese Navy struck first during the Russo-Japanese War of 1904-1905, executing a surprise raid against the Russian Navy’s Pacific squadron at Port Arthur, on the Liaotung Peninsula in north China. Japan’s navy struck again in 1941, deploying its carrier fleet to pummel the U.S. Pacific Fleet battle line at Pearl Harbor. In both conflicts Japanese leaders saw an opponent boasting vastly greater military potential bestir itself, converting natural resources and taxpayer largesse into actionable naval and military power. Given time it would build up insuperable armed might. Japan would lose out.
As a first mover, by contrast, Japan commanded an edge in military power in being at the onset of the Russo-Japanese War and the Pacific War. In both cases Tokyo chose to land the first blow, lashing out before declaring war. Japanese leaders’ calculus: opportunity was fleeting, and Japan must act before its moment passed—never to return.
And this made strategic sense. The notion that adverse trends—not just military but <mark>economic, demographic, you name it—might spur aggression</mark> finds sanction in the classics of strategy. Martial sage Carl von Clausewitz, to name one learned commentator, confirms this possibility. <mark>History</mark>, he points out, <mark>shows</mark> that <mark>weaker belligerents</mark> pick <mark>fight</mark>s with the strong under certain circumstances. “<mark>Supposing</mark> that a minor state is in conflict with a much more powerful one and expects <mark>its position</mark> to <mark>grow weaker</mark> every year,” he writes. “<mark>If war</mark> is <mark>unavoidable</mark>, should it not <mark>make the most of </mark>its <mark>opportunities</mark> before its position gets still worse?”
National leaders, then, may initiate <mark>fight</mark>ing <mark>if </mark>convinced the <mark>trendlines</mark> are <mark>turn</mark>ing hostile to their aspirations. Their relative position might be deteriorating because the nation is faltering domestically, because rivals are prospering, or—the nightmare scenario—both. If Communist Party leaders believe time will consign China to a weaker and weaker position relative to opponents such as the United States and its allies, they may resolve to seize the day.
In other words, <mark>a China in</mark> actual or <mark>perceived decline is</mark> a <mark>dangerous</mark> China.
Politics and warfare, it turns out, are messy affairs. That’s why I have never been a fan of the “Thucydides Trap,” Professor Graham Allison’s account of the dynamics between rising and declining powers. In Allison’s telling both rising and declining contenders are tempted to strike as the power on the make approaches the crossover point beyond which it is stronger than the erstwhile hegemon. To oversimplify a trifle, Allison’s thesis holds that the contender in decline may opt for war before its margin of superiority dwindles to zero, while the challenger on the ascent may opt for war to hasten its emergence or to parry its rival’s preemptive assault.
This, of course, is a riff on Thucydides’ classic History of the Peloponnesian War, which chronicles the war that roiled Greek antiquity during the fifth century BC. Or rather, a riff on one passage out of his treatise. The historian is meditating on the causes of war between rival leagues headed by the city-states of Athens and Sparta. He posits that the “real cause” of war, the most basic one, was “the one which was formally most kept out of sight.” Thucydides’ hypothesis: “The growth of the power of Athens, and the alarm which this inspired in Sparta, made war inevitable.”
Get that? The rise of an ambitious, powerful new challenger stokes fear on the part of the established power, making war inevitable. That is certainly one possibility. But think about it. That was how Thucydides interpreted the motives that goaded two specific combatants, Sparta and Athens, into the maelstrom. He produces no quotations from leaders or rank-and-file citizens on either side to back up his hypothesis—this despite recounting speeches by the dozen, and despite confessing that he sometimes made the speakers say what he thought they ought to have said. His omission is telling.
Nor does the historian announce that he intends to enunciate a universal law of international politics and warfare, even assuming he gets the Greek combatants’ hidden motives right.
But my main gripe is that the Thucydides Trap feels too linear, too mechanical, too pat. It feels like an attempt to reduce interactions between human societies to math. Sure, you might be able to graph two contenders’ relative power, and sure, in some instances you could gauge when the challenger will eclipse the established power. Making the effort might prove helpful. But think back to freshman calculus. Gradients change as curves wend their way along in time. Inflection points, maxima, and minima come and go. Fortunes reverse, and reverse again.
Seldom do human affairs track along smooth, continuous, straight-line projections from the past into the future. In fact, Clausewitz upbraids commentators who neglect “to take adequate account of the endless complexities involved” in war. “As we have seen, the conduct of war branches out in almost all directions and has no definite limits; while any system, any model, has the finite nature of a synthesis.” There’s a fractal nature to strategic competition and warfare.
Take it from a forefather of complexity theory.
So much for parables about rise and fall. Think about alternative possibilities. The challenger could top out before reaching the crossover point that heralds its primacy. The established power could regain its dynamism, restoring or expanding its relative superiority even if the challenger continues to surge. Or both contenders could decline, and the balance between them would depend on who decayed faster. Or discontinuities happen. Something could happen to one or both curves, interrupting the trendlines to unpredictable, black-swan effect. For example, a third challenger could arise, surpassing both competitors or giving rise to a triangular contest for supremacy. Etc.
Bottom line, the Thucydides Trap projects one possible competitive dynamic in a universe of alternative futures.
Back to Admiral Aquilino’s diagnosis of what ails China and could spur aggression. It could be that China has started its descent without ever overtaking U.S. power. Even if the United States has fallen into decline in absolute economic and military terms, China may have crested early and plunged into yet steeper decline. In that case, the margin between the contestants would widen even if both countries were on the wane. If that’s how <mark>Xi </mark>Jinping & Co. size things up, they might <mark>order the P</mark>eople’s <mark>L</mark>iberation <mark>A</mark>rmy <mark>into action</mark> while China stands its best chance of success. There is ample precedent.
With apologies to Thucydides, maybe we should rejigger his dictum to fit the U.S.-China competition: “The withering of the power of China, and the alarm which this inspired in Beijing, made war inevitable.”

Prioritizing Economic Development
Diversionary war theory posits that incompetent leaders tend to invoke foreign policy threats to divert public attention from their domestic turmoil.28 Some China analysts express concerns that during times of economic difficulties, China may engage in war-prone diplomatic assertiveness as a means to distract from domestic turmoil and rally popular support. As foreign policy experts Michael <mark>Beckley</mark> and Hal Brands write, “Rising powers become most aggressive when their fortunes fade, their difficulties multiply, and they realize they must achieve their ambitions now or miss the chance to do so forever. China has already started down this path.”29 However, upon closer examination, these claims <mark>may not hold true in</mark> the case of <mark>China, </mark>which has<mark> consistently reacted</mark> to domestic turmoil <mark>by prioritizing cooperative economic development rather than</mark> nationalist territorial <mark>expansion.</mark>
Echoes of History
Since the 1950s, according to MIT expert M. Taylor Fravel, Chinese <mark>leaders “have encountered strong</mark> domestic <mark>incentives to engage in diversionary crises</mark> or wars <mark>but almost never do</mark> so.<mark>”</mark>30 On territorial disputes, he argues, domestic <mark>insecurity creates stronger incentives</mark> for leaders <mark>to compromise</mark> with competing territorial claimants <mark>so</mark> that <mark>Beijing can</mark> secure the borders and concentrate resources to <mark>address internal challenges</mark> to its regime security.31 Remarkably, during Mao’s era, China successfully resolved most of its border disputes with neighboring countries.
In the late 1970s, <mark>when the</mark> Chinese <mark>economy was on</mark> the <mark>brink of collapse,</mark> Deng <mark>Xiaoping introduced a</mark> <mark>strategic approach</mark> aimed at resolving territorial disputes in the South China Sea and East China Sea. He proposed <mark>shelving</mark> these <mark>disputes and fostering friendly relations with</mark> Southeast Asian <mark>countries</mark> and Japan. Additionally, Deng also advocated for the innovative principle of “One Country, Two Systems,” which allowed Hong Kong and Macau to retain their capitalist economies and way of life, initially facilitating their smooth transition back to Chinese sovereignty. Deng and his successor, Jiang Zemin, continued the pursuit of friendly and transactional relations with Taiwan under the framework of the “One China, Different Interpretations,” often referred to as the 1992 Consensus. This consensus acknowledges that there is only one “China,” but recognizes the differing interpretations of what “China” represents across the Taiwan Strait (i.e., the Republic of China vs. the People’s Republic of China). The Chinese leadership’s efforts to resolve or shelve these disputes were driven by a well-organized objective: creating a friendly international environment that is essential for domestic development.
China’s Diaoyu/Senkaku policy is indeed a case that illustrates its prioritization of economic development over accommodating nationalism in territorial disputes. Accordingly, Beijing has played down several crises caused by Chinese and Japanese nationalists’ moves to assert sovereignty claims. For instance, in September 1996, David Chan, a Hong Kong (HK) Baodiao activist, drowned while attempting to swim to the islands, which led to a large-scale Baodiao movement in HK. HK citizens and politicians pressured Beijing to stand firm against Japan’s “new invasion” of Chinese territories. Beijing had strong incentives to accommodate, as HK’s collective expressions of patriotism were unprecedented, and a stable transfer of HK sovereignty back to China, scheduled in 1997, might have been jeopardized if Beijing failed to address HK citizens’ concerns. However, Beijing’s response was limited to diplomatic protests and a naval exercise allegedly to warn Japan.32
The major rationale behind Chinese behaviors during this period was China’s need for Japanese aid and foreign direct investment (FDI) to boost its industrial development. In 1978, Deng and his colleagues recognized the importance of introducing foreign equipment and technologies to enhance China’s industrial productivity. However, China’s foreign reserves, which stood at a mere $2 billion USD at the time, were insufficient to meet the substantial financial requirements of importing new equipment. Contracts signed with Western countries for equipment imports in 1978 amounted to $6.36 billion USD, creating a significant funding gap. To address this gap, Deng turned to Japan for both private investment and official development assistance (ODA), a decision that proved to be wise. Japan provided substantial loans amounting to ¥303 billion Japanese Yen in 1979 to finance China’s major projects. From 1979 to 2005, Japanese ODA accounted for a remarkable 67.2 percent of all foreign government aid received by China. Notably, Japanese ODA played a critical role in financing China’s basic infrastructure investments, particularly in the 1990s, reaching its peak in 1994 at approximately 27 percent of China’s total budgetary investment on basic infrastructure.33
<mark>China</mark>’s strategy of diversionary peace is rooted in the <mark>understand</mark>ing that economic <mark>development holds</mark> <mark>greater significance for</mark> the CCP’s <mark>legitimacy than</mark> pursuing <mark>assertive foreign policies</mark> to stoke domestic nationalism. <mark>Economic considerations</mark> <mark>outweigh</mark> the appeal of fervent <mark>nationalism, especially during</mark> periods of <mark>economic challenges.</mark> According to Professor Yan Xuetong, China has no intention of antagonizing the West, as “these exports [to the developed economies] are the lifeblood of the Chinese economy: they ensure a consistent trade surplus, and the jobs they create are a vital engine of domestic social stability.”34
China’s Diplomatic Reorientation
Over the past decade, Chinese diplomacy has undergone dramatic transformations, with the rise and subsequent recalibration of “wolf-warrior” diplomacy standing out as a defining phenomenon. Characterized by confrontational rhetoric, public rebukes of foreign governments, and frequent violations of established diplomatic protocol, this assertive style was embraced by a number of Chinese diplomats who sought to project national strength and defend China’s image with unprecedented bluntness. However, this approach drew widespread international criticism and increasingly strained China’s relations with the West.
Recognizing the potential economic and geopolitical costs of such combative diplomacy, Chinese <mark>leadership</mark> <mark>has</mark> gradually <mark>moved to moderate</mark> its diplomatic tone. In June 2021, <mark>Xi</mark> Jinping <mark>emphasized</mark> the importance of “adopting a narrative tone that reflects openness and confidence, yet conveys modesty and <mark>humility</mark>, in a bid to shape a reliable, admirable and respectable image of China.”35 The Wall Street Journal reported that this shift in approach has been <mark>driven by</mark> Beijing’s <mark>apprehension that</mark> its <mark>confrontational</mark> “wolf-warrior” <mark>diplomacy may</mark> exacerbate anti-China sentiment and potentially <mark>isolate the</mark> Chinese <mark>economy</mark>.36
Notably, outspoken and proactive <mark>diplomats known for</mark> their <mark>assertive</mark> wolf warrior <mark>style</mark>, such as former deputy spokesperson of the Chinese Foreign Ministry Zhao Lijian and former ambassador to Sweden Gui Congyou, <mark>have been retired or reassigned.</mark> Since 2022, <mark>China has been actively</mark> seeking to resume official and unofficial exchanges and <mark>trying to fix</mark> its broken <mark>relations</mark> with Europe, Canada, Japan, Australia, and India, and maintain stable working relations with the United States amid strategic competition. Since November 2023, China has unilaterally granted visa-free entry to citizens of forty-three countries including Germany, France, Italy, Australia, and Japan.37 This reflects a strategic effort <mark>to</mark> boost people-to-people exchanges, <mark>revitalize post-COVID economic engagement</mark>, and improve its global image amid rising geopolitical tensions.
China’s new charm offensive is targeting Japan as well. Xi has repeatedly stated his will to enhance China’s relations with Japan and met with Prime Minister Shinzo Abe ten times from 2014 to 2019. At the 2019 Buenos Aires APEC Summit, Xi told then-prime minister Abe that “China continues to welcome Japan to be part of China’s reform and opening up, sharing new opportunities of China’s development.”38 As mentioned, China and Japan have engaged in intensive minister-level meetings and social exchanges. Xi and then-prime minister Kishida’s meeting in November 2023 reaffirmed their commitment to “the comprehensive promotion of a mutually beneficial relationship based on common strategic interests.”39
A major impetus is both states’ willingness to improve their economic ties.40 Kishida and Xi agreed to establish a dialogue framework on trade, and their ministries in charge discussed several key issues such as the stability of the supply chain, export control of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, China’s entry into the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), and the acceptance of new members into the RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership).41 In January 2024, Chinese Premier Li Qiang met with Japanese business leaders, underscoring the significant role of economic and trade cooperation as “the ballast and propeller of bilateral relations,” two words echoing the previous language used to describe China-US relations in China’s official discourse
