PART 1 - SHARED THREATS AND CHALLENGES
The 2020s have been a defining moment for America’s position in the world. The chaotic withdrawal from Afghanistan fuelled perceptions, among allies and adversaries alike, that <mark>U.S.</mark> power had waned and that it might be entering an era of <mark>decline</mark>. Partly <mark>emboldened</mark> by this failure, the Russian President, Vladimir <mark>Putin</mark>, doubted the West’s resolve in supporting Ukraine, and launched a full-scale invasion of the country in February 2022, further undermining the rules-based international order. The October 7 attacks by Hamas on Israel threw the <mark>Middle East</mark> into volatility which the U.S. has, under successive administrations, failed to contain. All the while, in developing countries, democracy and the West are losing ground, with <mark>China</mark> filling the void left by the retreating West and Russia exploiting crises to expand its influence through paramilitary groups in nations like Libya, Mali, the Central African Republic, and Burkina Faso. Most pressingly, for the U.S., China has for over a decade been expanding its power across military, diplomatic, economic, and technological spheres as a challenge to America’s position in the world.
It is possible to identify certain trends in U.S. foreign policy that will, over the coming decades, only become more prominent regardless of the President and which party controls Congress. The U.S.-China competition looks likely to define this century. American grand strategy has been based for decades on the premise that a strong presence across Europe, Asia, and the Middle East would prevent the rise of a hostile power, enhance American wealth, and maintain Washington’s power.16 Yet, successive administrations have aimed to focus predominantly on the Indo-Pacific — an ambition hindered by a series of crises in Europe and the Middle East.
The current administration largely reflects this thinking. When the Secretary of Defense, Pete Hegseth, bluntly told European allies that the U.S. could no longer be the guarantor of European security, it was a realistic assessment of the limits of American power. He said that the U.S. faces “a peer competitor in the Communist Chinese with the capability and intent to threaten our homeland and core national interests in the Indo-Pacific. The U.S. is prioritizing deterring war with China in the Pacific.”17 This aligns with policy documents from previous administrations. Indeed, Joe Biden’s Indo-Pacific strategy outlined that “American interests can only be advanced if we firmly anchor the United States in the Indo-Pacific.”18 Biden’s policy was inherited from President Obama. While a shift to the Indo-Pacific does not necessarily mean an American withdrawal from NATO, it should result in Europe taking greater responsibility for its own security.
Washington will not willingly forego its position as the world’s leading political, economic, military, and technological power. If China does replace the U.S. as the leading world power at some point this century, a China-led world would leave the UK vulnerable to Beijing’s growing hostility. As its extreme nationalist foreign policy, ‘wolf warrior’ diplomacy, repression of ethnic minorities, and sanctioning of British Parliamentarians has shown, the Chinese Communist Party is no friend of the United Kingdom.
While China will be the dominant focus of U.S. foreign policy over the next two decades, other factors will feed into the State Department, Pentagon, and the Oval Office’s thinking. The Kremlin will continue to be vehemently anti-American and present a major foreign policy challenge for the United States. It will look to erode Washington’s role in the world and dominate its neighbours. In Russia’s zero-sum foreign policy, it will continue to regard the U.S. as hostile and take action, whether overt or covert, to lessen America’s influence and security to boost its own. There will be new theatres where the competition between the U.S. and Russia will open up and pose major concerns to policymakers on the Hill, most notably in the Arctic and in developing countries.
Likewise, anti-American movements from both Moscow and Beijing, driven by their soft power, diplomatic, and development efforts, will increase, seeking to erode U.S. alliances, power, influence, and prosperity. The <mark>final ambition</mark> of this movement <mark>is</mark> a <mark>global order antithetical to</mark> the <mark>U.S.</mark>, UK, and other NATO countries. The stability and longevity of the order that comes next depends on legitimacy and the buy-in of developing nations. The rising tide of China’s and Russia’s influence provides legitimacy to their vision and decreases the legitimacy of an order aligned with Western values and interests.

Through an ‘America First’ approach, there has been a deliberate policy over several administrations that foreign policy serves American economic interests. Regardless of the party, the general trend amongst the American electorate is that the President’s priority should be domestic issues. This does not mean a return to isolationism; more that U.S. foreign policy should primarily serve domestic interests. Economic resilience, industrial strength, and technological dominance are inseparable from U.S. national security and will drive the desire to secure resources and build economic partnerships across the world. All of these are also threats and challenges to the UK. China has recently been described by senior defence figures as being part of a “deadly quartet” alongside Russia, Iran, and North Korea that poses an immense security threat to the UK.19 Its technological development, expanding military capabilities, stranglehold of critical minerals and supply chains threaten both national and economic security. Meanwhile, Russia remains the most immediate and pressing threat to the UK, as the Strategic Defence Review and the National Security Strategy conclude, and will be a multifaceted adversary to the UK beyond the war in Ukraine across military, subthreshold, political, and economic dimensions. Like the U.S., the UK’s economy is acutely vulnerable to supply chain disruption, and as the Government seeks to bolster UK defence and manufacturing industries, the exposure of developing countries and supply chains to influence from Russia and China threatens the viability of a UK industrial strategy. Chapter 1 - The Threat from China A quarter of a century ago, there was a widespread belief that China could be ‘Westernised’ and steered towards liberalism. President Clinton, speaking in 2000 about China’s accession to the World Trade Organisation, outlined that while membership in the WTO would not make China liberal overnight, “it will move China faster and further in the right direction.”20 Today, that optimism has vanished. A rare bipartisan consensus has emerged in Washington: China is the United States’ most formidable competitor and the primary threat to its national security and interests. The 2017 National Security Strategy, issued under President Trump’s first administration, stated unequivocally that “China and Russia want to shape a world antithetical to U.S. values and interests. China seeks to displace the United States in the Indo-Pacific region, expand the reaches of its state-driven economic model, and reorder the region in its favor.” It also underscored American fears that Beijing was building the most capable and well-funded military in the world after the U.S., with that gap closing by the day.21 Trump’s successor, Joe Biden, largely continued the firm stance taken on China. Rather than reversing Trump-era policies, his administration expanded them by introducing a variety of economic measures, such as the Inflation Reduction Act. Likewise, a diplomatic strategy was undertaken to solidify U.S.-led alliances in the Indo-Pacific to push back against China’s increasingly aggressive posture. In 2022, President Biden’s National Security Strategy described that China “is the only competitor with both the intent to reshape the international order and, increasingly, the economic, diplomatic, military, and technological power to advance that objective.”22 It was this growing power that the administration saw as harming America’s interests and tilting the global order for the Chinese Communist Party’s (CCP) benefit. The current administration has appointed known China hawks to key positions: Marco Rubio as Secretary of State and Mike Waltz, formerly National Security Advisor and now nominee for Ambassador to the United Nations. Meanwhile, influential think tanks close to the Trump administration, such as the Heritage Foundation, have stated that “a totalitarian Communist dictatorship is engaged in a strategic, cultural, and economic Cold War against America’s interests, values and people.”23 This view has become embedded in the U.S. foreign policy establishment. Both parties agree that the goal is no longer to manage the competition with China but to win it. China, for its part, has undergone a similar transformation in perspective. It previously saw areas of cooperation with Washington, mainly in the realms of counter-terrorism and climate change, but now believes that the two countries are in a protracted competition with each other. Its approach to the competition with the U.S. is holistic, incorporating not just decision-makers in the Politburo but a range of different stakeholders and actors that are being mobilised in this competition, from the People’s Liberation Army (PLA) to businesses and academic institutions.24 Its objective is clear: displace the U.S. at the top of the international system through a combination of economic, technological, military, and diplomatic means. The Science and Tech Competition The science and technological dimensions of America’s competition with China run far deeper than its competition with the Soviet Union during the Cold War. President Biden’s National Security Advisor, Jake Sullivan, argued that the U.S. would pursue a policy of maintaining “as large of a lead as possible” over its adversaries in technology, attempting to keep China behind in strategic technologies with defence applications.25 Meanwhile, President Xi outlined in 2024 that while China had made breakthroughs in strategic technology fields, even becoming the world leader in some, there are weak spots. Therefore, he stated that China “must further enhance its sense of urgency and intensify its efforts … so as to secure a leading position in sci-tech competition and future development.”26 Xi’s remarks are the latest expression of a long-standing ambition of the Chinese Communist Party. Announced in 2015, the ‘Made in China 2025’ plan highlighted his goal of becoming the global leader in critical technology manufacturing and development, including semiconductors, electric vehicles and biotechnology. By 2049, it aims to dominate a broader set of strategic sectors, including AI, quantum, and robotics.27 As one former official at China’s Ministry of Science and Technology stated, “[t]he core of the U.S.-China rivalry is the technology war … Whether the U.S. can defeat China or whether China can rise from adversity will depend on the technology war.”28 In pursuit of this goal, China has rapidly increased its investment in the research and development of new technologies. By 2022, China was reportedly spending over 2.5% of its GDP on research and development (R&D), representing just shy of $650 billion, while the U.S. spent just over $700 billion.29 Due to the opacity of Chinese financial reporting, there is a possibility that its spending is higher than this. Notably, China’s R&D expenditure is mainly state-led, whereas the U.S.’s is mostly driven by the private sector. In quantum technologies, for instance, the Chinese government announced spending of $15.3 billion in 2022, compared to the U.S. government’s $3.7 billion and the UK’s $1.3 billion.30 This centralised, state-led model enables the CCP to more directly align technological investment with its broader strategic competition with the United States and do so faster. Beijing’s investments are paying dividends. According to the Australian Strategic Policy Institute’s (ASPI) Critical Technology Tracker, which assesses global research performance, strategic intent, and potential future science and technology capability across 64 critical technologies, including in defence, computing, cyber, and AI, China has made remarkable progress. Between 2003 and 2007, the U.S. led the world’s innovative research in 60 of the 64 tracked technologies. By contrast, China only led in three. Fast forward to 2019-2023 and the landscape had shifted dramatically. China led in 57 of the 64 technologies, while the U.S. led in just seven.31 This trend underscores the need for the U.S. and allies to expand its innovation efforts – presenting opportunities for deeper collaboration with key allies like the UK. Concerns about the development of Chinese innovation and technology are not localised in the U.S. Sir Alex Younger, the former Chief of MI6, recently stated that China’s mastery of key technologies is a “pacing threat” to the UK and one that is just as important as the threat currently facing the UK from Russia. He warned that the UK needed to stay at the forefront of the development of key emerging technologies, without which the UK’s “security prospects will be pretty dim.”32

The <mark>rapid development of Chinese capabilities</mark> in cyber, computing, and AI, not to mention military capabilities, poses a significant security and economic <mark>challenge</mark> to the UK. In 2003 to 2007, the UK ranked as a top five nation in the ASPI’s tracker for 47 technologies. By 2023, it had fallen to being in the top five for 36. Despite the challenges to its economy, such as high debt, an unstable property market, and a shrinking labour force,34 China will double down on its ambitions to displace the U.S as the world’s leading technological and economic power. Over the next two decades, this ambition will go beyond the economic competition, and translate into a sustained effort to challenge and erode U.S. <mark>military superiority</mark>. Much of China’s R&D spending on new technologies is geared towards military development.
Concern about Chinese Capabilities
Washington has grown increasingly alarmed by the expansion of China’s military capabilities – as have its Five Eyes allies.35 In its latest annual threat assessment, the U.S. intelligence community described China as “the most comprehensive and robust military threat to U.S. national security.”36 As part of its push to build a world-class military, China has complemented its advances in defence technology with rapid increases in defence spending and the size of its armed forces. Although Beijing is not necessarily seeking an open war with Washington, its military build-up signals a clear intent to close the gap to the U.S. The U.S. will struggle to match China numerically and the disparities are expected to grow. In just four years, the People’s Liberation Army Navy (PLAN) has expanded by the equivalent of the entire Royal Navy and now constitutes the largest navy in the world.37 Similarly, China has a shipbuilding capacity 230 times that of the United States.38
China’s primary intention for its rapid military build-up is to deter, and if necessary defeat, U.S. intervention in the Indo-Pacific. Previously, China was reluctant to display belligerence in the Indo-Pacific due to the capability gap with the U.S. This may help to explain why the CCP has not yet launched an invasion of Taiwan, opting instead for economic coercion and an increase in military exercises in the Taiwan Strait. However, as its capabilities have gradually caught up with Washington, <mark>Beijing</mark> has <mark>felt greater freedom</mark> to <mark>use assertiveness</mark> and aggression in pursuit of its aims.39
Belligerence over Taiwan <mark>risks</mark> a <mark>conflict</mark> with catastrophic global consequences – both in terms of human casualties and economic disruption. Such a conflict could very easily spill into other nations and regions. The most effective means of deterring Chinese aggression is by making it infeasible or too costly to pursue. That entails not only sufficient stockpiles of munitions and equipment – which war games conducted by the Center for Strategic and International Studies suggest would be exhausted within weeks – but also superiority in new military technologies.40
One of America’s enduring military advantages is its ability to project power globally. As of 2021, the U.S. had overseas military bases in at least 80 countries – a figure that has doubled since the Cold War. China, conversely, only has one overseas military base, located in Djibouti – only a short distance from Camp Lemonnier, a U.S. base. However, American intelligence outlines that the Chinese military intends to expand its global footprint in the decades ahead, describing that it “will continue to pursue the establishment of overseas military installations and access agreements to project power and protect China’s interests abroad.”42 It also suggests that China is looking to exploit its global infrastructure projects through the Belt and Road Initiative for military purposes.
Officers in the U.S. Navy have warned that since the Second World War, no power has come close to threatening U.S. command of the world’s oceans, “but China is poised to do so within the next several decades.”43 An expansion of Chinese overseas military bases could pose a threat to America’s military position. A greater number of PLAN overseas military installations – which in many ways depends on its diplomatic and development strategy – whether in Asia, Africa or Latin America and the Caribbean, can be used to collect vast amounts of intelligence, disrupt U.S. shipments in times of crisis and resupply its navy with weapons, ammunition, and equipment to challenge America’s control of the sea.44
Whilst China does not pose an immediate military threat to the UK like Russia does, it is widely recognised by defence and security officials that Beijing is a more long-term security threat to the UK, despite the reluctance of the Government to label it as such. The first reason for this is the use of sub-threshold activities, including cyber attacks, espionage, information warfare, and economic attacks. Following a series of cyber attacks on UK companies, government departments, critical infrastructure, and politicians, the UK’s National Cyber Security Centre named China as the “pacing threat in the cyber realm.”45 Second is the development of global power projection capabilities as outlined above. The NATO strategic concept in 2022 outlined that China poses a challenge not simply to the security of the Indo-Pacific but also the Euro-Atlantic due to the use of its political, economic, and military tools.46 Already, Beijing has been accused of conducting sub-threshold activities against European NATO members. The damage to undersea infrastructure in the Baltic Sea by Chinese vessels has demonstrated that the UK and European NATO members should not only increase capabilities and spending to counter Russia, but also China.
The ‘No Limits Partnership’
The U.S. currently has an advantage in its network of global alliances. For decades, U.S. alliances across Europe, Africa, and Asia have given it a decisive edge over adv˜ersaries. Its allies have supported the U.S. militarily, provided vital intelligence, and allowed unrivalled global power projection. China may have grown in power, but it has not generated comprehensive partnerships that have significantly boosted its geopolitical position.
However, the <mark>growing alignment between China, Russia, Iran, and No</mark>rth <mark>Ko</mark>rea <mark>presents</mark> a new and evolving <mark>challenge</mark> to U.S. security and interests. Though not a formal alliance, the relationships between these states have strengthened their capabilities and improved resilience.47 The deepening cooperation between Beijing and Moscow is the most concerning. Beyond the announcement of a ‘no limits’ partnership between the two countries, just before Russia’s illegal invasion of Ukraine, the two nations have been explicitly aligning themselves with one another to foster a global order in their image and support their shared objectives. China has provided considerable economic assistance to Russia since the start of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine, including exporting components used in weapons, and has improved Russian capabilities in autonomous, missile, and cyber systems.48 It has propped up the Russian economy by increasing imports of oil and gas and has supported Russia in conducting financial transactions in the face of sanctions.49
The deepening of China-Russia cooperation extends beyond the economic sphere. Over the past decade, the two countries have <mark>increased</mark> the number of <mark>joint military exercises</mark>, including in the Mediterranean.50 The Arctic, as will be discussed later, has the potential to be another area of increased China-Russia cooperation that could have severe consequences for both the U.S. and the UK. Immediately after the inauguration of President Trump, a conversation was held between Xi and Putin, firming up their support for one another, safeguarding the interests of the two countries, and suggesting that the two nations have far greater military ties over the coming years and decades, demonstrating the growing indivisibility of the Indo-Pacific and Euro-Atlantic.51
The growing cooperation between Russia and China, and to a lesser extent Iran and North Korea, has several consequences for the defence and security of the U.S. and UK. First, there is the higher potential of cyber attacks and espionage activity from both nations, with chances of coordinated sub-threshold activities. Already, it is widely believed that Moscow and Beijing are aligning cyber activities against the West with shared tactics and joint operations. Indeed, The Times reported in April 2022 that China-based hackers launched a cyber attack on Ukraine’s military facilities just before the full-scale invasion.52 Second, China’s investments in R&D for military technologies, as well as its rapid build-up of capabilities, can help to support and bolster Russia’s military capabilities through information and technology exchanges and exports of defence products. Third, their campaigns to undermine the West via developing countries has the potential to harm Western global power projection and create a world more permissive of Russian and Chinese aggression.
Chapter 2 - A Resurgent Russia
Moscow’s confrontation with the West and the U.S. will not disappear. Despite President Obama’s dismissal of Russia as a ‘regional power’, its elites envision a multipolar world in which it stands as one of the three dominant powers, alongside the U.S. and China, and will undertake military, economic, diplomatic and technological strategies to that end.53
There have been fears that after the war in Ukraine, Russia may look to confront NATO directly within a decade. Danish intelligence has warned that <mark>if Russia sees NATO as weak</mark>, it <mark>could</mark> be ready to <mark>wage</mark> a large-scale <mark>war</mark> with<mark>in</mark> five <mark>years</mark>.54 Meanwhile, General Sir Roly Walker, the UK’s Chief of the General Staff, has remarked that Vladimir Putin will emerge from the war in Ukraine “wanting retribution” against Kyiv’s allies.55 Whilst a direct attack by Russia on a NATO member is by no means a certainty, there is little doubt that Moscow will look to, reconstruct its military, and modernise both nuclear and conventional capabilities.
This is not lost on the foreign policy establishment in Washington. A similar stance persists in the U.S. State Department, among national security advisors, and across think tanks. Indeed, the U.S. intelligence community’s annual threat assessment for 2025 stated that “Russia’s current geopolitical, economic, military, and domestic political <mark>trends underscore</mark> its <mark>resilience</mark> and enduring potential threat to U.S. power, presence, and global interests,” adding that “Vladimir <mark>Putin</mark> appears <mark>resolved</mark> and prepared to pay a very high price to prevail in what he sees as a defining time in Russia’s strategic competition with the United States.”56
Russia’s Resilient Capabilities
Like previous wars, Russia has shown its ability to endure staggering losses. The International Institute for Strategic Studies has estimated that by January 2025, a minimum of 172,000 Russian troops had been killed in Ukraine and 611,000 wounded.57 Although many Russian troops in Ukraine are without substantial training or equipment, there remains concern in the U.S. Army about Russia’s ability to rearm and reconstitute its military.
Despite embarrassing setbacks to the Black Sea fleet, the Russian Northern and Pacific fleets have seen limited complications. Since the start of the fullscale invasion of Ukraine, <mark>Russia’s</mark> Northern and Pacific <mark>fleets</mark> have been almost continuously <mark>conducting</mark> global deployments and <mark>exercises</mark>, often alongside the PLAN. The Russian Navy has not been afraid to antagonise the UK and U.S. It has conducted drills around Britain, including in the English Channel and has spent time in the Atlantic.58 In June 2024, the U.S. and Canada dispatched vessels to track Russian warships and nuclear submarines 25 miles off the coast of Florida as they moved towards Cuba. Russian state media claimed that its presence was to “show the flag and ensure a naval presence in operationally important areas of the far ocean zone.”59
Its surface fleet has become less of a priority to the Kremlin than its submarine fleet, particularly its nuclear-powered submarines. Nikolai Tevmenov, the former Commander-in-Chief of the Russian Navy, stated in 2023 that the Russian Navy “will continue to pay priority attention to the development of the nuclear submarine component of the Submarine Forces, which are the basis for guaranteed state security in maritime and ocean areas.”60
Despite the ocean between the U.S. and Europe, Russia will continue to be a threat. This danger stems from its capabilities in long-range missile strikes, cyber warfare, and a broad array of technological advancements. Of particular concern is the sub-threshold threat.

Adversaries in the Arctic Another matter of concern is Russia’s role in the Arctic. There is another rare bipartisan consensus in Washington that America needs to be paying closer attention to the region, both due to the opportunities and the strategic challenges it presents. As sea ice recedes, the region is becoming more accessible. This can reduce shipping times between Europe and Asia by as much as 50%. Likewise, there are enormous energy reserves in the Arctic, with the region holding an estimated 13% of the world’s undiscovered conventional oil resources and 30% of its undiscovered conventional natural gas resources, as well as substantial critical mineral reserves.66 Once seen as a zone of cooperation insulated from geopolitics, the Arctic has emerged as a frontier of great power competition. The U.S. Coast Guard during the first Trump administration argued “[t]he Arctic’s role in geostrategic competition is growing,” warning that U.S. adversaries are using the Arctic to conduct their activities “under a cloud of ambiguity that makes it difficult to mount an effective or timely response.”67 In 2024, the Biden administration released an Arctic strategy, which outlined that the region “is critical to the defense of our homeland, the protection of U.S. national sovereignty, and our defense treaty commitments.”68 Interest in the Arctic spiked further following President Trump’s suggestion that the U.S. acquire Greenland for national security reasons. Access to, and dominance of, the Arctic is a central consideration in Russia’s global strategy. Russian leaders have spoken extensively about the need for Russia to increase its military presence in the Arctic. In 2022, Sergei Shoigu, then the Defence Minister, declared that Russia needed to “create an appropriate grouping of troops in Northwest Russia.”69 Although Moscow has struggled to have a large army presence in the Arctic due to the focus on Ukraine, its Northern Fleet – making up two-thirds of Russia’s nuclear strike capabilities – has been largely unaffected.70 Even before the war in Ukraine, the Kremlin outlined its intention to upgrade its Northern Fleet with new anti-ship cruise missiles and an expanded fleet of nuclear-powered submarines as part of a goal to phase NATO out of the Arctic.71 According to the Council on Geostrategy, the total missile firepower of Russia’s Northern Fleet comes to over 800 missiles, with 720 of these from submarines.72 Tensions between autocracies and the West in the Arctic are likely to intensify. The region acts as a first line of defence for the U.S., particularly through its partnerships with Canada and Northern European nations. NORAD, for example, which is a joint U.S.-Canada military command, is responsible for early warning systems and the protection of North America and has recently tracked Russian and Chinese aircraft around Alaska.73 The Arctic is an area of deepening China-Russia cooperation. Due to the strains of the war in Ukraine, Russia has sought a closer partnership with China in the Arctic to offset NATO countries’ perceived advantages.74 In 2018, China described itself as a ‘near-Arctic’ power and is now starting to match Russian interest in the region. While Beijing cites scientific research and economic development as its primary motivations, there is an unmistakable strategic dimension to its ambitions. The Arctic represents the shortest missile flight trajectory between China and the U.S., making it crucial for any potential conflict.75 There are also fears in Washington that submarines passing through the Arctic and the Greenland Iceland-UK Gap undermine NATO’s ability to prevent Russian and Chinese infiltration of NATO waters. Such movements could undermine NATO navies, transatlantic shipping, and critical undersea cables, whilst also allowing U.S. adversaries better intelligence. In response to rising collaboration between Russia and China and due to the gaps in its Arctic capabilities, the U.S. has been looking to strengthen collaboration with NATO allies in the region. One glaring disparity is in icebreakers. Russia far exceeds the U.S. in terms of icebreakers and ice-capable patrol ships, causing serious concern in Washington. These vessels are essential for asserting an Arctic presence, enabling scientific research, facilitating trade, and supporting military operations throughout the year. To address this shortfall, the U.S., along with Canada and Finland, signed ICE Pact to produce polar icebreakers and allow each member to access new equipment and capabilities in the region, as well as creating a more competitive shipbuilding industry. U.S. officials have stated that this arrangement is a “message to Russia and China that the United States and its allies intend to … doggedly pursue collaboration on industrial policy to increase our competitive edge in strategic industries.”77 Despite recent tensions between Washington and Ottawa, this arrangement is likely to continue unscathed as the U.S. is in desperate need of these icebreakers and an increase in its Arctic capabilities. There is a strong willingness from the U.S. to strengthen collaboration with NATO allies in Arctic security to counter the activities of Russia and China in the region, including with the UK. Over the next decades, the threat from Russia will not disappear, even as the threat to the U.S. from China intensifies. It is committed to military modernisation, will look to destabilise the West through covert activities, and will attempt to take steps to challenge and erode NATO’s maritime role. Furthermore, like China, it is turning to developing countries to undermine Western influence.
