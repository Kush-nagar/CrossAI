[T] Backdoors are vulnerable.
Paul Walsh 25, 7-23-2025, “Why “Lawful Access” For Encrypted Communications Is Just a Back Door with Better PR,” https://paul-walsh.medium.com/why-lawful-access-for-encrypted-communications-is-just-a-back-door-with-better-pr-7d794835b7e4, accessed 10-01-2025]~
In a recent blog post, child safety advocate John Carr argued that governments should have “lawful access” to end-to-end encrypted messages. He criticised the media for using the term “back door” and claimed it unfairly stokes fear. Carr believes strong encryption without government access is irresponsible and even dangerous. I disagree.
Carr’s argument assumes that <mark>encryption</mark> can be <mark>designed</mark> to work <mark>differently</mark> depending <mark>on who is using it.</mark> That’s <mark>not how</mark> maths or <mark>software works</mark>. <mark>If</mark> you build a <mark>tool</mark> that le<mark>t</mark>s any<mark>o</mark>ne <mark>decrypt</mark> private messages, that tool <mark>exists</mark>. Once it exists, it <mark>introduces</mark> a <mark>vulnerability that can’t be confined to “the good guys.”</mark> No one has invented a form of encryption that selectively fails only when a warrant is present.
Technology <mark>experts</mark> are not <mark>opposing lawful access</mark> because of ideology. Their position is based <mark>on math</mark>ematics. Literally. Once you introduce a flaw into a secure system, that system is no longer secure. You cannot build an exception that works safely for some without exposing everyone to risk. That is why every respected cryptographer has warned against this idea for decades.
Calling it something softer, like lawful access, doesn’t change what it is. <mark>It’s a back door.</mark> It’s a <mark>deliberate weakness</mark> built into a system that is supposed to be secure. Legal <mark>safeguards don’t fix</mark> this. Authoritarian regimes will demand access. Criminals will look for it. If a <mark>door exists, someone will try to walk through</mark> it.
