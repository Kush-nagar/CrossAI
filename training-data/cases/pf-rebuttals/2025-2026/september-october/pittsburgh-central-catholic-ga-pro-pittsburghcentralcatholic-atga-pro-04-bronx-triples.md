A fair procedure unlocks a wealth of physiological benefits. Rejecting it induces negative emotional AND cognitive effects that spill over interpersonally.
Li ’24 [Qi, Xinyu Lai, Ting Li, Kristoffer Hougaard-Madsen, Jing Xiao, Kesong Hu, Chunliang Feng, Di Fu, and Xun Liu; August 15; Associate Professor of Psychology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Ph.D. in Cognitive Neural Science from the Institute of Psychology at the Chinese Academy of Sciences; Ph.D. Student in the Department of Mathematics and Computer Science at the Technical University of Denmark; Researcher in the Institute of Brain and Psychological Sciences at Sichuan Normal University; Professor in the Department of Applied Mathematics and Computer Science at the Technical University of Denmark; Ph.D. in Psychology from Capital Normal University; Assistant Professor of Psychology at the University of Arkansas at Little Rock; Researcher of Neuroscience at South China Normal University; Lecturer in Cognitive Neuroscience at the University of Surrey; Researcher at the Institute of Psychology in Beijing; NeuroImage, “Brain Responses to Self and Other Unfairness Under Resource Distribution Context: Meta-Analysis of FMRI Studies,” vol. 297]
<mark>Unfairness</mark> to self <mark>is</mark> usually <mark>linked with negative</mark> feelings and rejection. The typical response to unfair treatment is negative emotions. According to self-reports from the victims, when given disadvantageous allocation, negative emotional reactions such as <mark>anger, irritation and jealousy</mark> increased, whereas positive affective responses such as pleasure and happiness decreased (Osumi and Ohira, 2009; Xiao and Houser, 2005). In line with these findings, neuroimaging results reported aversion-related areas (Bellucci et al., 2018; Corradi-Dell'Acqua et al., 2016), and the anterior insula (AI) was activated when participants received unfair instead of fair offers (Cheng et al., 2015; Civai et al., 2012; Guroglu et al., 2011; Sanfey et al., 2003).
