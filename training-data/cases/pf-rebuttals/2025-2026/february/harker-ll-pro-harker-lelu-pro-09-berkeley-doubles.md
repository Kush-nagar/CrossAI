1. TURN. Reading history is scientifically positive. It’s a prerequisite to their topical education.
Sharp ‘25 [Tim Sharp; Founder of The Happiness Institute, Forefront of the Positive Psychology Movement; 10-22-2025; "Founder of The Happiness Institute, Forefront of the Positive Psychology Movement"; Dr. Happy; https://drhappy.com.au/2025/10/22/the-psychological-benefits-of-engaging-with-history/; accessed 02-16-2026] leon
One of <mark>the most powerful benefit</mark>s of studying history <mark>is perspective</mark>. <mark>When we learn</mark> about wars, revolutions, pandemics, and social upheavals, <mark>we realise</mark> that <mark>human</mark> being<mark>s</mark> have always <mark>faced uncertainty</mark> and hardship. Psychologists call this cognitive reframing—the ability to reinterpret our circumstances by putting them into a broader context.
For example, when I hear about how people endured the Black Death in the 14th century or rationing during World War II, my modern frustrations feel more manageable. My daily stresses—emails, deadlines, or even the latest news—don’t disappear, but they shrink in comparison. Research shows that this kind of reframing can reduce anxiety and build resilience, as it helps us avoid catastrophising our own challenges.
2. The Comfort of Continuity
<mark>Another benefit is</mark> the feeling of <mark>continuity</mark>. Psychologists describe humans as “temporal beings”—we make sense of our lives by placing ourselves in time. History reminds us that we are part of a long chain of human experience. We’re not alone in our struggles or triumphs.
<mark>This connection to the past provides</mark> what’s called <mark>existential comfort</mark>. <mark>It reassures us that</mark> our <mark>problems</mark>, <mark>while real</mark>, are not unique or insurmountable. Knowing that countless generations before us faced crises, adapted, and carried on <mark>gives us confidence</mark> that <mark>we can too</mark>. In fact, research in positive <mark>psychology suggests</mark> that <mark>feeling connected</mark> to the past can strengthen identity, boost self-esteem, and foster a sense of belonging.
3. Learning from Mistakes—and Successes
<mark>History also feeds</mark> our natural <mark>problem-solving</mark> drive. <mark>By looking at past mistakes</mark> and successes, <mark>we gain</mark> what psychologists call <mark>vicarious learning</mark>. It’s the idea that <mark>we</mark> can <mark>learn indirectly from others’ experiences</mark> without having to live through them ourselves.
For instance, <mark>studying</mark> the <mark>missteps that led to</mark> economic collapses or political <mark>conflict</mark>s <mark>helps us recognise warning signs</mark> in the present. On the flip side, seeing how societies rebuilt, innovated, and created flourishing cultures reminds us of human ingenuity. This not only makes us wiser citizens but also gives us hope: progress is possible, even after setbacks.
4. Awe and the “Small Self” Effect
<mark>Some history is</mark> simply <mark>awe-inspiring</mark>—standing before the pyramids, reading about the Renaissance, or listening to stories of space exploration. <mark>Research</mark> on awe shows that it <mark>triggers the</mark> “<mark>small self</mark>” <mark>effect</mark>: <mark>a</mark> healthy <mark>shrinking of</mark> our <mark>ego as we feel part of something larger</mark>.
