Our results suggest that online poker players were willing to pay a premium to play on sites regulated by state gaming commissions, and that this
premium increased for low-stake players who view regulated sites as less risky. The average micro-stake player was willing to pay a $0.88 per-hour
premium to play on regulated sites and this rate increases to $5.35 for middle-stake players. This finding is consistent with the notion that
regulated sites can provide services, assurances, and/or peace of mind that is
not provided by unregulated sites. The finding that respondents were also willing to pay a premium for sites that employ
services defined as Advanced Security, holding Regulation constant, provides evidence that private certification alleviates problems associated
with asymmetric information and generates value in this market. Our results further supported the notion that financial restrictions
on deposits and withdrawals significantly increase the demand for regulated sites. Micro-stake and
small-stake players who agree that regulation makes it easier to deposit and withdraw funds were willing to pay $0.57 and $1.83 more, respectively, for
government regulation than players who do not agree. The estimated WTP for regulation becomes negative for players who agree that regulation
increases the likelihood that winnings are taxed. Overall, these findings provide support for the notion the amount players are willing to pay for
government regulation is heterogeneous and that differences in attitudes about regulation's effect on site security, risk, tax liability, and payment
convenience affect micro- and small-stake players' WTP for regulation. Across all our results, we find that the moderation effects from consumer
attitudes towards regulation tend to disappear at the middle-stake level. Players at this level are willing to pay a
premium for government regulation, regardless of their subjective views about risk and site security on regulated sites,
withdrawal/deposit convenience, or taxation. It may be the case that there are other benefits viewed by this group from regulation that make it
desirable, such as legitimization of their profession or hobby or broader growth in popularity of the game. Finally, results from our exploratory analysis
provided some indication that part of attitudes towards regulation will be environmental. Canadian residents who have had wider access to regulated
and unregulated markets, generally had the least favorable attitudes towards the benefits of regulation. Individuals in U.S. states with regulated online
poker also viewed regulation less favorably, albeit not to the same levels as the Canadian respondents. 6. Conclusion Online businesses face multiple
challenges in establishing consumer trust. In addition to satisfying perceptions of confidence in the operator's reliability and integrity, they must also
satisfy consumer skepticism over the conduct of other users (Chen et al., 2009). This absence of trust by parties can lead to market failures when
asymmetries in information between different sides of the market result in a failure to transact (Akerlof, 1970). In few markets is the potential lack of
trust more salient than in online poker, where consumers may have distorted views about the product (Fortune & Goodie, 2012) and there is also a
public history of defrauded consumers (McMullan, 2012). Trust in online poker is a multifaceted construct, shaped not only by assurances like secure
deposits or verified randomness, but also by more abstract dimensions of perceived legitimacy. Trust may emerge from a consistent alignment between
player expectations and experienced reality (Schoorman and Mayer, 2007). This alignment transcends any single protective measure and necessarily
relies on a set of interdependent safeguards, some visible and explicit, and others implicit and cultural. For example, on a site with convenient payment
processing but low perceived trust, use
rs may elect to reduce their own risk by only depositing funds to their accounts when needed to play, and otherwise not keep any deposits with
operators.In a regulated market, transparent licensing arrangements, clear dispute
resolution protocols, and demonstrable oversight from third-party auditors lend
weight to the idea that the game operates within a structured framework. Trust is not just
about ensuring that funds are safe or that player data are secure but includes players believing they are participating in a
well-ordered system where fairness is the expected norm. Indirect signals also contribute to user
interpretations of a site's commitment to fair play, including factors like responsiveness of customer support, timeliness of problem resolution, and the
perceived earnestness of communication. This study finds that government regulation can provide benefits beyond
those created by private site-security features. The results from our DCE demonstrated that online poker players
in the U.S. are willing to pay a premium for both Government Regulation and Above-Average Security. Attitudes towards the
benefits of regulation also impact WTP at lower stake levels, with micro- and small-stake players both willing to pay a premium for government
regulation if they hold attitudes that view regulation favorably in terms of its implications for site security, site risk, deposits and withdrawal
convenience, and tax liability.

The online gambling landscape in the US has undergone significant changes in recent years, bringing both liberalization and
regulation. A decision by the Supreme Court made gambling laws a matter for the individual US states, rather than a national issue.
In response, several states have introduced their own laws, allowing people to play casino games or bet on
sports online, in strictly regulated environments. That is the best scenario from the
point of view of the public, because it protects them from shady operators. That is a
very real concern, due to the digital gambling industry being such a lucrative one. There are lots of incentives for people to set up
unlicensed casino sites to capitalize on that. This article will look at how legal frameworks are protecting the
interests of US online casino users. The Protection Offered by US Legal Frameworks The big shift that has
taken place in the online gambling regulations for the US is from the federal to the state level. When it was a federal matter, the main
plank of the law was the Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act, which was passed in 2006. That legislation did not make it
illegal for people to play at online casino sites, but it did make it illegal for the operators of the sites to accept payments. The Act is
still in force, but now it is applied on a state-by-state basis, instead of as a nationwide ruling.

cooperation with state attorneys general (AGs) nationwide and presenting best practices to ensure
continued effective collaboration. The report, directed by the FTC Collaboration Act of 2021, “Working Together
to Protect Consumers: A Study and Recommendations on FTC Collaboration with the State Attorneys General” makes legislative
recommendations that would enhance these efforts, including reinstating the Commission’s authority to seek money for defrauded
consumers and providing it with the independent authority to seek civil penalties. “Today’s consumer protection
challenges require an all-hands-on-deck response, and our report details how the
FTC is working closely with state enforcers to share information, stop fraud, and
ensure fairness in the marketplace,” said Samuel Levine, Director of the Bureau of Consumer Protection. “We look forward to seeking
new opportunities to strengthen these ties and confront the challenges of the future.” In June 2023, the Commission announced a
request for public information (RFI) seeking public comments and suggestions on ways it can work more effectively with state AGs to
help educate consumers about, and protect them from, potential fraud. After reviewing and analyzing the comments received, the
agency developed the report to Congress issued today. The report is divided into three sections: 1) The FTC’s Existing Collaborative
Efforts with State Attorneys General to Prevent, Publicize, and Penalize Frauds and Scams; 2) Recommended Best Practices to
Enhance Collaboration; and 3) Legislative Recommendations to Enhance Collaboration Efforts. The first section lays out the roles and
responsibilities of the FTC and state AGs in protecting consumers from frauds and scams, provides an overview of their respective
law enforcement authority, and discusses how federal and state enforcers share their information and expertise to facilitate effective
communication and cooperation. It also provides a breakdown of the FTC’s structure and a description of the Consumer Sentinel
consumer complaint database, the largest such information-sharing network in the United States. The second section details best
practices used to enhance strong information-sharing between the FTC and its state law enforcement partners, discusses how the
Commission coordinates joint and parallel enforcement actions with state AGs and other state consumer protection agencies, and
presents ideas on expanding the sharing of expertise and technical resources between agencies. Finally, the third section stresses
the legislative need to restore the FTC’s Section 13(b) authority to seek equitable monetary refunds for injured consumers, presents

ways to enhance collaboration and conserve resources by providing the Commission with the independent authority to seek civil
penalties, and describes the agency’s need for clear authority to pursue legal actions against those who assist and facilitate unfair or
