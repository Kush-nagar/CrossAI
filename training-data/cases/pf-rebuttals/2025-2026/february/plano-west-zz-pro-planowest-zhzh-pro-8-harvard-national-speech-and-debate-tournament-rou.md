Regress in judge decision-making procedure is inevitable: judges still have to think and intervene to determine how they evaluate a truth claim or the reasoning of your framework cards. NSDA rules dictate Public Forum should focus on advocacies and generalized, practical solutions derived from the subject of the resolution, not a prescribed set of burdens like truth testing. Prefer it- NSDA rules are the most predictable and the only guidelines functionally constitutive of the activity.

NSDA, “High School Unified Manual 2025-26” Aug. 7 2025, www.speechanddebate.org/high-school-unified-manual/. Accessed 26 Aug. 2025. //CM
Resolution: Specific resolutions for district tournaments held during certain months and the National Tournament topic are available online at www.speechanddebate.org/topics. <mark>Public Forum Debate focuses on advocacy of a position derived from the issues presented in the resolution, not a prescribed set of burdens.</mark> Entries: An entry is comprised of two students from the same school; each debating both sides of the resolution and advancing on its own record. No substitution is permitted once the tournament has begun. Procedure and Order of Speeches: Prior to EVERY round, a coin is tossed virtually or by one of the teams. The team that wins the flip may choose one of two options: EITHER the SIDE of the topic they wish to defend (pro or con) OR the SPEAKING POSITION they wish to have (begin the debate or end the debate). The remaining option (SIDE OR SPEAKING POSITION) is the choice of the team that loses the flip. Once speaking positions and sides has been determined, the debate begins (the con team may lead, depending on the coin flip results). Following the first two constructive speeches, the two debaters who have just given speeches will stand and participate in a three-minute "crossfire". In "crossfire" both debaters "hold the floor." However, the speaker who spoke first must ask the first question. After that question, either debater may question and/or answer at will. At the conclusion of the summary speeches, all four debaters will remain seated and participate in a three-minute "Grand Crossfire” in which all four debaters are allowed to cross-examine one another. The speaker who gave the first summary speech must ask the first question. The speakers from each team will continue to ask and answer questions. Teams should alternate asking and answering questions rather than allowing one team to dominate so that a balance between teams is achieved. All speakers are encouraged to participate in the Grand Crossfire. Speakers should listen respectfully to opponents’ questions and answers. Public Forum Debate Time Limits First Speaker - Team A 4 Minutes First Speaker - Team B 4 Minutes Crossfire 3 Minutes Second Speaker - Team A 4 Minutes Second Speaker - Team B 4 Minutes Crossfire 3 Minutes Summary - First Speaker - Team A 3 Minutes Summary - First Speaker - Team B 3 Minutes Grand Crossfire 3 Minutes Final Focus - Second Speaker - Team A 2 Minutes Final Focus - Second Speaker - Team B 2 Minutes Prep Time 3 Minutes per Team Plans/Counterplans: In Public Forum Debate, the Association defines a plan or counterplan as a formalized, comprehensive proposal for implementation. Neither <mark>the pro or con side</mark> is permitted to offer a plan or counterplan; rather, they <mark>should offer reasoning to support a position of advocacy. Debaters may offer generalized, practical solutions.</mark> Prompting Philosophy: Oral prompting, except time signals, either by the speaker's colleague or by any other person while the debater has the floor, is discouraged though not prohibited and may be penalized by some judges. Debaters may, however, refer to their notes and materials and may consult with their teammate while they do not have the floor and during the Grand Crossfire.

Even if we can’t 100% prove our knowledge claims, we should act based on the best possible information

Friedrich Kratochwil, 2007, Political Sciences @ European University Inst., “Of False Promises and Good Bets: a Plea for a Pragmatic Approach to Theory Building”, Journal of International Relations and Development
Here <mark>pragmatism</mark> seems to hold some promise for several reasons. First, as its most basic level it <mark>does not begin with ‘things’ or</mark> with ‘reason’ or thought, <mark>but with ‘acting’</mark> (prattein), <mark>thereby preventing</mark> some <mark>false starts</mark>. <mark>Even if the mos</mark>t <mark>rigorous</mark> and secure <mark>system of thought turns out not to be contradiction free</mark>, <mark>this</mark> revolutionary <mark>realization does not prevent mathematicians from going on to solve problems and most of us</mark> <mark>have to</mark> <mark>act</mark> most of the time <mark>without having the privilege of basing our decisions on</mark> secure <mark>universally valid knowledge</mark>. Thus, the cure of the anxiety induced by radical doubt consists not of the discovery of absolute certainty, which is a phantasmagorical undertaking that is engendered by its equally fantastic starting point, since nobody begins with universal doubt (e.g. Pierce 1868/1997)! Rather, the remedy for this anxiety consists of the recognition of the unproductive nature of universal doubt. Letting go of unrealizable plans and notions that lead us down the road to delusional projects and <mark>acquiring</mark> instead <mark>the ability to ‘go on’</mark> <mark>in spite of</mark> <mark>uncertainties</mark> and the unknown <mark>is</mark> probably <mark>the most valuable lesson to learn</mark>. Second, <mark>by giving up</mark> on <mark>the idea that warranted knowledge is generated through</mark> either <mark>logical demonstration or</mark> the <mark>representation</mark> of the world ‘out there’, <mark>a pragmatic starting point</mark> not only takes the always preliminary character of scientific knowledge seriously but it <mark>also accounts for cumulative knowledge in a more coherent fashion</mark>. <mark>If the world ‘out there’ were</mark> ready <mark>made only to be discovered, then scientific knowledge would have to be a simple accumulation of</mark> more and more true <mark>facts</mark>, <mark>leading</mark> us virtually automatically closer and <mark>closer to ‘the Truth’</mark> conceived as the totality of all true statements. Here Popper’s (1972) ‘Third World’ comes to mind and his first interpretation of scientific progress as the self-correcting process of conjectures and refutations. But as the history of science has suggested, scientific progress was characterized by conceptual revolutions and not only ‘normal’ science, quite aside from the embarrassing problem of what to do with all those parts of the ‘third world’ that turned out to be false after all, such as the indivisibility of atoms, ether, phlogiston, or what have you. Similarly misleading is the imagery of scientific progress as an ever closer approximation to ‘the Truth’ without, however, ever reaching it, thereby foxing the problem of revision. Obviously the image of approximation in Popper’s (1963: Chapter 10) verisimilitude argument draws its persuasiveness from the successive approximation of polygons in approaching the perimeter of a circle, when determining the enclosed area. But if we have learned anything from the studies of various disciplines, then it is the fact that <mark>progress consists of being able to formulate new questions that could not even be asked previously</mark>. Thus, whatever we think of Kuhn’s argument about ‘paradigms’, we have to recognize that in times of revolutionary change the bounds of sense are being revolutionized and we donot simply now know some more of the encircled area!9 Third, <mark>pragmatism recognizes that science</mark> as a process of knowledge production <mark>is a social practice determined by rules</mark> in which <mark>scientists</mark> are not only constitutive for the definitions of problems (rather than simply lifting a veil over nature), they <mark>also debate questions that seem ‘undecidable’ and</mark> they <mark>have to ‘weigh’ the evidence</mark>, instead of being able to rely on the bi-valence principle of logic as an automatic truth finder (Kratochwil 2007). To that extent, the critical element of the epistemological project is retained, only that the ‘court’ which Kant believed to be reason itself, consists of the practitioners themselves. Instead of applying the standards as suggested by the epistemological project and the unity of science position, each science provides its own court and judges the appropriateness of its own methods and practices

Leventhal 25 - Justin Leventhal, Senior policy analyst for the American Consumer Institute, 10-9-2025, "The Economic Standard: Stop Taxing Bettors Back to the Black Market", The Economic Standard, https://econstandard.com/story/stop-taxing-bettors-back-to-the-black-market, accessed 2-2-2026 // ezpeasy
<mark>State</mark>s have also grown to rely on <mark>gambling revenue</mark>. So much so that some states are <mark>overreaching</mark>. New Jersey raised its online sports betting tax to 19.75 percent. Maryland moved from 15 percent to 20 percent, and Louisiana raised it to 21.5 percent. New York imposes a 51 percent tax on online sports betting. None of these are the highest—Illinois figured out an even a <mark>tax</mark> of $0.25 per wager on the first $20 million of bets, and $0.50 thereafter, that companies are already passing on to bettors and that hits small bets the hardest and is expected to <mark>push</mark> some <mark>gamblers back to illegal sites</mark>.
These taxes are all in addition to standard business taxes in those states.
Taxes eating into bettors’ odds aren’t the only barrier. Some proposals, like New York’s suggested $5,000 daily cap on wagers—not losses, wagers—could cut a bettor off after winning a single large bet. The law presumes to know what a person can afford to bet and assumes that it is a daily activity.
These <mark>restrictions</mark> are often <mark>sold as</mark> addiction <mark>safeguards</mark>, citing increases in calls to gambling helplines. Yet the prevalence of problem gambling has not increased with legalization, and remains about 1 percent of U.S. adults. Destigmatizing treatment and moving bets to legal platforms make it easier to addiction services to gambling addicts.
States can help without punishing responsible bettors. Make self-exclusion registries that are applicable to all operators. Require an option for player-set deposit and loss limits—not wager limits. Earmark a portion of the tax revenue generated to fund and promote addiction services.
High taxes and blunt force regulations don’t cure addicts—they <mark>push addicts</mark> int<mark>o</mark> one of the most <mark>dangerous places</mark>, illegal markets. Instead, <mark>states need to</mark> ensure legal options are competitive by <mark>keep</mark>ing <mark>taxes low</mark>—preferably the same as other businesses—and ensuring regulations don’t punish responsible gamblers. Keeping the legal industry healthy enhances state revenue and protects bettors.

2. [DL/T] - The root cause for the illegal market is the state fragmentation. AFF solves—it’s a try or die.

Heitner, Darren [B.A and J.D @ the University of Florida; Founder of Heitner Legal; Founder/Chief Editor of Sports Agent Blog, Focusing on Sports, Entertainment, and Intellectual Property Litigation]. “For 21 States Considering Sports Betting, Lack of Uniform Legislation Is a Problem.” Forbes, 2 Mar. 2018, www.forbes.com/sites/darrenheitner/2018/03/02/lack-of-uniform-wise-legislation-is-bad-for-21-states-considering-sports-betting/. Accessed 5 Feb. 2026.//JZ
Some of the more than 400 proposition bets for Super Bowl LI between the Philadelphia Eagles and the New England Patriots, which could be bet outside Nevada depending on the future of sports betting laws. ⁋ What would the sports betting landscape in the U.S. look like if the Supreme Court decides to strike down or modify the current federal prohibition on single-game sports wagering outside of Nevada? The simple answer is that it would probably be a hodgepodge of different rates of taxation, regulations and involvement by sports leagues. A lack of uniformity should be a major concern for the gaming industry as the days dwindle before a court decision is issued. ⁋ Many states are readying for a scenario wherein a wagering ban no longer exists. Just yesterday, Louisiana became the 21st state considering legislation that would permit sports betting, to a limited extent, if the federal prohibition falls. But Louisiana is currently looking at allowing sports wagering only at four racinos within the state and not affording an opportunity for online and mobile wagering. That is part of the problem with how the landscape for sports betting is being carved. ⁋ Nevada sportsbook operators pay a federal excise tax of 0.25% on overall handle (i.e. the total amount wagered) and a 6.75% state tax on the hold (i.e. the gross revenue actually earned by the sportsbook after the winners are paid out). ⁋ No payments are made by Nevada-based sportsbook operators to sports leagues, as “integrity fees” or otherwise. The sportsbooks are not required to use sports leagues’ statistics or data. Mobile betting is becoming the preferred choice of bettors and is very much available to consumers. In-game betting has provided consumers with new forms of exciting wagering options. The fees are low enough to encourage the vast majority of sports bettors located in Nevada to place wagers through the regulated system as opposed to using underground bookies. ⁋ The problem is that Nevada’s model is different from what Louisiana is proposing, which is not the same as what for instance the Massachusetts Gaming Commission recommends. Furthermore, the NBA and MLB, once ardently in opposition of any expansion of sports betting, have now come to the table and are even openly lobbying for the passage of legislation, state-by-state, only as long as that legislation includes a 1% “integrity fee” that would in reality force sportsbook operators to pay roughly 20% of the hold to the leagues when their teams are involved in a wager. Missouri and Illinois are considering bills that include the full integrity fee, New York is debating a 0.25% “reduced” integrity fee, and Connecticut seems to be unwilling to pay any type of integrity fee whatsoever. ⁋ “What I am not for, quite frankly, is legislation that in some way, shape or form would line the pockets of ... major league sports owners,” Connecticut Rep. Joe Verrengia said recently. ⁋ Ultimately, if the federal sports betting prohibition falls, the result will likely be a <mark>mix</mark> of varying fees, formats and <mark>regulations</mark> spread across the states, which could <mark>encourage a black market to coexist and flourish</mark> depending on the hold that operators feel pressured to promote <mark>based on pressure</mark> received <mark>from</mark> respective <mark>state governments.</mark> Additionally, such a scenario will <mark>make it difficult for operators to comply</mark> with myriad divergent laws <mark>and states</mark> will likely also find it <mark>hard to enforce</mark> their own stringent regulations. The result could be an actual <mark>clouding of the integrity of the games</mark> played <mark>or,</mark> at a minimum, <mark>the wagers placed,</mark> which is what sports leagues have stated was their biggest concern for over twenty years. ⁋ I propose for states that have already passed sports betting legislation and others currently considering bills to take a close look at the white paper published by the Massachusetts Gaming Commission this week. It correctly recognizes that <mark>any true curbing of an underground market will require the proliferation and acceptance of regulated</mark> online and mobile <mark>sportsbooks,</mark> which is something that Louisiana's new legislation does not permit. Additionally, the Commission acknowledges that creative options, such as in-game betting, need to be offered, as they will be offered on the black market as a substitute and cause many gamblers to shy away from regulated forms of wagering. Importantly, the Commission uses a tax rate of 10% on the hold as the base model, with Nevada's 6.75% correctly being the lowest reasonable tax rate and 15% labeled as a high tax rate. Any state considering taxation of the hold at a higher rate of 15% needs to reconsider the justification for same and the possible negative outcomes for oppressing operators in such a manner. ⁋ Optimally for the operators, states considering sports betting laws would adopt Nevada’s 6.75% state tax, but it appears that far too many states are hungry for the tax dollars, which increase exponentially if the states allow for online/mobile wagering. An increase to 10% on the hold is likely a non-issue, as long as the sports leagues do not effectively also lobby for a 20% cut on the hold as well. At that point, there is a major margin decrease, which could cause operators to increase their holds and force consumers to the black market, benefiting neither the operators, state budgets nor sports leagues.
