The FTC is weaponized by Trump and is inefficient. Even if they win that the FTC is spread less thin, they still use resources the wrong way in the world of the aff.
Tuttle 25 --- (Bryce Tuttle; Bryce Tuttle is a student at Stanford Law School. He previously worked in the office of FTC Commissioner Bedoya and in the Bureau of Competition, 5-28-2025, "The FTC Should Not Be Wielded as a Weapon of Authoritarianism", https://www.thesling.org/the-ftc-should-not-be-wielded-as-a-weapon-of-authoritarianism/) //doa1-29-2026 + ELIMAFIA 🕵️

Needless to say, the investigation is without merit. <mark>Media Matters’ efforts to stop hate speech</mark> on Musk’s platform are in <mark>no way</mark> the type of horizontal <mark>collusion</mark> cognizable <mark>under</mark> the <mark>antitrust</mark> laws. Indeed, there is no mention of collusion, nor any claim of antitrust violation, in Musk’s original suit. (The suit instead claims that Media Matters interfered with Twitter’s contracts with advertisers, disparaged the quality of the platform.) The legality of such an attempt to coordinate a political boycott was settled long ago in NAACP v. Claiborne Hardware, a case concerning a boycott of racist businesses during the Civil Rights Era. Even the 5th Circuit has acknowledged the danger of this suit—specifically the potential for coercion or intimidation Musk’s discovery efforts to get the names of Media Matters’ donors poses. Under the first Trump administration, the Justice Department pursued a similar theory of coordination among carmakers that reached an accord with California over emission standards—a clear act of political lobbying exempted from antitrust scrutiny under the Noerr-Pennington doctrine. (The case was smartly withdrawn in the early days of the Biden administration.) <mark>Despite</mark> this <mark>background </mark>and clear caselaw, the <mark>FTC has decided to follow</mark> Musk’s <mark>vendetta </mark>and<mark> use its limited resources</mark> <mark>to</mark> <mark>pursue</mark> a <mark>claim</mark> with little-to-no chance of success.

Having worked in the agency recently, I am often asked by law school professors and classmates what I think will happen to antitrust enforcement at the FTC during the second Trump administration. Generally, I have said that I assume that not too much will change. The agency’s Republican leaders generally support strong enforcement—for example, Mark Meador is on the record as supportive of the Robinson-Patman Act (RPA), a law hated by big business. (To my disappointment, Meador voted to withdraw the FTC’s pending (meritorious and well-researched) RPA claim against PepsiCo.) In many areas of federal law enforcement, the presumption of regularity has already broken down for this administration. I hoped (though perhaps did not expect) the FTC would be different. That an agency with a history of independence would remain so. That is, after all, what happened during the first Trump administration.

I was wrong. Wednesday’s actions, likely directed by Chair Ferguson and his staff, indicate that the <mark>Chair of the FTC plans to use </mark>his <mark>agency as a weapon in Trump’s antidemocratic arsenal</mark>. Ferguson will, presumably in pursuit of Trump’s favor and Musk’s political war chest, <mark>sacrifice</mark> the<mark> goodwill the agency has built up</mark> with the judiciary and the public over the decades. I fear that <mark>after this administration is over</mark>, the <mark>agency</mark> where I started my career <mark>will be</mark> a shadow of what it once was—<mark>depleted of all its credibility</mark>, quality <mark>staff</mark>, and <mark>legitimacy</mark>. The Wall Steet Journal editorial page lambasted Lina Khan for “politicizing” antitrust by trying to dismantle concentrations of economic power—even though dismantling such concentrations was the original purpose of the FTC Act. Yet Khan’s <mark>FTC</mark> never once <mark>use</mark>d i<mark>ts power to harass</mark> <mark>a</mark> political <mark>enemy</mark>. But that’s precisely what Ferguson’s FTC is doing.

The anti-monopoly community cannot continue to behave as if business-as-usual will continue at the FTC and DOJ. We cannot continue to segment anti-monopoly policy off from the rest of the Trump administration’s authoritarian actions. Under this administration, there is only one policy issue—democracy protection. We should do everything in our power to make sure that agencies like the FTC are not wielded as weapons of authoritarianism. If that means sacrificing the credibility of the agency I love, so be it.

Bernstein 25 --- (Dan Bernstein; Dan Bernstein is an Assistant Editor at Sportico. Prior to joining, he edited and reported for global sports publications such as The Mirror, GOAL and Sporting News. 10-28-2025, "Trump Family Deepens Sports Betting Ties Amid Industry Disruption", https://www.sportico.com/business/sports-betting/2025/donald-trump-truth-social-prediction-markets-sports-bets-1234875114/) //doa1-29-2026 + ELIMAFIA 🕵️
President Donald <mark>Trump’s family</mark> has <mark>held ties to</mark> <mark>sports</mark> <mark>prediction</mark> markets since the start of his second term, and on Tuesday, his links to the exchanges that offer <mark>a form of betting available in all 50 states</mark> became more concrete.

Trump’s social media platform Truth Social announced a prediction market partnership with Crypto.com, which owns Nadex, a futures trading exchange. The agreement will enable Truth Social users to place sports bets within its app.

The news comes at a time prediction markets are upending the U.S. sports betting industry, spooking investors in traditional sportsbooks who are now scrambling to react. Shares in DraftKings and FanDuel parent Flutter were down slightly on Tuesday in the immediate aftermath of the Truth Social announcement.

Crypto.com has curried favor with the president’s family, and the relationship has led to business deals. In August, Trump Media & Technology Group, the owner of Truth Social, announced it would buy about $105 million in the Singapore-based company’s affiliated token, CRO, as part of the creation of a new company called Trump Media Group CRO Strategy.

Kris Marszalek, the CEO of Crypto.com, visited Trump’s Mar-a-Lago resort last December, and his company donated $1 million to Trump’s inauguration.

The Crypto.com prediction market agreement will make Truth Social “the first social media platform to offer its users technology to access embedded prediction markets capabilities,” according to a press release.

While Kalshi and Polymarket are official partners of X, those deals are only promotional in nature and thus far do not include the ability to place bets within the Elon Musk-owned platform.

Truth Social’s prediction market announcement specifically mentions it <mark>will include bets “across all major sports leagues</mark>.” That follows the NHL’s move to become the first major U.S. league to formally embrace prediction markets last week.

Premium subscribers to Truth Social will receive special subsidies for their bets. Truth Social will begin beta testing the prediction-market integration soon, followed by a full launch in the U.S. It eventually plans to operate globally.

Trump’s main connection with prediction markets is through his son, Donald Trump Jr., who is an advisor to Kalshi and Polymarket and an investor in the latter via 1789 Capital. Trump Jr. is on Trump Media’s board of directors, meaning he now has links to a third prominent prediction market operator through the Crypto.com tie-up.

Before assuming office for his second term, Trump transferred his stake in Trump Media to a revocable trust in which Trump Jr. is the sole trustee.

<mark>Trump has ties to the casino industry</mark> dating back to his investments in Atlantic City in the 1980s, some of which resulted in bankruptcies.
