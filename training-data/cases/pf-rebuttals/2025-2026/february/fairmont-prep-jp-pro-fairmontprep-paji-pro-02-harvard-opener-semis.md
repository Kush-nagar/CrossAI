Donnelly 25 [Martin Donnelly, worked @ HM Treasury and the European Commission, MA in Philosophy, Politics and Economics @ Oxford, 6-19-2025, "The next steps for the UK-EU reset", Centre for European Reform, accessed 8-18-25, https://www.cer.eu/insights/next-steps-uk-eu-reset, Oliver J]
The <mark>UK-EU summit</mark> last month was an important step towards closer co-operation and a strategic partnership. Both sides now need to turn that ambition into detailed sectoral negotiations with a clear timescale.
Inevitably, the summit’s conclusions were <mark>more of</mark> a <mark>roadmap than</mark> a set of <mark>agreed legal outcomes</mark>. But it marks the changed relationship between <mark>London and Brussels</mark>, moving on from the contested aftermath of the 2016 referendum, with potential to deliver increased growth as well as shared security for both parties. The UK signalled its return to genuine collaboration with its European partners and neighbours through a Security and Defence Partnership, a political accord to make progress on key sectors including energy, and an agreement which defused fish as an issue. 2. The new deal does nothing.
Kundnani 25 [Hans Kundnani, associate fellow and former Europe programme director at Chatham House, 5-20-2025, "The EU-UK reset exposes the limits of a “geopolitical Europe”", New Statesman, accessed 8-18-25, https://www.newstatesman.com/international-politics/2025/05/limits-eu-uk-reset-starmer-von-der-leyen, Oliver J]
The EU has not yet given the UK anything that it does not give to other “third countries” – the term it uses for countries that are not EU member states. For example, as critics of the deal have pointed out, the <mark>UK will be</mark> <mark>consulted</mark> on changes in EU food standards in the <mark>same way that</mark> European Economic Area (<mark>EEA</mark>) countries such as Norway <mark>are</mark>. Even now, in other words, the <mark>UK</mark> is <mark>not</mark> being <mark>given any</mark> kind of <mark>special treatment</mark>, <mark>despite</mark> its important <mark>role as</mark> a <mark>security provider</mark> for Europe, which the war in Ukraine has underlined and the re-election of Trump has made even more important.
Thus, the EU-UK “reset” illustrates the limits of the idea of a geopolitical Europe – something there has been much talk of in Brussels and other European capitals in the past five years. When Von der Leyen became European Commission president in 2019, she promised a “geopolitical Commission”. Although she never quite spelled out what that meant, the implication was that instead of simply enforcing EU rules in order to maintain the integrity of the single market, the Commission would act in a more strategic way under her leadership.
However, the EU stuck to the same approach regarding the UK that it had taken since the referendum in 2016 – that is, it sought to impose economic costs on the UK for leaving the bloc. A more strategic approach might have been to recognise the UK’s role in European security, which was especially important as the threats to Europe and uncertainty about the US security guarantee increased, and to make concessions to Britain even if doing so undermined the integrity of the single market. But instead, the EU took its traditional rules-based approach. Even with the new deal, this has not changed – nor, given the way the EU is set up, can it.
For all the talk of a reset, therefore, the basic <mark>dynamic between</mark> the <mark>EU and</mark> the <mark>UK remains</mark> the <mark>same as</mark> it was <mark>before</mark> the <mark>Lancaster</mark> House summit. Even as Starmer has sought to improve relations with Brussels – and even tentatively to reintegrate the UK into some EU institutions and structures – political realities mean that he cannot go much further, even if Remainers want him to. In particular, he has made it clear that under his leadership the UK will not rejoin the single market or the customs union.
Meanwhile, it sometimes seems as if the <mark>EU is incapable of</mark> having a <mark>cooperative relationship</mark> with countries in what it calls its “neighbourhood” without trying to integrate them into its own institutions and structures. The <mark>only way it seems to be able to conceive of</mark> European <mark>countries is</mark> either <mark>as a</mark> future <mark>member state</mark> or as a threat to the bloc. As a former member state that is also an important security provider, the UK is neither. It remains to be seen whether the EU can find a new way of co-existing with it – and if so, what that might look like.

1. DL/T - The CPTPP is bad---benefits are minimal, risks are pervasive, and the plan is key for material solvency. 
Lowe 24 [Independent Commission on UK-EU Relations, organization specializing on researching the departure of the UK from the EU, citing <mark>Sam Lowe</mark>, visiting senior research fellow @ Policy Institute, Kings College London with a focus on multinational trade, April 2024, "CPTPP: economic impact and implications for UK-EU relations — Independent Commission on UK-EU Relations", Independent Commission on UK-EU Relations, accessed 8-1-25, https://www.ukeucommission.org/commentary/cptpp-economic-impact-and-implications-for-uk-eu-relations, Oliver J]
Trade and economic impacts
Responses to UK accession vary from cheerleading its success as an independent trading nation to bemoaning the fact that it will do little to remedy trade lost with the EU due to the nature of Boris Johnson’s Brexit agreements. Some fear that membership will make it more difficult in future for the UK to rejoin the EU or its customs union, should a future government choose to apply.
The truth is somewhere between the two. CPTPP membership is of some value to UK businesses and to the UK economy but will not compensate for lost trade with the EU.
<mark>CPTPP is not</mark> an Asian version of the EU or even its <mark>single market</mark>. It is, says Sam Lowe of Flint Global, a “pretty decent free trade agreement” between eleven countries, soon to be twelve. As with most free trade agreements it does more <mark>for</mark> trade in goods (via tariff reduction/removal) than <mark>trade in services</mark>.
Unlike deeper agreements, such as the EEA, <mark>it does not</mark> in practice do much to <mark>combat regulatory barriers</mark> to trade, but it does include some provisions relating to how members approach things such as conformity assessment.
CPTPP is a big trading bloc; it will be worth 15% of global GDP after the UK joins. But given the <mark>UK already has</mark> bilateral <mark>agreements with most members</mark> its <mark>added value to</mark> the <mark>UK economy will </mark>be small. Accession is expected to <mark>add</mark> a <mark>mere 0.08%,</mark> or £1.8 billion, to the size of the UK's economy over the next 10 years.
Given existing agreements membership will have only <mark>minimal</mark> impact on tariffs. There is greater benefit in changes to rules of origin rules.
From a goods perspective, says Lowe, the main benefit of CPTPP for UK firms is the diagonal cumulation provisions. CPTPP will give UK exporters the additional option of sourcing parts and inputs from CPTPP members, which for something like EVs could be useful.
CPTPP accession has not received the same levels of criticism as <mark>previous trade deals with Australia</mark> and New Zealand, both of which have <mark>put</mark> the <mark>UK’s agriculture sector at risk</mark>. However even the UK Government’s analysis from 2021 suggests that the UK the <mark>semi-processed food sector will be harmed</mark> as a result of accession. Most other sectors will feel little or no impact.
CPTPP will not turbocharge the economy or alleviate the difficulties facing UK consumers and businesses during the cost of living crisis and sluggish growth forecasts. The <mark>best</mark> way to <mark>remedy</mark> this <mark>is a close</mark>r <mark>EU relationship</mark>, which would have a positive ripple effect across the UK economy.
Impact on UK-EU relations
Nonetheless, and understandably given the lack of economic benefit to the UK, many are concerned that CPTPP membership will preclude closer UK-EU relations in future, which remains the UK’s best option to improve its trade balance and promote economic growth.
<mark>CPTPP will not make up for</mark> the economic <mark>costs of Brexit</mark>. The Office for Budget Responsibility (OBR) has previously said <mark>Brexit would reduce</mark> the <mark>UK's potential economic growth by</mark> about 4% in the long term. The Centre for European Reform puts this figure at <mark>5</mark>.5<mark>%</mark>; there is <mark>no doubt</mark> that <mark>Brexit</mark> has <mark>caused</mark> at least some of the <mark>problems of low growth</mark> and productivity now experienced by the UK economy.
