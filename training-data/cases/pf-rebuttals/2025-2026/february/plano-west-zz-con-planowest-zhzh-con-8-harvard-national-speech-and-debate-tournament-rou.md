In theory, the objectives outlined in the previous section could be achieved through regulation by the federal government, rather than regulation by state governments. Because the Court in Murphy only held that the federal government could not prohibit states from legalizing sports betting, their decision leaves open the possibility of legalization and regulation at the national level.122 However, direct <mark>federal</mark> <mark>involvement in sports betting would be unwise</mark> for several reasons. Second,<mark>states have historically been the primary actors in regulating other forms of gambling</mark>, such as casino game<mark>s, and state regulation has generally been effective</mark>.124 Many <mark>states already have gaming commissions or other agencies who specialize in oversight of the gaming industry, while no such agency exists at the federal level.</mark> Stat<mark>e governments</mark> <mark>have more experience and expertise in dealing with gambling issues, so they are better suited to</mark> <mark>assume responsibility for regulating sports betting</mark>.<mark> Because federal regulation is unwise, the </mark>19 states in which sports gambling is still illegal should pass legislation that legalizes sports gambling. Allowing the establishment of legitimate sportsbooks will help suppress the influence of underground and offshore sports betting operations. These unregulated enterprises give bettors no protections against fraudulent bookmakers, lost funds, or market manipulation, and are often associated with undesirable activities like organized crime, money laundering, and match-fixing.125 People will always seek to gamble on sports, but by legalizing sports betting, states can give bettors safer and less socially harmful alternatives for placing their bets. Legalizing the sports betting industry can also create significant economic benefits for states. Taxation of sports betting will provide much-needed revenue for state governments,126 and jobs will be created at casinos, sportsbooks, and in related industries. Finally, states can collect much more information on sports betting activity if it is legal. This information can help states uncover illegal activity around sports – for example, unusually heavy betting on one team could be an indicator that a game has been fixed. Additionally, information on individual gamblers can be used to identify people with gambling problems so that the state can help them get the services they need, similar to existing systems already found in casinos. Legalizing sports betting may also contribute to reducing the stigma around gambling, which would make it easier for people to seek help for gambling addiction.127

1. [T] - Regulations increase illegal market activity—Netherlands and UK prove.

Gibbs ‘25 — Erik Gibbs, Writer @ NEXT.io [4-15-2025, NEXT.io, "Tightened gambling regulations coincide with black market rise," https://next.io/news/regulation/tightened-gambling-regulations-coincide-black-market-rise/, Accessed 1-29-2026] // cpsof
Newly released data <mark>in the Netherlands and</mark> the <mark>UK</mark> suggest a <mark>significant surge in black market gambling linked</mark> directly <mark>to</mark> recent increases in <mark>government</mark>-imposed <mark>restrictions</mark> aimed <mark>at</mark> curbing online <mark>gambling</mark> harm.
Both countries have implemented tighter <mark>controls on legal gambling</mark> operations over the past year, including spending limits and mandatory verification procedures.
In turn, the measures appear to have unintentionally <mark>pushed</mark> more <mark>gamblers to</mark>ward unlicensed and <mark>illegal platforms</mark>.
The Netherlands Gambling Authority (Kansspelautoriteit, or KSA) has reported a near parity between the amounts of money generated in the legal and illegal online gambling markets during the final quarter of 2024.
The agency estimated that Dutch gamblers spent approximately €305m on illegal gambling websites, compared to €306m on regulated platforms over the same three-month period.
This marks a dramatic shift from earlier in the year, when about 58% of Dutch gambling losses went to legal sites.
Offshore rise linked to mandatory spending caps
The decline in legal market share coincided with the introduction of new protective measures on 1 October, 2024. These measures included mandatory monthly spending caps for online gamblers.
Players under the age of 24 were limited to €150 per month, while those older than this were capped at €350.
Exceeding these limits requires gamblers to submit a request and demonstrate financial capability to the gambling platform. These restrictions were designed to minimise gambling-related harm, particularly among younger adults.
While the KSA noted some positive trends resulting from the new rules — including a reduction in high monthly losses — the impact on consumer behaviour has been complex.
The average loss per adult gambler over the age of 24 fell from €160 to €148 per month in the second half of 2024.
Additionally, the proportion of users losing more than €1,000 monthly dropped from 4% before October to 1.4% after the rule change. However, these gains were offset by a concurrent rise in spending on unlicensed gambling sites.
In total, the <mark>amount staked</mark> minus winnings paid out <mark>rose 6%</mark> in 2024 to nearly €1.5bn.
That growth slowed in the latter half of the year, possibly influenced by the earlier spike in gambling around June’s European Football Championship.
UK study reinforces black market increase
Meanwhile, a report from UK gambling harm awareness group Deal Me Out revealed a similarly concerning trend.
The group has identified a notable increase in traffic to <mark>offshore gambling</mark> web<mark>sites</mark>, particularly those promoted by social media influencers and gambling streamers.
According to the report, as many as 5 million social media clicks per month are directing users to unlicensed platforms via referral links.
These influencers often instruct viewers on how to bypass geo-restrictions using virtual private networks (VPNs), and promote sites that do not require Know Your Customer (KYC) checks.
One major factor driving this behaviour is the perception that black market sites offer a more exciting gambling experience.
These platforms frequently showcase high-roller games and features that have been banned or restricted on licensed UK websites, such as turbo spins and autoplay options.
Deal Me Out’s findings also revealed a demographic detail: an estimated 400,000 underage individuals are engaging in gambling activity on offshore websites, especially those associated with video games, loot boxes, and in-game currency purchases.
This activity often circumvents age verification protocols required by licensed operators in the UK.
A separate study recently found that UK <mark>black market</mark> gambling has <mark>risen 522%</mark> over the past three years.
The growing preference for black market platforms comes at a time when the UK is rolling out stricter gambling regulations.
Among the most controversial have been mandatory affordability checks, tighter limits on spending, and design changes to certain high-risk gaming features.
While intended to safeguard vulnerable <mark>consumers</mark>, these measures appear to have alienated a portion of the gambling population, <mark>frustrated over intrusive data collection</mark> and reduced gameplay options.
