Automatic funding cutoff. There should be an automatic funding cutoff for operations that do not comply with the War Powers Resolution, without requiring further action by Congress. As noted above, Congress not only has the constitutional authority to “declare war” but also possesses the <mark>power of the purse</mark>. And yet that power <mark>has been</mark> largely <mark>ineffectual</mark>. <mark>Due to</mark> the <mark>massive defense budgets</mark>, most <mark>mil</mark>itary <mark>op</mark>eration<mark>s</mark>, <mark>even large ones</mark>, <mark>do not require</mark> separate <mark>appropriations</mark>. Gomez-Thomas 25 [Raphael Gomez-Thomas, BA in International Relations @ Brandeis University, 05-18-2025, " From Corruption to Combat: The Militarization of Organized Crime in Mexico", Brandeis University, https://scholarworks.brandeis.edu/esploro/outputs/undergraduate/From-Corruption-to-Combat-The-Militarization/9924479764601921, DOA: 3-24-2026] chiara *OCGs=Organized Crime Groups*
Federalization as an Accelerator of Aggression This section examines how, during the mid-2000s, the escalating instability of organized crime groups (OCGs) and their increasing use of violence prompted the <mark>Mexican government</mark>, under President Calderón, to adjust its strategy to focus on <mark>limiting OCGs' power through state force</mark>. As the state shifted from protecting them to opposing them, OCGs faced increased security risks, further <mark>accelerating</mark> the denigration of the gray-zone relationship between them. This former structural compromise between actors was disappearing, representing the shift from second-generation OCGs to third-generation groups.¶ The State as a Competitor for Control From the mid-2000s onward, the state began to take a more active role in confronting OCGs, seeking to contest OCGs’ impunity from the state in their criminal governance regimes. OCGs began to <mark>encroach on the state's</mark> fundamental <mark>authority</mark> and undermine themselves <mark>through increased acts of violence</mark> (Brands, 2009, p. 7). OCGs fought openly over plazas such as Juarez, Tijuana, Nuevo Laredo13 , and various ports, creating open conflict that, while not directly targeting the state, weakened its position due to its inability to contain the violence (Brands, 2009, p. 7). OCGs to ensure and demonstrate their influence and control in their territories displayed the violence openly in their urban environments by using public areas to demonstrate dismembered corpses, body parts, and public assassinations (Shirk & Wallman, 2015, p. 1354).¶ A breaking point came when PAN candidate Felipe Calderón assumed the presidency in 2006 and immediately declared a “war on cartels” (Werthmeir & Beaubien, 2011). In the city of Uruapan in Michoacan14 , fighting OCGs dumped five severed heads onto a nightclub's dance floor, making national headlines (Flannery, 2013, p. 181). This open act of violence in Calderón’s home <mark>state</mark> of Michoacán, which had already seen 400 homicides that year, thrust him into a <mark>legitimacy crisis</mark> (Flannery, 2013, p. 181). As a newly elected conservative who campaigned on a platform of stability and order, Calderón had to take drastic measures to ensure that his presidency and the Mexican State maintained legitimacy (Moreno, 2013, p. 18). The Calderón Administration implemented a nationwide anti-OCG strategy to curb the rising violence and the increasing power of OCGs. A series of policy decisions brought national security forces into direct conflict with OCGs as they competed for a monopoly on power (Sullivan, 2013, p. 4). As state institutions became more actively involved, it intensified OCGs' trend toward greater violence and confrontation with the state (Sullivan, 2012, p. 4).¶ <mark>In response to</mark> increasingly <mark>militarized crackdowns</mark> driven by a federal security strategy, some <mark>OCGs</mark> sought to <mark>challenge the state more directly</mark>. The contention between the state and OCGs <mark>shift</mark>ed the positions of those OCGs with <mark>weaker corruption</mark> relationships from criminal governance <mark>to </mark>quasi-<mark>rebel governance</mark> (Bunker, 2013, p. 132). With this transition, OCGs were no longer cohabitors but <mark>contenders against the state</mark>, with the state aiming to eradicate both the OCG and its criminal governance regimes. Furthermore, in this contention, OCGs needed to <mark>control</mark> both the <mark>licit</mark> and illicit <mark>worlds to oppose state rule</mark>. OCGs sought support from the local population to aid them in undermining the state's authority and to expand regional control to ensure they could conduct their operations. One of the earliest prolific examples was La Familia Michoacana15 in the State of Michoacan, an OCG that utilized extreme violence to establish extensive racketeering networks and secure routes for illicit contraband in the early 2000s(Falbab-Brown, 2011, p. 25). This compelled the local population, not involved in illicit activities, to submit to the governance of La Familia, relying on them for safety and paying extortion fees as a form of taxation, thereby directly competing with the state's most fundamental role.¶ The desire of OCGs to “regulate the social, political, and economic lives of non-combatants,” following the concept of rebel governance, introduced an insurgent dimension to their criminal activities (Albert, 2022, p. 623; Brands, 2009, p. 11). In this new paradigm, in the most extreme cases, OCGs employed violence to undermine the state's position in all socio-economic aspects within a given territory. This development prevented the state from interfering with the OCG's criminal activities (Bunker, 2013, p. 132). The development of the criminal governance regimen relies on OCGs' ability to exert control in their territories and their dominance in the relationship with the state.¶ This use of violence to achieve broader goals beyond illegal gain and in direct opposition to the state effectively made organized crime groups (OCGs) into quasi-insurgents. As defined by the Central Intelligence Agency, insurgents are domestic actors who <mark>employ</mark> irregular <mark>military forces</mark> and unlawful political organizations to weaken government authority and legitimacy (Byman, 2008, p. 169). They do this to seize control of a specific area and <mark>establish an alternative governance system</mark> (Byman, 2008, p. 169). OCGs began to align with these definitions as they aimed to undermine the state and prove themselves through force to gain control of a territory. OCGs began operating under quasi-rebel governance, not to achieve full state capitulation, but to create de facto zones of impunity by using conventional and guerrilla tactics to challenge state security forces (Bunker, 2013, p. 132).¶ The transition from criminal to quasi-rebel governance challenges the state's and OCGs' relationship. OCG's attempt to overshadow the state in certain areas implicitly meant that they relinquished any claim to state protection. In regions where the rebel governance model emerged, the state could impose its authority, only through armed forces. While state militarization at the federal level to address a quasi-insurgency contributed to a rise in OCG violence though increased conflict, other factors also played a part. Economic downturns, external cartel influences, and shifting drug demand in the U.S. may have further intensified inter-OCG conflicts.¶ Federal Policy: Strategic Implications The new anti-OCG policy decisions of the Calderón Administration prompted direct government action against Organized Crime Groups (OCGs), but the policy's implementation varied across states and changed over time. This inconsistency greatly affected outcomes and requires careful analysis to understand the consequences fully. The change in the use of federal resources brought government institutions such as the Army, Navy, and Federal Police, commonly known as Federales, into a more prominent role in confronting OCGs, accelerating the capability of violence (Brands, 2009, p. 14).¶ Previously, a federal anti-narcotic policy had enacted search-and-destroy tactics using the army to eradicate heroin and marijuana cultivation operations (Toro, 1995, p. 18). This tactic received support from the United States regarding resources and diplomatic backing. However, endemic corruption within the Mexican government often undermined its effectiveness (Toro, 1995, p. 17, 71). Historically, the government response was much more robust for traditional 39 ideologically driven insurgencies, such as the Zapatista16 uprising in the 1990s and Mexico's covert dirty war17 in the 1970s (Negroponte, 2011). Although OCGs pursued profit and power rather than ideology, Calderón’s administration responded as if confronting an ideological insurgency, heavily militarizing federal security forces.¶ The development of federalization meant a transition from standard policing to a militarized approach as the federal police and the military began conducting operations. The militarization of Mexican security policy introduced two tactical approaches that escalated violence between organized crime groups (OCGs) and the state. The first tactic involved operations focusing on High-Value Targeting, or HVT, in a <mark>Kingpin Strategy</mark>18 . This tactic focuses on <mark>decapitating the leadership of OCGs</mark> to eliminate key personnel and weaken their operational capabilities. Law enforcement carried out this strategy using traditional tactics of arrest and imprisonment. However, the plan also included security forces conducting extrajudicial killings and eliminating targets during operations, similar to the tactic employed by the United States in the Global War on Terror. This tactic <mark>resulted in</mark> further <mark>fractalization </mark>of OCGs, with <mark>minimal reduction in operation</mark>al capabilitie<mark>s</mark>, <mark>leading to increased violence</mark> due to competition among OCGs and reaction to aggression from the state.
