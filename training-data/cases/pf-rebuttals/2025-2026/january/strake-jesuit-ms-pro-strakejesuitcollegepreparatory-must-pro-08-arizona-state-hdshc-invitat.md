Greer 16 [Adam Greer, officer in the United States Air Force and a Pacific Forum, CSIS WSD-Handa Fellow; 07/20/2016; “The South China Sea Is Really a Fishery Dispute”; The Diplomat; https://thediplomat.com/2016/07/the-south-china-sea-is-really-a-fishery-dispute/; DOA: 11/29/2025] Sumith recut doobz
Last week the Permanent Court of Arbitration in <mark>The Hague ruled</mark> overwhelmingly in favor of the Philippines in its case <mark>against China’s</mark> South China Sea (<mark>SCS</mark>) <mark>claims</mark>. The nearly 500 page ruling undercut Beijing’s claims to control all the land features and water inside China’s nine-dash line and concluded that the disputed land features are either rocks that generate small (12 nautical miles) territorial seas or low-tide elevations that convey no exclusive rights <mark>to exploit resources.</mark> Although the ruling—and much of the surrounding analysis—has necessarily placed considerable emphasis on sovereignty disputes in the SCS, less attention has been given to the <mark>underlying incentives</mark> that <mark>drive</mark> claimant positions and behaviors.
Given its power and recent <mark>assertiveness</mark> in the South China Sea, China’s interests deserve special attention. Aside from enlarging China’s security perimeter, China’s regional interests can be roughly lumped into three “P”s—politics, petroleum, and proteins (fish). The last <mark>of</mark> these <mark>interests, competition over dwindling</mark> SCS <mark>fisheries</mark>, may be <mark>most consequential</mark> in driving competition, but has not received sufficient analytic attention.
Although the SCS covers only 2.5 percent of the Earth’s surface, it is home to some of the world’s richest reef systems and over 3,000 indigenous and migratory fish species, comprising some 12 percent of the total global fish catch. Unfortunately, the region’s fisheries are in serious jeopardy. As of 2008, virtually all SCS fishery stocks are collapsed (roughly 25 percent), over-exploited (roughly 25 percent), or fully-exploited (roughly 50 percent). The situation is only worsening.
The most important aspect of the Spratly Island disputes is <mark>not</mark> oil or <mark>sovereignty</mark>—it is whether or not SCS fish continue to appear on Asia’s menus. Four trends in particular are important: sustainability, economic importance, rising demand, and declining access.
The First Trend: Sustainability
Three indicators reveal the extent of SCS fishery degradation. First, catches have remained an unsustainable 10-12 million tons per year for decades—a number that could double when Illegal, Unreported, and Unregulated (IUU) fishing is incorporated. Steady catches mask a serious problem: catches increasingly consist of smaller species whose populations have boomed as natural predators have been overfished—a phenomenon commonly referred to as “fishing down the food web.”
Secondly, fishermen Catch Per Unit Effort (CPUE) has sharply declined over the last several decades as fishermen are forced to spend more time and fuel to bring in the same amount of fish from shrinking fisheries. Destructive practices, including the use of coral-damaging bottom trawlers, muro-ami nets, or even dynamite and cyanide are often employed to squeeze more from dwindling fisheries.
Third, critical habitats are disappearing. A 2012 meta-analysis of maritime studies found that in just the last 10-15 years, SCS coral coverage rates in disputed regions have declined from over 60 percent to just 20 percent. Reefs along China’s coastline are in even worse shape, and have declined over 80 percent in the last several decades.
The Second Trend: Economic Importance
The <mark>fishing</mark> industry continues to be <mark>critical to China’s economy</mark>. Fishing revenues make up about 3 percent of China’s GDP <mark>and</mark> generate up to $279 billion (1.732 trillion RMB) annually. Perhaps more importantly, China <mark>employs</mark> between 7 and 9 million fishermen (over <mark>14 million</mark> industry-wide) who operate over 450,000 fishing vessels (nearly 200,000 are ocean-going vessels) comprising the largest fleet on Earth. Many of these workers have little option for alternative employment.
The Third Trend: Rising Demand
Fish is increasingly important to the Chinese diet. China’s fish consumption grew annually at 6 percent between 1990 and 2010, and <mark>China consumes 34 percent of</mark> the global <mark>fish</mark> food supply, nearly triple that of Europe and Central Asia combined, and over five times the amount of North America. Furthermore, the World Bank estimates China’s fish consumption will increase 30 percent to over 41 kilograms per capita by 2030, more than double the projected global average. <mark>Although aquaculture accounts</mark> <mark>for</mark> the <mark>majority of</mark> China’s fish <mark>production</mark> (73.6 percent), <mark>growing demand</mark> threatens to <mark>outstrip supply, necessitating</mark> ongoing <mark>expansion of</mark> maritime <mark>fishing</mark> <mark>op</mark>eration<mark>s</mark>—the vast majority of which (78 percent) occur <mark>in</mark> Chinese-claimed waters, including <mark>the S</mark>outh <mark>C</mark>hina <mark>S</mark>ea.
The Fourth Trend: Declining Access
China perceives its access to disputed fisheries as declining. Three categories of grievances are commonly cited. First, China’s fishing bans and sustainability efforts are commonly ignored by foreigners, leading to complaints that Chinese restrictions allow foreigners free access to fish without Chinese competition. In one 2011 example, Vietnamese fishermen reportedly caught 110,000 tons of SCS tuna compared to China’s 300 tons; a data point that seemingly confirmed fears that regulations undercut China’s potential profits.
Second, analysts complain foreign fishermen are “killing the chicken to harvest its eggs” (杀鸡取卵) through overfishing or using destructive practices. Some of this is understandable—while China has made gains in domestic regulation, the millions of “small-boat” fishermen employed by the region’s developing economies pose a serious challenge to governance and sustainability.
Third, Chinese fishermen widely complain of foreign harassment. Chinese sources report that between 1989 and 2011 there were over 360 cases of attacks, robberies, detainments, or shootings, with 25 Chinese fishermen killed or missing. Fear of interference has reportedly deterred Chinese fishermen from venturing into some disputed fisheries—although China historically issues around 500 Spratly Island fishing permits, far less fishermen reportedly travel to the region (prior to 2013) due to concerns over interference.
Actions and Reactions in the “South Sea”
Endangered fisheries pose a serious dilemma as claimants seek to promote sustainable governance that would maintain fish stocks over the long term without incurring economic loss or conceding ground on sovereignty disputes. The efforts of different Chinese actors to lay claim to political narratives within conflicting policy objectives (sustainability versus exclusive-access, in this case) produce policy outcomes that are often erratic, contradictory, or even self-defeating.
At the regional level, decentralized and often contradictory policies driven by domestic interest groups can undermine potential international solutions to shared problems. The lack of effective international governance is at the heart of SCS sustainability problems. The SCS lacks the multilateral Regional Fisheries Management Organizations (RFMO) that successfully manage fisheries elsewhere. And unlike the East China Sea and Yellow Sea, there are no bilateral or multilateral fishing agreements in the SCS. Furthermore, because the region’s EEZs remain hotly contested, UNCLOS fails to provide adequate governance. China has already dismissed the Permanent Court of Arbitration’s ruling on SCS EEZ disputes as invalid, and absent Beijing’s cooperation, disagreements over regional boundaries will likely persist well into the future.
One of two courses of action seems necessary: claimants must either agree to binding, multilateral action to govern contested areas or one country must provide undisputed oversight over fishery stocks.
Few regional states possess the power necessary to attempt the latter course of action. China is the exception. China’s numerous efforts at regional governance include ongoing seasonal SCS fishing bans (1999); the administrative upgrade of Sansha city from a county to a prefecture-level city (2012); the complete overhaul of China’s maritime bureaucracies into a new China State Oceanic Administration (2013); Hainan province’s law requiring foreign fishermen to seek State Council approval prior to entering Chinese-claimed SCS waters (2013); and most recently, China’s massive island-building projects in the Spratly Islands (2013-2015).
China’s sustainability efforts are undermined by overriding incentives to retain full, if not necessarily exclusive, access to disputed fisheries to provide employment and food security. Since 2007, China has paid out billions in fuel subsidies to compensate for the industry’s unprofitability (as reflected by declining CPUE values). Although subsidies keep the industry afloat—and some 14 million industry workers employed—they have the unfortunate externality of undermining sustainability efforts by skewing market forces to allow fishermen to keep squeezing over-exploited fisheries.
Additionally, China’s domestic sovereignty narratives further complicate efforts. Decades of commitment to the nine-dash line as a sovereignty demarcation has produced a great deal of political inertia that can be leveraged by Chinese groups for their own interest. Supporters of the Chinese National Offshore Oil Corporation (CNOOC), for example, argue that government-subsidized oil exploration in disputed SCS regions should be used to demonstrate sovereignty—an argument oft-recycled along the spectrum of would-be SCS actors.
Separating Sovereignty From Sustainability
<mark>Fishing is equally important to other</mark> SCS claimant<mark>s</mark>. The Philippines employ some 1.5 million traditional fishermen and the industry accounts for 2.7 percent of national GDP, with three-fourths of the total fishing production from the SCS. Fish comprises some 35.3 percent of all animal proteins consumed in Vietnam and in the Philippines and Indonesia that number is even higher—42.6 percent and 57.3 percent respectively. As one Filipino senator put it, retaining <mark>access</mark> to fisheries in the face of Chinese advances <mark>is</mark> not just <mark>a matter of</mark> economics, but of <mark>“starvation.”</mark>
Given the importance of fish to the region, <mark>unilateral Chinese actions</mark> are unlikely to <mark>produce</mark> a stable and sustainable status quo, especially given <mark>increasingly negative reactions</mark> to Chinese assertive SCS policies <mark>by</mark> claimant states and <mark>major regional powers.</mark> Multilateral governance is needed. Whereas RFMOs typically provide such governance, a new SCS Fishery Management Organization (SCSFMO) may be problematic for several reasons. For one, RFMO’s are founded on UNCLOS provisions, which have become a proxy battleground for sovereignty issues. China’s rejection of the UNCLOS tribunal’s arbitration ruling makes an UNCLOS-based solution harder for Beijing to accept. Moreover, RFMOs that manage fish stocks—as opposed to migratory species—only operate in waters outside of countries’ 200 nautical mile EEZs. Were The Hague’s ruling universally accepted, organizing a SCSFMO would be fairly straightforward; countries would individually manage their own EEZs and jointly manage the remaining international seas. Because some 65 percent of the SCS Large Marine Ecosystem (LME) is disputed however, an effective SCSFMO would need to transcend disputed maritime boundaries to provide effective governance.
A Way Forward
If dwindling fisheries are significant drivers of regional competition, <mark>there may be a silver lining</mark> that gives some grounds for optimism. <mark>Fish are</mark> much more <mark>tangible objects of negotiation</mark> than sovereignty or historical ownership claims. Claimant states, and China in particular, must work to resolve the tensions between pursuing maritime sustainability and retaining unlimited access. Durable regional solutions must begin with domestic approaches that work to sustainably supply China’s fish food demand and provide employment alternatives to millions of over-subsidized Chinese fishermen. Allowing domestic groups to leverage sovereignty narratives to advance their individual interest impedes constructive regional solutions and works against China’s broader national interests.
Sovereignty and sustainability need to be separated in the South China Sea. <mark>Claimants might explore</mark> multilateral options under <mark>UNCLOS Section 197</mark>, <mark>which mandates</mark> that <mark>regions</mark> “shall” <mark>cooperate</mark> as required <mark>to</mark> formulate and elaborate “international rules, standards, and recommended practices and procedures… for the <mark>protect</mark>ion and preservation of <mark>the marine environment</mark>, taking into account characteristic regional features.” Such regional cooperation is urgent and necessary, <mark>even when</mark> the “characteristic regional <mark>features</mark>” <mark>include intractable</mark> sovereignty <mark>disputes. Deng Xiaoping’s</mark> 1979 <mark>proposal to</mark> shelve sovereignty disputes and <mark>pursue joint development of resources</mark> might <mark>provide</mark> a <mark>political basis for Beijing</mark> to pursue this approach—especially <mark>if</mark> it is <mark>framed</mark> <mark>as</mark> the <mark>necessary</mark> condition <mark>for continued</mark> Chinese <mark>access to</mark> sustainable <mark>fisheries</mark> in the SCS that can help meet future demand.
Ultimately, the region’s current free-for-all approach to resource management is ineffective at best and disastrous at worst. The latter scenario appears more likely. If, however, claimants can set aside intractable border disputes to negotiate inclusive fishery management, SCS fish will remain on menus for generations to come. Just as importantly, the <mark>intensity of</mark> sovereignty <mark>disputes</mark> may <mark>ease if claimants</mark> can <mark>disarm</mark> some of the <mark>underlying</mark> <mark>tension</mark> stemming <mark>from fishery mismanagement.</mark>  An important part of Asia’s dinner plate—not to mention <mark>regional harmony</mark>—<mark>is at stake.</mark>

Prioritizing Economic Development
Diversionary war theory posits that incompetent leaders tend to invoke foreign policy threats to divert public attention from their domestic turmoil.28 Some China analysts express concerns that during times of economic difficulties, China may engage in war-prone diplomatic assertiveness as a means to distract from domestic turmoil and rally popular support. As foreign policy experts Michael <mark>Beckley</mark> and Hal Brands write, “Rising powers become most aggressive when their fortunes fade, their difficulties multiply, and they realize they must achieve their ambitions now or miss the chance to do so forever. China has already started down this path.”29 However, upon closer examination, these claims <mark>may not hold true in</mark> the case of <mark>China, which has consistently reacted</mark> to domestic turmoil <mark>by prioritizing cooperative economic development rather than</mark> nationalist territorial <mark>expansion.</mark>
Echoes of History
Since the 1950s, according to MIT expert M. Taylor Fravel, Chinese <mark>leaders “have encountered strong</mark> domestic <mark>incentives to engage in diversionary crises</mark> or wars <mark>but almost never do</mark> so.<mark>”</mark>30 On territorial disputes, he argues, domestic <mark>insecurity creates stronger incentives</mark> for leaders <mark>to compromise</mark> with competing territorial claimants <mark>so</mark> that <mark>Beijing can</mark> secure the borders and concentrate resources to <mark>address internal challenges</mark> to its regime security.31 Remarkably, during Mao’s era, China successfully resolved most of its border disputes with neighboring countries.
In the late 1970s, <mark>when the</mark> Chinese <mark>economy was on</mark> the <mark>brink of collapse,</mark> Deng <mark>Xiaoping introduced a</mark> <mark>strategic approach</mark> aimed at resolving territorial disputes in the South China Sea and East China Sea. He proposed <mark>shelving</mark> these <mark>disputes and fostering friendly relations with</mark> Southeast Asian <mark>countries</mark> and Japan. Additionally, Deng also advocated for the innovative principle of “One Country, Two Systems,” which allowed Hong Kong and Macau to retain their capitalist economies and way of life, initially facilitating their smooth transition back to Chinese sovereignty. Deng and his successor, Jiang Zemin, continued the pursuit of friendly and transactional relations with Taiwan under the framework of the “One China, Different Interpretations,” often referred to as the 1992 Consensus. This consensus acknowledges that there is only one “China,” but recognizes the differing interpretations of what “China” represents across the Taiwan Strait (i.e., the Republic of China vs. the People’s Republic of China). The Chinese leadership’s efforts to resolve or shelve these disputes were driven by a well-organized objective: creating a friendly international environment that is <mark>essential for domestic development</mark>.
China’s Diaoyu/Senkaku policy is indeed a case that illustrates its prioritization of economic development over accommodating nationalism in territorial disputes. Accordingly, Beijing has played down several crises caused by Chinese and Japanese nationalists’ moves to assert sovereignty claims. For instance, in September 1996, David Chan, a Hong Kong (HK) Baodiao activist, drowned while attempting to swim to the islands, which led to a large-scale Baodiao movement in HK. HK citizens and politicians pressured Beijing to stand firm against Japan’s “new invasion” of Chinese territories. Beijing had strong incentives to accommodate, as HK’s collective expressions of patriotism were unprecedented, and a stable transfer of HK sovereignty back to China, scheduled in 1997, might have been jeopardized if Beijing failed to address HK citizens’ concerns. However, Beijing’s response was limited to diplomatic protests and a naval exercise allegedly to warn Japan.32
The major rationale behind Chinese behaviors during this period was China’s need for Japanese aid and foreign direct investment (FDI) to boost its industrial development. In 1978, Deng and his colleagues recognized the importance of introducing foreign equipment and technologies to enhance China’s industrial productivity. However, China’s foreign reserves, which stood at a mere $2 billion USD at the time, were insufficient to meet the substantial financial requirements of importing new equipment. Contracts signed with Western countries for equipment imports in 1978 amounted to $6.36 billion USD, creating a significant funding gap. To address this gap, Deng turned to Japan for both private investment and official development assistance (ODA), a decision that proved to be wise. Japan provided substantial loans amounting to ¥303 billion Japanese Yen in 1979 to finance China’s major projects. From 1979 to 2005, Japanese ODA accounted for a remarkable 67.2 percent of all foreign government aid received by China. Notably, Japanese ODA played a critical role in financing China’s basic infrastructure investments, particularly in the 1990s, reaching its peak in 1994 at approximately 27 percent of China’s total budgetary investment on basic infrastructure.33
<mark>China</mark>’s strategy of diversionary peace <mark>is</mark> rooted in the <mark>understanding that</mark> economic <mark>development holds</mark> <mark>greater significance for</mark> the CCP’s <mark>legitimacy than</mark> pursuing <mark>assertive foreign policies</mark> to stoke domestic nationalism. <mark>Economic considerations</mark> <mark>outweigh</mark> the appeal of fervent <mark>nationalism, especially during</mark> periods of <mark>economic challenges.</mark> According to Professor Yan Xuetong, China has no intention of antagonizing the West, as “these exports [to the developed economies] are the lifeblood of the Chinese economy: they ensure a consistent trade surplus, and the jobs they create are a vital engine of domestic social stability.”34
China’s Diplomatic Reorientation
Over the past decade, Chinese diplomacy has undergone dramatic transformations, with the rise and subsequent recalibration of “wolf-warrior” diplomacy standing out as a defining phenomenon. Characterized by confrontational rhetoric, public rebukes of foreign governments, and frequent violations of established diplomatic protocol, this assertive style was embraced by a number of Chinese diplomats who sought to project national strength and defend China’s image with unprecedented bluntness. However, this approach drew widespread international criticism and increasingly strained China’s relations with the West.
Recognizing the potential economic and geopolitical costs of such combative diplomacy, Chinese <mark>leadership</mark> <mark>has</mark> gradually <mark>moved to moderate</mark> its diplomatic tone. In June 2021, <mark>Xi</mark> Jinping <mark>emphasized</mark> the importance of “adopting a narrative tone that reflects openness and confidence, yet conveys modesty and <mark>humility</mark>, in a bid to shape a reliable, admirable and respectable image of China.”35 The Wall Street Journal reported that this shift in approach has been <mark>driven by</mark> Beijing’s <mark>apprehension that</mark> its <mark>confrontational</mark> “wolf-warrior” <mark>diplomacy may</mark> exacerbate anti-China sentiment and potentially <mark>isolate the</mark> Chinese <mark>economy</mark>.36
Notably, outspoken and proactive <mark>diplomats known for</mark> their <mark>assertive</mark> wolf warrior <mark>style</mark>, such as former deputy spokesperson of the Chinese Foreign Ministry Zhao Lijian and former ambassador to Sweden Gui Congyou, <mark>have been retired or reassigned.</mark> Since 2022, <mark>China has been actively</mark> seeking to resume official and unofficial exchanges and <mark>trying to fix</mark> its broken <mark>relations</mark> with Europe, Canada, Japan, Australia, and India, and maintain stable working relations with the United States amid strategic competition. Since November 2023, China has unilaterally granted visa-free entry to citizens of forty-three countries including Germany, France, Italy, Australia, and Japan.37 This reflects a strategic effort <mark>to</mark> boost people-to-people exchanges, <mark>revitalize post-COVID economic engagement</mark>, and improve its global image amid rising geopolitical tensions.
China’s new charm offensive is targeting Japan as well. Xi has repeatedly stated his will to enhance China’s relations with Japan and met with Prime Minister Shinzo Abe ten times from 2014 to 2019. At the 2019 Buenos Aires APEC Summit, Xi told then-prime minister Abe that “China continues to welcome Japan to be part of China’s reform and opening up, sharing new opportunities of China’s development.”38 As mentioned, China and Japan have engaged in intensive minister-level meetings and social exchanges. Xi and then-prime minister Kishida’s meeting in November 2023 reaffirmed their commitment to “the comprehensive promotion of a mutually beneficial relationship based on common strategic interests.”39
A major impetus is both states’ willingness to improve their economic ties.40 Kishida and Xi agreed to establish a dialogue framework on trade, and their ministries in charge discussed several key issues such as the stability of the supply chain, export control of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, China’s entry into the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), and the acceptance of new members into the RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership).41 In January 2024, Chinese Premier Li Qiang met with Japanese business leaders, underscoring the significant role of economic and trade cooperation as “the ballast and propeller of bilateral relations,” two words echoing the previous language used to describe China-US relations in China’s official discourse prior to the two countries’ trade war.42 Additionally, China is active in promoting East Asia integration, partly to offset the economic disengagement between China and the United States. <mark>China, Japan, and Korea agreed to accelerate free</mark> <mark>trade negotiations in March</mark> 2025—which had been on pause since 2019 and were resumed in 2024—<mark>amid</mark> the growing concerns over renewed <mark>tariff</mark> conflict<mark>s</mark> triggered by a second Trump administration.
Unlike diversionary war theory, domestic challenges have actually generated pacifying effects over China’s foreign behavior. Conversely, Chinese leadership’s perceptions of favorable distribution of power in the international system—particularly since the 2008 financial crises—have reinforced a more assertive, albeit cautious, posture in the East China Sea and South China Sea.43 As pointed out by Aaron Friedberg, “<mark>When Beijing feels stronger it acts more assertively; when</mark> it feels weaker or more <mark>vulnerable it acts with greater caution and restraint.</mark>”44

Long History of Broken Agreements
In addition to Chinese laws that outline several human rights protections, Beijing has signed onto several international human rights and standards agreements that the regime continually violates, according to the report.
In 1979, Beijing ratified the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations which requires consular officers to respect the laws and regulations of the receiving state, and Chinese embassies in the United States have many times found to be engaging in the CCP’s transnational repression against critics of the regime, such as the high profile incident of instigating violence against peaceful protestors during the 2023 APEC summit in San Francisco, and carrying out espionage.
In 1981, it ratified the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, requiring a commitment to reverse laws or regulations that perpetuate racial discrimination. Yet the CCP is currently actively erasing minority cultures by forcing Tibetan, Uyghur, and Mongolian children to attend “colonial boarding schools” that teach a CCP-centric curriculum and penalize the use of native languages and cultural customs.
In 1984, Beijing ratified the Sino-British Joint Declaration, meant to guarantee Hong Kong “a high degree of autonomy” with independent executive, legislative, and judicial powers. In recent years, the world witnessed as the CCP cracked down on protesters in Hong Kong and the passing of vaguely worded national security laws that give the CCP sweeping power to target dissenters.
In 1988, the regime ratified the Convention Against Torture, but regular reports from human rights lawyers and religious prisoners show that the regime is continuing to torture political and religious prisoners such as the Uyghur Muslims and Falun Gong practitioners, which have found to be targets of live organ harvesting by the regime. Falun Gong, also known as Falun Dafa, is a spiritual practice that teachers truth, compassion, and tolerance. It was introduced to the public in China in the early 1990s and banned by the regime in 1999. The commission recommended passing bills introduced that would prohibit U.S. public funds from paying for medical care related to organ transplants obtained in China and sanction perpetrators of organ harvesting.
In 1996, it ratified the U.N. Convention on the Law of the Sea, which the regime has violated by building and militarizing artificial islands in contested territory in the South China Sea and increasing maritime aggression against Philippine vessels.
In 2001, Beijing ratified the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights, which includes agreements safeguarding fair labor practices. However, the only labor union in China is one led by the CCP, and the regime typically treats strikes as a criminal offense. Multiple investigations in recent years have also uncovered regime-imposed slave labor, including the exploitation of Uyghurs in Xinjiang.
An Environmental Justice Foundation investigation also revealed the forced labor of North Korean workers on Chinese fishing ships, and the Brazilian government has accused China’s BYD of employing at least 163 workers in “slave-like conditions” and withholding workers’ passports and salaries. The report also noted that workers are subjected to “excessive overtime practices,” including coffee farm workers in Yunnan who supply coffee to Starbucks and Nestle.
The covenant also protects parents’ freedom to choose schools to “ensure the religious and moral education of their children,” which the CCP continues to violate.
In 2022, the regime ratified the Forced Labor Convention, but the commission found that systemic forced labor practices involving Uyghurs and Turkic minorities have expanded in Xinjiang this past year. The United States government considers the CCP’s persecution of the Uyghur minority a genocide and has enacted law to prohibit the entry of goods made with forced labor in response. The commission recommended strengthening existing mechanisms and expediting the blacklisting of companies using forced labor and mandating transparency in supply chains. The vast majority of Chinese cotton is produced in Xinjiang, and U.S. lawmakers have pressed for better disclosures and due diligence from Chinese fashion retailers.
Seafood caught or processed with forced labor, mainly North Korean workers on Chinese vessels, has been found in the American market, according to investigations. Lawmakers have introduced a bill to prohibit Chinese origin seafood involving forced labor, and the commission recommended agencies prohibit all procurement of such seafood.

Primary resources, also known as natural resources, occur naturally and are derived from the environment in order to process them. Apart from resources such as water, air, sunlight, forest area or agricultural land, which exist as separate entities, other resources such as metals ores and fossil fuels have to be extracted from the soil to make them usable. Their value is predominantly determined by the relative scarcity of the resource in combination with its exploitability for industrial use. In modern industry, most natural resources are used as raw materials from which upstream materials and finished products are produced. As primary resources for energy and industrial production, they are a vital requirement of any economic activity.

<mark>Australia</mark>
Australia is a major rare earths producer, with significant operations at the Mount Weld mine (Lynas Rare Earths), which also processes rare earths at facilities in Malaysia and is expanding its domestic processing capacity. Australia is recognized as the largest non-Chinese supplier of rare earths and is investing in further refining and separation infrastructure257.
<mark>Myanmar</mark> (Burma)
Myanmar is a key supplier of medium to heavy rare earths, particularly to China. Most of its production is exported to China for processing, but the country has become a critical part of the global rare earth supply chain, especially for elements like dysprosium and terbium126.
<mark>Thailand</mark>
Thailand has rapidly increased its rare earth production in recent years and exports significant quantities to China. The country hosts both mining and some downstream processing facilities125.
Nigeria
<mark>Nigeria</mark> has emerged as a new player, significantly increasing its rare earth output. The country is in the early stages of developing both mining and processing capacity, with international partnerships to advance its industry26.
<mark>India</mark>
India holds substantial reserves and produces rare earths, mainly from beach sand minerals. While its production remains modest compared to its potential, India is investing in expanding both mining and processing capabilities, including plans for new alloy and magnet plants247.
Madagascar
Madagascar produces rare earths from the Ampasindava peninsula, which hosts significant ionic clay deposits. However, production has declined recently due to local opposition to mining activities2.
<mark>Brazil</mark>
Brazil has the world’s second-largest rare earth reserves and has recently begun commercial production at the Pela Ema deposit. The country is expected to become a more significant producer and processor in the coming years, with plans to produce all four key magnet rare earths (neodymium, praseodymium, terbium, dysprosium)47.
<mark>Russia</mark>
Russia has modest rare earth mining and processing capacity, with ongoing efforts to expand its role in the global market5.
