As a part of its efforts to gain supremacy in the microelectronics and next-generation power markets, the People’s Republic of China (PRC) has used its Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) to invest in critical mineral operations abroad. This paper investigates how these investments impact the subsequent trade of these critical minerals to China. Our previous work has examined the vulnerabilities present in the critical minerals supply chain based on trading patterns. In our Microelectronic Supply Chain Analysis series of white papers, Vulnerability (2025) and Lock-In and Substitutability (2025) have identified overly <mark>important actors</mark> in the critical mineral trade network, such as the Democratic Republic of the Congo (<mark>DRC</mark>) <mark>with cobalt or Turkey with boron</mark> products. Overreliance on these countries and their unique geological assets <mark>makes it difficult for China to find </mark>appropriate <mark>substitutes</mark> when challenges arise. However, this earlier work was limited to the insights trade data can provide and left questions about how these dependencies are formed and maintained. We have identified infrastructure investment as a key indication of relationships that will become highly dependent and would be resistant to substitutability. Investments support critical mineral trades by providing the capital needed to build necessary infrastructure that might not be attainable otherwise. But subsequently, the investment could create downstream vulnerabilities as it concentrates a country’s focus on the return of that investment. For example, in Indonesia, China spent over $600 million on a nickel-processing facility. This has allowed Indonesian mining to produce more nickel than it might have previously, and was only made possible by Chinese funding. As a result, China has an extra interest in recouping that investment by selling to Chinese manufacturers. This also supports Chinese manufacturing priorities, like their domination of lithium battery production. This investment is not usually a one-time payment. In most cases, China retains large portions of equity in the mining or processing of the backed mineral. Both the infrastructure investment and the retained equity create a relationship that pushes China to prioritize trade with their investee countries. These countries find that these investment and trade connections are very difficult to renegotiate in the future and place them in a disadvantaged position vis-a-vis China. We find that investment does lead to great increases in trade between the investee country and China. But importantly, we show the highly-reliant nature of these relationships, as the huge majority of <mark>Chinese imports of these minerals comes from the investee country, rather than from a variety of suppliers.</mark> In turn, the investee countries also find that they are dependent on Chinese buyers and struggle with exploitative contracts that they cannot renegotiate. We do not see the same type of investment relationships leading to increased trading power for the United States, at least based on our limited investigation of US resources.

<mark>Climate Change is Now on an Irreversible Course in the Short Run</mark>–Mounting Evidence In the last two decades, <mark>global</mark> carbon dioxide <mark>(CO2) emissions</mark> from fossil fuel burning, cement industries and land use change <mark>rose from 35.3</mark> GtCO2 <mark>in 2005 to 41.4 Gt</mark>CO2 <mark>in 2024</mark> (Global Carbon Budget). ⁋ Fossil CO2 emissions account for ~69% of the total emissions, driven primarily by energy and industrial sectors. Global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions were 57.7 GtCO2e in 2024, a 2.3% increase from 2023. Deforestation and land-use change accounted for 53% of the overall emissions increase (UNEP, 2025, Emissions Gap Report 2025). ⁋ Except for the EU, the emissions of the five of the six largest emitters (China, the USA, India, the EU, Russia, and Indonesia) increased in 2024. India had the highest absolute growth in emissions, followed by China and Indonesia. ⁋ <mark>To remain within</mark> the <mark>1.5 oC</mark> warming red line in 2100, <mark>emissions should drop by 8</mark>-10<mark>%</mark> pa between now and 2035 (UNEP, 2025. Emissions Gap Report 2025). Given the growing gap between emissions and what is desired to remain under the desired 1.5 oC warming, such a drastic drop is considered not feasible. ⁋ The global average atmospheric warming above pre-industrial levels was 1.45 °C in 2023 and 1.55 °C in 2024, the latter being the hottest year in the last 175 years (WMO, State of the Global Climate 2023 and 2024). ⁋ Global warming is predicted to have many impacts that will persist for a long time, even centuries after carbon emissions are reduced. The predictions for some climate impacts, and the time for recovery, if possible at all, for a ~3 oC warming are described below. ⁋ Warm-water coral reefs are in rapid decline. Over 99 % of warm-water coral reefs are expected to experience very frequent severe bleaching and be functionally lost already by a ~2 °C warming. At 3 °C, global coral reef collapse will happen. Coral reefs are very likely to undergo irreversible phase shifts beyond ~1.5 °C. Once species are extinct, full ecological recovery is impossible(AR6). ⁋ Between 1992-2018, Greenland lost 3.8 trillion tonnes of ice, raising the global sea level by 10.6 mm. Greenland tipping threshold is somewhere between ~1–3 °C warming; a sustained 3 °C warming will cause a large, mostly irreversible, mass loss over the coming millennia (Multistability and transient response of Greenland Ice). If fully melted, the Greenland ice sheet could raise global sea levels by ~7.4 m. Even if temperatures fall subsequently, the Greenland Ice Cap is unlikely to fully recover even after 10,000 years, making this impact virtually irreversible (Greenland ice loss much faster than expected). ⁋ If large volumes of meltwater from the Greenland ice sheet enter the North Atlantic Ocean, the surface salinity of the Labrador and Irminger seas will be lowered. That will weaken the sinking branch of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC), a circulating surface and deep-sea ocean current that connects the major oceans of the world, and distributes heat from the equator towards the poles and influences climate on all the continents. A weaker AMOC will alter global heat distribution and regional climate and storm patterns, i.e., a weakened AMOC has the potential to alter global climate. ⁋ By 2100, in a high emissions scenario, the AMOC is predicted to weaken by 24-39%. Even if the emissions decrease, the AMOC is expected to weaken substantially (Climate impact of a weakened AMOC in a warming climate). A recent study finds the tipping threshold for AMOC collapse to be around 3 °C warming with a lower bound of ~2.2 °C (Substantial Risk of 21st Century AMOC Tipping even under Moderate Climate Change). ⁋ If there is no AMOC collapse, it may partially recover in 100-200 years, but may not recover fully to its original strength, even after a millennium. However, if it collapses, it may recover partially after centuries, and the collapse may be irreversible in human timescales. ⁋ <mark>Warming has</mark> begun to <mark>release methane</mark> (CH4) <mark>from the permafrost</mark> in Siberia, Northern Canada and Alaska, and from undersea methane hydrates, where an estimated 4,000 GtC are stored (CH4 hasa global warming potential that is 1-2 orders of magnitude higher than that of CO2). In a 3–5 °C warming, up to ~90 % of near-surface permafrost area can thaw in many regions, turning them into net carbon sources. Permafrost carbon <mark>losses are irreversible</mark> even on centennial to millennial timescales (Review of permafrost science in IPCC’s AR6 WG1). ⁋ A large fraction of the world’s ~215,000 glaciers are already committed to loss due to warming. At a ~2.7–3 °C warming, up to ~75 % of these glaciers and a large share of ice volume will be eventually lost (IPCC). Even if temperatures drop below 1.5 oC warming, changes of glacier mass will be irreversible for centuries (Irreversible glacier change and trough water after overshooting 1.5 oC). ⁋ A warming of ~3 °C by 2100 will cause a global mean sea-level rise of ~0.6–1.0 m(AR6). After a 3 °C warming peak, sea level will keep rising for millennia, potentially reaching several metres higher even if temperatures fall modestly (The multi-millennial sea level commitment of global warming). Sea rise will leave thousands of climate refugees permanently homeless in the small island nations and low-lying lands in countries such as Bangladesh. ⁋ Ocean pH has already fallen by ~0.1 pH since pre-industrial; continued high emissions up to 2100 will drop it further by 0.1–0.3 pH (NOAA). This will severely impact calcifying organisms (corals, molluscs, some plankton) and the ecosystem structure (Ocean acidification and climate change: advances in ecology and evolution). ⁋ Wildfire risk and burned area increase strongly with warming. The global annual area of forest disturbance due to fire in 2023 and 2024 was 36.6 and 38.3 million ha, respectively. Compared to the 2002–2022 average, annual forest disturbance due to fire, the 2023–2024 disturbance was 2.2 times higher globally, and 3 times higher in the Tropics. More than 25% of all 2024 forest disturbance from fire occurred in tropical forests (Unprecedentedly high global forest disturbance due to fire in 2023 and 2024). ⁋ While the frequency of fires may decline rapidly with a return to pre-industrial climate, repeated burning will trigger ecosystem changes—e.g., boreal forest changes to shrubland/grassland, Amazon forest dieback in some regions may be hard or impossible to reverse on timescales less than centuries (IPCC AR6). ⁋ The WMO has identified global drought hotspots all across the world: in Africa, in the Mediterranean region, in South America, and in South and Southeast Asia. Data on drought trends are scanty. One study (Global Health Emergencies of Extreme Drought Events) estimates that between 2000 and 2023, 1.6 billion people were affected by drought globally. FAO notes that drought is the leading cause of agricultural loss and affects the livelihoods of large parts of the population, especially in developing countries. A consequence of drought is global hunger, and with that, global unrest and global conflict. ⁋ With rising emissions, droughts are expected to increase in intensity and severity in future (Global drought trends and future projections). A 3 oC warming will increase the frequency of drought. While drought frequency may decrease when temperatures drop, the consequences of drought–groundwater depletion, soil degradation, vegetation changes, and desertification are much slower to reverse, possibly centuries, if reversal happens at all. ⁋ The increase in intensity and frequency of storms and superstorms around the world is a result of oceanic warming. The average number of Category 4 (200-250 km/hr windspeeds) and Category 5 (>250 km/hr windspeeds) storms has increased from an average of 15 storms per year during the period 2000-2004 to an average of 21 such storms per year during 2020-2024 (IBTrACS). ⁋ Storms correlate directly with global temperature and ocean heat content. When temperatures stabilise or decline, storm patterns will adjust over a few decades, but with permanent changes to coastlines, infrastructure and ecosystems. ⁋ The year 2021 saw the second-largest number of floods recorded globally in the period 1990 to 2023 (WMO). In 2020, South Asia had devastating floods, and in 2022, Pakistan floods inundated a third of the country and affected 33 million people. In 2023, Italy was affected by floods and in the next year, Spain was inundated. In the period 2022-24, Niger, Chad, Nigeria, Somalia, and Tanzania experienced floods. India, in particular, has experienced floods and landslides after heavy rainfall events, which have caused heavy loss of life, e.g., more than 300 lives were lost in the 2024 Wayanad Landslide Disaster. ⁋ At 2-4 oC warming, heavy rainfall events are expected to become roughly 1.7–2.7 times more frequent and 14–30% more intense (IPCC). As warming decreases, extreme rainfall events will revert to the pre-industrial era pattern over a period of a few decades, but changes to river morphology, sedimentation, changes in floodplains, and loss of wetlands imply that pre-industrial flood risk is not recoverable just by changing the climate. ⁋ Heat is among the biggest direct health risks, especially in South Asia, the Middle East, and parts of Africa (WHO-10 Key Heat Health Messages from the IPCC AR6 Report). Once global mean temperatures peak and decline, heatwaves will decline on decadal scales, but a return to pre-industrial heat risk levels is impossible unless all warming is reversed. ⁋ The Arctic ice extent in September of any year, when it is at its minimum, shrank from 6 million km2 in 2004 to 4.28 million km2 in 2024, a 28% loss in area in 20 years (National Snow and Ice Data Center, CIRES, University of Colorado, Boulder). At 3 °C warming, the Arctic will be practically sea ice-free in September in most years, with progressively longer ice-free seasons with more warming (AR6). ⁋ Loss of Arctic ice extent changes the reflectance (albedo) of the Arctic waters, resulting in significantly greater absorption of incoming radiation in the Arctic Ocean and adjacent seas like the Kara Sea off Siberia. ⁋ Sea ice is a fast-response cryosphere component. If atmospheric CO₂ is brought back down, Arctic sea ice cover can regrow on decadal scales (Regionally-Dependent Arctic Sea Ice Recovery to CO2 Removal). ⁋ Climate change has already increased the burden of heat-related mortality, vector-borne, food, and water-borne diseases. At 3 oC warming, climate-related health risks will increase further (IPCC). ⁋ If the climate cools, some vectors and pathogens could decrease over decades, but they do not contract fully. Climate-related health risks can be reduced substantially within decades through adaptation and mitigation measures, but a return to pre-industrial disease patterns is impossible, as the ecological and social systems would have changed significantly. ⁋ There are many other climate-related impacts for which forecasting how they may pan out is beyond the scope of this document, e.g., an increase in desertification, which may take centuries to reverse, if it reverses at all, an increase in marine heatwaves, loss of food and water security, social changes, indebtedness, political changes within and between countries. ⁋ After a 3 °C temperature rise by 2100, even if emissions fall, the climate system will not revert to its original state.
