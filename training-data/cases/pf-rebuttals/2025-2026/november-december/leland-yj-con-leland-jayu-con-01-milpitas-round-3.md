<mark>ON Doctors</mark>
[Delink] - Doctor shortages are a result of a laundry list of structural flaws
[Herrick, Clare, and David Armstrong. [King's College London, London, United Kingdom, Medicineat United Medical and Dental Schools, United Kingdom] “How Many Doctors Does a Health System Need? Histories of Workforce Planning in the NHS.” Social Science & Medicine, Feb. 2025, p. 117882, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.socscimed.2025.117882.] sandy + g^2 :)
It is always tempting to see present day crises as aberrations, but our historical analysis suggests that <mark>workforce shortages</mark> have <mark>been</mark> <mark>a consistent structural problem of</mark> the <mark>NHS</mark>. Indeed, efforts to calculate and plan the number of doctors needed by the NHS have been ‘ad hoc’ (Maynard and Walker, 1977, 62), complicated by the concept of ‘need’ itself and limited by a lack of basic data and studies of what doctors actually do. And, despite the NHS being the single most significant sector of UK government spending, <mark>workforce planning has</mark> ironically and consistently <mark>occurred in isolation from financial planning.</mark> The basic question of ‘how many doctors will be needed in future years’ (Milne, 1978, 1032) has consequently never really been properly tethered to the question of ‘how many doctors are we likely to be able to afford?’ (Ibid.) And, as Anthony Culyer controversially noted five decades ago, ‘the NHS does not exist … to give employment to doctors, but to give effective care to patients’ (1976, 72). Thus, while doctors' pay has been a consistent feature of professional, public and political debate in the UK for as long as the NHS has existed (see for example Richard Titmuss and Brian Abel-Smith's 1956 study of the cost of the NHS), rarely have these tethered individual pay to the total cost of salaries to the NHS and, moreover, implications for general taxation rates (Herrick, 2025).
As our analysis shows, workforce planning maybe a sporadic and neglected arena of public policy in the UK, but its effects are deeply felt and its contours intensely cultural and political. As a result, our animating question of how many doctors are needed really and importantly, ‘depends on who you ask’ (Culyer, 1976, 71). As an example, a 1974 report by the Hospital Consultant and Specialists Association concluded that hospitals were facing a shortage of 20,000 doctors, a figure wholly derived from a questionnaire sent to their members. This asked one question: ‘based on the current workload of a typical district general hospital, how many doctors would be needed to handle it?’ (Lancet Editors, 1974, 1151). The answer that came back was a rather inexact ‘twice as many as there are at present’ (Ibid). The consultants' <mark>calculation was unrelated to medical need and instead a reflection of their current working conditions.</mark> While this study was at the more unscientific end of the methodological spectrum, it does illustrate the complexities of normative staffing calculations that can simultaneously address medical need, optimise the patient experience, and provide clinicians with a meaningful career. As our analysis as shown, while it has almost always been the case that calls have been for more doctors, there have been notable <mark>moments when too many doctors were equally seen as a professional threat</mark>. And yet none of these situations holds the answer to how many doctors are needed. This is not a question that plagues NHS alone. Indeed, even the world's most “effective” healthcare systems, such as Germany, are struggling to meet demand and address clinical need amid ever-increasing costs (Anandaciva, 2023).
What is distinctive about the history of workforce planning in the NHS is how it reveals as much about the acceptable parameters of political debate as it does the complexities of estimating future clinical need. And, while health workforce shortages are a shared feature of healthcare systems across the world, the NHS highlights the political stakes of drawing on the example of other healthcare systems in workforce planning. Indeed, Maynard and Walker's contribution to the Royal Commission on the NHS in 1978 cautioned against the use of ‘naïve international comparisons’ when forecasting future manpower needs (1978, 13). This caution has remained. For example, in Bloor and Maynard's 2003 report on international workforce planning they argued that drawing meaningful comparisons between countries to calculate future doctor numbers was fraught with jeopardy given national differences in clinical practice, medical infrastructure, health system funding, doctor payment, and different forms of labour and capital substitution. The idea that the number of doctors in the NHS needed to be compared to other countries really only become a feature of workforce planning discourse with the election of New Labour whose NHS Plan acknowledged that the government was ‘conscious that England has fewer doctors per head of population than many other Western Countries … due to a long-term bias towards undersupply’ (2000, 24).
The increasing use of international comparative data shows that the NHS is distinctive in its exceptionally high universal health coverage rates (the seventh highest in the world), but also for having comparatively low capital investment in infrastructure, relatively poor health outcomes compared to its European neighbours and an exceptionally high reliance on overseas staff. More broadly, this data has also served a political purpose. From the early 2000s, the number of doctors relative to other OECD countries became an increasingly common metric to illustrate the need for more doctors, especially by the BMA (British Medical Association, 2022). Yet the use of OECD averages as the base metric by which to assess present doctor shortages and future need (ibid) masks profound inconsistencies between patient outcomes and staffing. For example, research by the King's Fund shows that while the number of NHS doctors (3 per 1,000 population) may be the fourth lowest of the countries analysed in 2019; <mark>UK</mark> avoidable <mark>mortality rates</mark> (69 per 100,0000) <mark>equalled</mark> those of <mark>Greece</mark>, a country <mark>with twice as many doctors</mark> (Mallorie, 2023). To further increase the confusion, Japan enjoyed both the lowest levels of avoidable mortality (47 per 100,000) and the lowest number of doctors (Ibid). There has never been and there remains no easy answer to the question of how many doctors the NHS needs. Instead, and as we have explored here, it is likely that present numbers will always be deemed imperfect for the medical profession and inadequate for patients.
