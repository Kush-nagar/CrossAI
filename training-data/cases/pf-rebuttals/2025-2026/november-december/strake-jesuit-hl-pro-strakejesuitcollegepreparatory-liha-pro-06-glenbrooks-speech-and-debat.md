<mark>Americans remain divided over</mark> whether <mark>government</mark> should be able to <mark>access</mark> encrypted communications
The issue of encryption – specifically, whether or not the government should legally be able to bypass or decode encrypted communications when investigating criminal cases – has long been a hot-button topic in the ongoing debate over the appropriate balance between individual privacy concerns and the needs of law enforcement in the digital age. This issue again became front page news in 2016, when the FBI obtained a court order to compel Apple to unlock the iPhone of one of the perpetrators of the mass shooting in San Bernadino, California.
In a Pew Research Center survey conducted at that time, <mark>51% of Americans felt</mark> that <mark>Apple should be required to unlock</mark> the <mark>iPhone at</mark> the <mark>FBI’s request</mark>, while <mark>38% felt</mark> that Apple should <mark>not</mark> be required to do this.
This survey posed a more general question (with two competing statements) about the tradeoffs between security and privacy, and it finds that Americans’ views on this subject remain divided. Fully <mark>46%</mark> of Americans <mark>agree</mark> with the statement: “<mark>The government should be able to access encrypted communications</mark> when investigating crimes.” On the other hand, a comparable share (<mark>44%</mark>) <mark>agrees</mark> that “technology <mark>companies should be able to use encryption</mark> technology <mark>that is unbreakable</mark>, even to law enforcement.” An additional 4% of Americans volunteer that their answer to this question depends on the circumstances.

3.2. Weakening of encryption
At the same time, there is also a push from states in the opposite direction – attacking instead of strengthening encryption. To be clear, I'm not saying that all these attacks are unlawful – with the right warrant, some of them might be in line with human rights requirements. The framework for this analysis will be provided in this article. For now, it's important to gain a better understanding of what governments are doing in this realm.
Firstly, some <mark>governments</mark> have over the past years <mark>invest</mark>ed <mark>heavily in</mark> undermining these technologies. For example, the US spying agency NSA (under the Bullrun project) and the UK spying agency GCHQ (under the Edgehill project) have <mark>secret</mark>ly weakened the effectiveness of widely used encryption technologies.21 Wired calculated that around USD <mark>11 billion is allocated</mark> to these activities <mark>by the NSA alone</mark>.22 One of the more important ways in which they achieved these feats, is by undermining encryption standards set in international fora – by suggesting technical changes to algorithms which are actually vulnerabilities, but which the other participants would not recognise as such.23 And while the Snowden disclosures provide evidence of the NSA’s and GCHQ’s attempts to weaken encryption technologies, this does not imply that the UK and US are the only countries doing so. It is quite likely that other countries running significant offensive cybersecurity operations, such as China, Russia and Israel, do the same.
Parallel to this, <mark>goverments decrypt</mark> information <mark>through brute force</mark> attacks – that is, by using extensive amounts of computing power to try or discover every possible key. In 2020, EuroPol and the European Commission together launched a new decryption platform, the result of an investment from the European Commission in decryption capabilities.24 States, including the EU, Germany, the UK and the Netherlands, are also investing in the development of <mark>quantum computers</mark>, which <mark>in the coming</mark> 10–15 <mark>years may break</mark> a commonly used form of <mark>encryption</mark>.25
And <mark>governments have attacked</mark> parts of <mark>the system</mark> in which encryption <mark>technologies are embedded</mark>, for example by hacking secure chat services. Dutch law enforcement revealed in 2020 that it was able to intercept over 20 million encrypted chat messages, between suspects, sometimes in real time, because it had been able to infiltrate an encrypted chat service provider that was popular with criminals called EncroChat.26 Criminals then switched to encrypted communications service Sky ECC, and the judicial authorities in Belgium, France and the Netherlands started monitoring that service.27 After that service was shut down, the FBI, DEA, Dutch police and the Swedish police together with 16 other countries developed <mark>and covertly operated their own encrypted communications company</mark>, to ensure that criminals would switch to another service they could monitor.28 Undoubtedly, this cat-and-mouse game is playing out further as this article is being written.

<mark>Individuals</mark> from opposite ends of the world communicate seamlessly over the Internet using several choices of popular messengers like Signal, Whatsapp, or Telegram. We innately <mark>expect</mark> such conversations we have over the Internet to have the same <mark>privacy</mark> as us conversing with our correspondent in person in a private space. The <mark>Snowden</mark> revelations from 2013 [68], however, <mark>revealed</mark> that <mark>this</mark> expectation <mark>was fallacious</mark>, resulting in a widespread call to arms within the security and privacy community to proactively resist mass surveillance [1, 60]. In practice, this eventually culminated with the adoption of end-to-end encryption as a standard for the vast majority of communications over the Internet [35, 66, 75]. <mark>E</mark>nd <mark>to</mark>-<mark>e</mark>nd <mark>encryption</mark> effectively hides the contents of any communication between two parties from network adversaries. Nonetheless, by virtue of how these communications tools operate, they <mark>inherently leak</mark> the <mark>existence of</mark> a <mark>conversation and</mark> the <mark>metadata of</mark> who conversed with whom, when, and how much did they converse. Unfortunately, in the era of global surveillance that we live in, this metadata might be sufficient for terrible consequences [3, 52]. Free and democratic societies depend on an informed public, which depends on <mark>whistleblowers</mark> shedding light on misdeeds and corruption [13, 68]. Imagine the plight of a whistleblower, faced with the daunting task of publishing confidential documents that would expose acts of incompetence, fraud, or inhumane practices carried out by their government or employer that thrives on deceiving the public. Any action in such a direction often results in incarceration or risks to their lives [9, 74]. The same is true of individuals in oppressive regimes whose lives are endangered by their mere sexual orientation or political stance [26, 59]. Protecting individuals’ communications requires a communication system with strong security and privacy guarantees for both data and metadata. ¶ Related Work. In order to tackle the problem of metadata leakage, <mark>various</mark> forms of anonymous communication <mark>networks</mark> (ACNs) <mark>with</mark> varying degrees of <mark>metadata protections</mark> and design goals have been <mark>proposed</mark>. <mark>In practice users</mark> are <mark>limited to</mark> a few choices like Tor [27] or I2P [78]. These systems are designed to enable their users to protect their communications from a local eavesdropping network adversary like the user’s Internet Service Provider (ISP). These <mark>systems</mark> have low latencies, but they are innately <mark>susceptible to traffic</mark> analysis <mark>attacks</mark> by a global passive network adversary. Moreover, <mark>recent attacks</mark> have surfaced that <mark>make a</mark> Tor <mark>user</mark>’s Internet usage <mark>susceptible to privacy breaches</mark> by even local network adversaries [37, 54, 73]. Cwtch [43] internally leverages Tor’s v3 onion services protocol to facilitate peer-to-peer communications, and consequently inherits the privacy limitations of the Tor network, and similarly I2P’s threat model too is limited to local adversaries.
