2. The US is experiencing a bottom-half downturn---“tech growth” that their UQ says is holding up the economy is unsustainable.
Shukla 25 [Piyush Shukla is a digital news writer that attended the National Institute of Technology, 11/14/25, "Recession warning: The bottom half of America’s K-shaped economy is starting to crack", Economic Times, https://economictimes.indiatimes.com/news/international/us/recession-warning-the-bottom-half-of-americas-k-shaped-economy-is-starting-to-crack/articleshow/125332742.cms, DOA: 11/18/25] phab
A <mark>recession is brewing</mark> at the bottom of the K-shaped economy, and the first signs are already visible in the data. The split is widening fast. The top half is spending confidently. The <mark>bottom half is slipping into contraction</mark>. And the numbers now show a clear economic divergence that is hard to ignore.
The warning starts with consumer stress. <mark>Lower-income households</mark> are <mark>running out of</mark> pandemic <mark>savings.</mark> Credit card balances are at record highs. Delinquencies on auto loans and personal loans are climbing at the fastest pace in years. Monthly budgets are getting tighter. Many <mark>families</mark> now <mark>spend more on debt</mark> payments <mark>than on essential</mark> good<mark>s</mark>. <mark>This</mark> is the kind of financial <mark>strain</mark> that <mark>typically appears </mark>months <mark>before</mark> a formal <mark>recession</mark> shows up in national data.
The top of the K still looks strong. Wealthier Americans continue to drive travel, luxury spending, home upgrades and tech demand. High asset values in homes and stocks keep confidence elevated. Many in this group locked in low mortgage rates and carry less variable debt, giving them a cushion against rising borrowing costs. Their spending props up national retail numbers and masks the slowdown forming underneath. But this strength is concentrated, not broad-based, and cannot hold the economy alone if the bottom half keeps weakening.
The lower half is absorbing the hit from sticky prices. Inflation may be lower than last year, but key essentials remain sharply higher than pre-2020 levels. Groceries, rent, utilities and transportation costs continue to push household budgets to the edge. <mark>Wage growth is slowing</mark> at the same time. Jobs in retail, hospitality and small-service sectors are stabilizing but not accelerating. Pay increases are no longer keeping up with the cost of living. The math is breaking for millions of working-class families.
Spending patterns confirm the shift. Data shows a clear drop in discretionary purchases among lower-income consumers. Restaurant visits are declining. Local retail foot traffic is weakening. Spending on personal services, low-tier travel, clothing and home goods is falling. These <mark>pullbacks are early</mark> recession <mark>markers</mark>. Economists call this a “bottom-half downturn”—a contraction that starts quietly with households that have the least financial room to absorb rising costs.
<mark>Businesses are noticing</mark> the <mark>slowdown</mark>. Small shops report softer demand and higher expenses. Many face a double squeeze: costs remain elevated, and customer flow is falling. Restaurants, salons, repair services and family-owned retailers are operating on thinner margins. If the spending slowdown worsens, these businesses <mark>could reduce hours or cut staff, triggering the next stage</mark> of the cycle—job losses that further weaken local economies.
The danger is how fast the gap is widening. The K-shaped divide is no longer just a post-pandemic concept. It now shows up in credit data, wage gains, savings levels, and spending behavior. The top leg rises. The bottom leg bends downward. When this separation accelerates, it creates conditions where a recession can exist for one group even while the broader economy appears healthy.
This divergence carries national risk. If lower-income households continue to pull back, the sectors that depend on high-volume spending could deteriorate. This would <mark>drag down GDP growth, weaken job creation and slow state tax revenue</mark>. A narrow recovery driven mostly by wealthy consumers is historically unstable. It can hold for a while. But it rarely lasts when the broader base is under stress.
Economists are now watching several pressure points. Rising delinquencies. Slowing wage growth. Falling savings. Declining foot traffic. Softening discretionary spending. All of these indicators typically move before headline data turns negative. And they are already signaling escalation.
The question is not whether the bottom half is in recession. Many analysts argue it already is. The question is whether that downturn spreads upward. If the strain continues, the slowdown will not stay contained. It will feed into job markets, local businesses and mid-tier sectors that rely on broad consumer demand<mark>. A top-heavy recovery cannot shield the economy forever.</mark> The US is not in an official recession. But for millions of households at the bottom of the K, the downturn has already arrived. The data is clear. The stress is rising. And unless conditions shift, the broader economy may soon feel it too.
Why is the top of the K still strong?
A recession is forming at the lower end of the US K-shaped economy, and the latest numbers show the strain spreading quickly. Lower-income households have nearly exhausted their excess savings, with estimates showing that the bottom 80% have less cash buffer than before the pandemic.
Credit card balances are rising and now sit above $1.3 trillion, while 90-day-plus delinquency rates have climbed at the fastest pace since 2009. Auto loan delinquencies are also rising, hitting younger and lower-income borrowers hardest. Wage growth has slowed sharply from its mid-2022 peak, falling back toward pre-pandemic levels at a time when core essentials remain elevated.
Housing costs are still up more than 20% from 2020 levels. Grocery inflation remains sticky even after headline improvements. Utility bills in many cities remain higher than historical averages. These pressures are squeezing disposable income, and the impact is showing up in spending data.
Working-class households have begun cutting back on restaurants, apparel, home goods, and local services. Transaction volumes at discount retailers are rising, but average ticket sizes are falling, suggesting shoppers are trading down and buying less. The slowdown is not visible in headline GDP because high-income households, supported by stock market gains and home-equity strength, continue to spend firmly. Their spending masks the downturn occurring underneath.
But early recessionary markers are now visible: falling real disposable income for the lower half, a decline in discretionary outlays, weakening small-business revenue in service-heavy neighborhoods, and a pickup in short-term borrowing. Payroll growth in lower-wage service sectors has softened, and job openings in retail and food services have dropped.
Consumer sentiment among households making under $50,000 has fallen to multi-month lows. Student loan payments have added strain for borrowers in their 20s and 30s, with delinquency risks building again. Household debt overall has climbed to nearly $18.6 trillion, and the share of income required for debt servicing is rising fastest for the bottom income tiers.

Wagner ND [Ann Wagner, ND, “Lawful Access to Encrypted Data Act,” house.gov, https://wagner.house.gov/sites/evo-subsites/wagner.house.gov/files/Lawful%20Access%20to%20Encrypted%20Data%20Act%20Final%20One%20Pager.pdf, DOA: 11/18/25] phab
The Lawful Access to Encrypted Data Act balances consumer privacy with public safety and includes three components:
LAWFUL ACCESS
<mark>Mandates</mark> service providers and device manufacturers to provide assistance, pursuant to a warrant and appropriate legal process, required <mark>to recover encrypted information</mark> stored on a device, operating system, or remote computing service, or access encrypted information in transit over a wire or electronic communication service.
Authorizes the Attorney General to issue directives to service providers and device manufacturers to report on technical capabilities to comply with court orders and timelines for developing and deploying those capabilities.
The Attorney General may not specify the technical means needed to implement the required capabilities.
Anyone issued a directive may petition a federal court to set aside the directive by proving by clear and convincing evidence that it is scientifically impossible to comply with the directive, or that the directive is otherwise unlawful.
<mark>DOJ would be responsible for compensating anyone subject to</mark> a directive for <mark>reasonable expenses</mark> incurred in complying with the directive.
PRIZE COMPETITION
Provides <mark>$50 million authorization</mark> for the Attorney General to design a prize competition <mark>to incentivize creative technological innovation in developing lawful access solutions</mark> in encrypted technology, while maximizing privacy and security.
LAWFUL ACCESS TRAINING
Authorizes <mark>$50 million</mark>, contingent on solicitations matching the authorization amount, <mark>to create a training program and real-time assistance for law enforcement</mark> personnel at the National Domestic Communications Assistance Center (NDCAC).
