US companies break encryption themselves.
Leetaru 19 [Kalev Leetaru, Forbes Contributor, 3-24-2019, "Silicon Valley - Not Governments - Will Kill Encryption," Forbes, https://www.forbes.com/sites/kalevleetaru/2019/03/24/silicon-valley-not-governments-will-kill-encryption/, Willie T.]
Almost since the dawn of consumer encryption, governments across the world have feared its ability to shield the communications of their citizenry from mass surveillance. Silicon Valley has long been a flagbearer of the encrypted revolution, wielding it as a tool against intrusive governments as it rolled out ubiquitous HTTPS and consumer end-to-end <mark>encryption</mark> and refused the growing deluge of requests to build in backdoors. Yet, as these early efforts increasingly <mark>collide with</mark> companies’ own <mark>bottom lines</mark>, it is likely that it will be <mark>Silicon Valley</mark> that once again takes the lead, this time to <mark>roll back</mark> the protections of <mark>encryption</mark>, not for the needs of governments to combat terrorists and criminals, but for their own profit-minded needs to continue mining, monetizing and manipulating their users.
Silicon Valley has long embraced encryption as a way of securing internet communications from mass government surveillance and ordinary cybercriminals. One of the reasons for this early support was the fact that encryption was a value-add that had no impact on their own use of their users’ data. The Edward Snowden disclosures were an added incentive to move to securing their communications in transit, allowing them to claim to be standing up to governments on behalf of their users, while in reality encouraging their users to share ever more intimate information to be mined.
The movement from HTTP to HTTPS was an easy sell for the major internet companies in that the immense cost of migrating the web at large, from SSL certificates to increased hardware demands to software changes to application upgrades and reconfigurations to website redesigns were all borne by the world’s websites themselves, not Silicon Valley. The only added cost for the major internet firms was the added cryptographic computational cost, necessitating some additional hardware investment.
Most importantly, SSL only protected user communications in transit. Web companies could still access all of the data of their users in unencrypted form to mine, manipulate and monetize their users en masse.
The growing popularity of end-to-end encryption threatens to upend this uneasy truce between digital security and the ability of web companies to mine our personal data.
Facebook is both a major vendor of end-to-end encryption through its immensely popular WhatsApp application and one of the world’s leading <mark>digital advertising companies</mark> whose entire existence is <mark>prefaced on</mark> the <mark>ability to mine</mark> its users’ most personal and <mark>private communications</mark>.
As the company promotes its encryption-first future, it is a forgone conclusion that <mark>it</mark> has in mind ways of <mark>work</mark>ing <mark>around</mark> the protections of that <mark>encryption</mark> in order <mark>to continue mining</mark> its users’ <mark>private communications</mark>.
Communications metadata is by necessity unencrypted and Facebook has both alluded to using such data for counter-terrorism efforts and confirmed its use in its advertising efforts today. Given the company’s reliance on such metadata for its Messenger-powered Portal video conferencing device, it is almost a foregone conclusion that Facebook will be relying heavily on communications metadata to work around the loss of content access in an encrypted world.
Yet, metadata alone is insufficient for the kinds of rich behavioral and interest profiling that forms the lifeblood of Facebook’s surveillance empire.
Instead, Facebook is likely to turn to client-side decrypted content mining in which it moves its data mining efforts from its own servers to users’ devices, outsourcing the hardware computational costs to its users and enabling it to claim the protections of encryption while still mining their data. <mark>Zuckerberg</mark> himself has <mark>alluded to such efforts</mark> and the company has not denied moving in this direction.
Silicon Valley thus sits at a paradoxical intersection of both championing encryption and refusing to build governmental backdoors into its products while at the same time working to defeat the protections of encryption in order to ensure it is able to continue mining the most sensitive data of its users.
Instead of building backdoors into their encryption systems for governments, it is far more likely that companies would be amenable to building filtering tools into their device-side clients, since they will need to build such device-side filtering and mining for their own commercial ad-supported needs. <mark>As companies integrate</mark> client-side <mark>mining into</mark> their end-to-end <mark>encryption</mark> products <mark>for advertising</mark>, it will be easy for them to add government-mandated filtering and content alerting.
Putting this all together, the encryption debate has long centered on governments and their desires for backdoors and other mechanisms to continue their mass surveillance empires. Silicon Valley has long embraced encryption as a simplistic way of reassuring the public of their data’s privacy, while continuing to mine and monetize their users without constraint.

Consumer pressure may not truly be driving the move toward more encryption; instead, companies may be pressuring each other to move in this direction. <mark>Consumers</mark> seem to <mark>care more about being</mark> able to be <mark>connected to friends</mark>, having <mark>easy to use and reliable products</mark>, <mark>and</mark> having <mark>sleek interfaces</mark> and useful applications, and seem willing to sacrifice some privacy and security in exchange. If consumer pressure is not driving this shift, then perhaps the <mark>fear</mark> that U.S. <mark>companies</mark> will lose market share and that the economic viability of U.S. companies would be <mark>hurt by</mark> a <mark>lawful access</mark> requirement <mark>is overstated</mark>. As long as U.S. companies continue to lead in the areas consumers care about the most, while still making the most secure products and services possible that comply with a lawful access requirement, U.S. companies will likely continue to dominate the technology market.

The nature of the events examined in this work is clearly different compared to common security breaches which have been studied in the literature. <mark>Snowden reveal</mark>ed, basically through the press, news that concern customer data privacy from phone and digital communications, Internet platform breaches, phone and communication records collection from secret services, economic scandals and other similar-type information that one would expect they would have an impact on the brand name of the related companies. Nonetheless, our results illustrated that they do <mark>no</mark>t create the <mark>economic losses</mark>, <mark>in</mark> terms of the direct <mark>market</mark> response, that security breaches have resulted to in the past as indicated in the examined related work. As reported in the comprehensive study of Spanos and Angelis [28], the vast majority of the previous studies concluded that the impact of such events was negative and in fact, most of them were significantly negative. Moreover, only in the case of Chen et al. [54] neutral impact was reported. Nonetheless, the nature of this study is radically different, as Chen et al. study solely phising alerts and not actual attacks. Therefore, while their sample is rather dense, it consists of 1030 samples, they are not actual attacks but warnings to users of potential attacks that could be launched.
Snowden revelations, due to their content, were definitely expected to cause “noise” in the markets and in the broadcast news. Their impact is so large that public authorities and organisation are still battling over them, but as data analysis reveals, they do <mark>no</mark>t have any <mark>impact on</mark> the <mark>equity returns</mark>. On the contrary, the insignificant (zero) impact of day 0 seems to turn into a positive statistically significant CAR the day after (using FF-model) and the next 3 days (using CAPM).
Apparently, our research <mark>outcomes</mark> are <mark>align</mark>ed with <mark>other findings</mark> in privacy in the post-Snowden era. While some surveys indicate that there are some changes for the individuals [55], as they have started using or at least considered using more secure and private technologies, few new users have actually embraced privacy-enhancing technologies [9]. Like individuals, the response of the market towards these revelations can be considered skeptical, as they actually had no impact on the stock prices. Of course, as highlighted in other surveys –even by the authors– the market today is mature enough to understand and handle cyber security events appropriately, compared to the past. Nonetheless, Snowden’s revelations are beyond a cyber security event as they disclosed that several companies are undermining the security and privacy of their users. The pessimistic view of these facts would argue that privacy is not highly regarded in our society, by both individuals and the market. Perhaps, a more grounded to the facts view would be that such actions, cannot be considered unforeseen; not in such scale though, and that the market understands that <mark>affected customers can</mark>no<mark>t immediately alter</mark> their <mark>consumption</mark> patterns especially <mark>when</mark> the majority of a whole industry has been compromised, <mark>so</mark> the <mark>effects</mark> will <mark>appear gradually</mark> in the long run, as changes in surveillance laws have already been introduced. In this regard, the actual impact of these revelations can only be weighted in a macroeconomic fashion so it is too soon now to determine what economic changes were triggered by them
