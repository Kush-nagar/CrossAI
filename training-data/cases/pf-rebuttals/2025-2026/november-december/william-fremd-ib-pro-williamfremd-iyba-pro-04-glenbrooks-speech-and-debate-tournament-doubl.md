B] Companies will use rigorous testing.
Geoffrey S. Corn 15, George R. Killam Jr. Chair of Criminal Law, Professor at Texas Tech University, Gary A. Kuiper Professor of National Security, B.A. from Hartwick College, J.D. from George Washington University, “Averting the Inherent Dangers of "Going Dark": Why Congress Must Require a Locked Front Door to Encrypted Data,” Washington and Lee University School of Law Scholarly Commons, 6/1/15, https://scholarlycommons.law.wlu.edu/wlulr/vol72/iss3/12/, tristan
To be clear, this “split key” proposal is not a subterfuge method of creating “back door” access to data. It, like Director Comey, seeks to achieve preservation of “front door” access to encrypted communications. This might seem like a minor distinction, but it is not, as those who have misrepresented Comey’s comments understand perfectly. Unlike a “back door,” which generally refers to an undisclosed vulnerability in an application or device, a front door is a well-documented and clear <mark>mechanism for</mark> both encrypting and <mark>decrypting data</mark>, whether it be data in motion (communications) or at rest (stored data). <mark>To be secure</mark>, encryption should be <mark>subject to rigorous testing</mark>. Thus, its presence should be <mark>open to the public</mark> and <mark>available for attack</mark>, both in laboratories and in the real world. This is the only way to truly evaluate the trustworthiness of encryption, with <mark>vulnerabilities</mark> being <mark>corrected as</mark> they are <mark>discovered, to constantly strengthen</mark> the protocol and its <mark>implementation</mark>. Essentially, a front door is the digital equivalent of a big, ingeniously engineered lock on the only entrance to an otherwise secure building. It is a lock that has been tested by every available lock picker and found to be secure, with any identified weaknesses being constantly fixed. Such a lock is always superior to a secret entrance in the rear of a building.
