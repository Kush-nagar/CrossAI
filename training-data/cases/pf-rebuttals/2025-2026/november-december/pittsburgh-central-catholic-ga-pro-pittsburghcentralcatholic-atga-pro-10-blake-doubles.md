Great Powers May Have Limited Motivations for Involvement in Military Conflicts in Africa
Although all three great powers have considerable interests in <mark>Africa</mark> and competition for influence there is becoming more significant, the nature of great-power interests is <mark>not</mark> likely to provide <mark>a</mark> sufficiently <mark>strong motivation for</mark> any of the <mark>powers to be</mark>come <mark>involved</mark> in African conflicts—<mark>even </mark>in some of the countries <mark>where</mark> the potential for competition and <mark>opportunities </mark>for conflict <mark>are</mark> relatively <mark>high</mark>. For the United States, <mark>no</mark>ne of the <mark>interest</mark>s that it has in Nigeria or Mozambique <mark>warrants</mark> substantial <mark>involvement </mark>in a conflict. The most-plausible motivations for involvement are connected to <mark>counterterrorism.</mark> Washington also has <mark>economic</mark> interests <mark>and humanitarian concerns</mark>. Yet all of these interests <mark>are unlikely to motivate</mark> a significant expenditure of resources to support <mark>a proxy</mark> in the scenarios that we considered.
At least in our two scenario countries—Nigeria and Mozambique—<mark>China and</mark> <mark>Russia</mark> also <mark>lack</mark> the kind of significant <mark>interests</mark> in Africa <mark>that</mark> might <mark>motivate them to expend resources</mark> on substantial support to parties in local conflicts. Like the United States, the two <mark>competitors are not at high risk of</mark> direct impact from violent <mark>extremist networks present in these countries</mark>, even if all three powers are concerned about international terrorism generally—and especially closer to their homelands. For China, its greatest motivation to become involved in a conflict would stem from its economic interests, particularly in maintaining stability around the Belt and Road Initiative. Russia’s most-plausible motivations to engage in a conflict would be based on a combination of its strategic and economic interests—to maintain influence, ensure access to local political leadership, and support its interests in Africa’s markets and natural resources. Because of the relatively low willingness to engage substantially, <mark>the most-plausible </mark>forms of <mark>intervention</mark> in our scenario analyses <mark>entail indirect</mark> forms of support. The most-plausible conflict scenarios that we identified in Nigeria and Mozambique would entail counter-VEO engagements, <mark>training, </mark>non-combatant evacuations, support through <mark>PMSCs, or</mark> simply <mark>humanitarian assistance.</mark> Military engagements are most likely to involve small, specialized units or training teams. In the future, <mark>if U.S.-China </mark>and U.S<mark>.-Russia relations deteriorate further</mark> and increasingly become zero-sum, the powers’ relations in Africa may follow the same trajectory. However, <mark>it is unlikely</mark> that, <mark>even then, the countries would engage in large</mark>r-scale military <mark>interventions</mark>; rather, <mark>they would focus on non-military competition</mark>, support for local actors, and small-scale engagements. The involvement of competing powers in the same conflict could, however, increase the likelihood that Chinese or Russian forces would harass U.S., partner, or allied forces and civilian personnel.

Opper 15. Chris Opper, managing editor at Bloomberg Law. [China and the U.S. Can Avoid a Space War by Making That Hotline Bling, 12-9-2015, https://science.howstuffworks.com/china-the-us-can-avoid-a-space-war-making-that-hotline-bling.htm]
The <mark>U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>and China aren't</mark> 100 percent <mark>chummy</mark> these days, <mark>but</mark> that doesn't mean the two superpowers don't <mark>talk</mark>. As Uncle Sam and the world's largest Communist country look to outdo each other on the economic, military and political fronts, the two countries also recently came together <mark>to</mark> try to <mark>avoid</mark> a full-blown <mark>fight</mark> either <mark>in</mark> outer <mark>space</mark>. A new "hotline" will make it easier for officials from the States and the Middle Kingdom to share information about what they're doing up there in the final frontier, all to avoid any international dust-up.
The new <mark>communication system</mark> will allow the countries' leaders to <mark>talk</mark> directly about their space activities <mark>via</mark> secure <mark>text</mark> message. It's intended <mark>to cut through</mark> diplomatic channels and other bureaucratic <mark>middlemen</mark> so that the presidents can speak quickly to keep each other informed about operations in space. It's also meant <mark>to avoid</mark> both accidental <mark>collisions and</mark> any possible misinterpretations about space activity that could be taken as acts of <mark>aggression</mark>.
The space hotline is a first between the two countries---the <mark>U.S. and Russia</mark> already <mark>have one</mark> in place---and world leaders have been relying on similar systems <mark>to avoid hostilities</mark> on the ground and in the air for decades.
Shortly after stepping back from the ledge of World War III <mark>during</mark> the <mark>Cuban missile crisis</mark>, the U.S. and Russia established a direct telegraph system to allow them to alert each other of military and other moves. It turns out that the mythical red phone connecting the Pentagon with the Kremlin exists only on the big screen. Still, a super-secure email and audio messaging system linking the two countries has proven its worth as a way to keep leaders aware of each other's intentions when it comes to unrest in other nations.
During a <mark>1967 skirmish between Israel and</mark> its neighbors in the <mark>Middle East</mark>, for example, President Lyndon Johnson used what was then a teletype terminal to inform Russian leader Alexei Kosygin that U.S. military planes would be flying to the Mediterranean Sea.
Richard Nixon used the hotline for similar updates during wars <mark>in Pakistan and Cyprus</mark> during the late 1960s. The goal was to keep the Russians aware of certain military movements so that they didn't think the Americans were picking a fight.
The same idea has helped build the existing war hotlines that China shares with Russian, India, Vietnam and Korea. Earlier efforts to create a direct communication line for China and the U.S. have borne little fruit. After the Tiananmen Square massacre in 1989, President George H.W. Bush tried contacting his Chinese counterpart phone only to have that call go unreturned.
This new hotline offers something for both sides: Namely, the chance to avoid orbital warfare and a space arms race.

No North Korea war.
Lewis 24 [James; January 30th; Senior Vice President at CSIS, Senior Adviser for Four UN Groups of Governmental Experts on Information Security, “Is Kim Jong-un Crazy? Why North Korea Won't Start a War”, National Interest, https://nationalinterest.org/blog/korea-watch/kim-jong-un-crazy-why-north-korea-wont-start-war-208959]
Is North Korea's <mark>Kim</mark> Jong-un Crazy? The short answer is no, he’s not crazy, and while he may engage in all sorts of theatrics, he <mark>won’t start</mark> a <mark>war</mark>. Even suggesting this is silly.
North Korea's <mark>decision to reject reunification does not open</mark> “a <mark>perilous new era</mark>,” as some would have it. Kim’s efforts to build <mark>nuc</mark>lear weapon<mark>s</mark> and long-range missiles are intended to <mark>prevent war</mark>, to prevent the U.S. from invading or attacking North Korea, not to win one. Before saying “we would never do that,” look at American interventions through Kim’s eyes (and the perspective he shares with Russia, Iran, and China) and consider Iraq in 2003. For dictators, the U.S. is a rogue state, unwilling to accept a status quo they prefer. <mark>Kim wants to prevent invasion</mark> and protect family rule, and <mark>starting a war does not do that</mark>.
The <mark>North Korea</mark>ns may <mark>know</mark> that <mark>nuclear</mark>-armed <mark>states are</mark> very <mark>cautious</mark> in starting wars with each other – this is a point lost in much of the discussion of potential Russian or Chinese actions. It is one reason Iran wants nuclear weapons and why Kim has spent billions to develop them. This does not mean states will not use coercive actions, refrain from making bellicose threats, or even invade neighbors who are not under a nuclear umbrella, but it does <mark>mean</mark> that the <mark>likelihood of war</mark> on the Peninsula <mark>is remote</mark>. Interstate conflict is in a new era and uses new coercive tools, and using 20th-century weapons is not the first choice of competitors.
In some ways, western strategic discussions are predicated on pretending this nuclear effect does not exist. Nostalgia for the 20th Century, when men were men and battle lines drawn in black and white, exerts a powerful hold on Western strategic thinking and leads to policies and investments often irrelevant to achieving larger strategic goals. They are not irrelevant to North Korea, however, <mark>given</mark> its <mark>limitations and geography. It knows it cannot win a</mark> conventional or nuclear <mark>war</mark>, and the result of any war would be reunification on American terms, while Kim would have to flee to Moscow or Beijing. Keeping less than 30,00 troops (North Korea has 1.3 million), supported by immense American air power, is enough to prevent invasion.
One counterargument is that this assumes a degree of rationality among our opponents. <mark>We must distinguish</mark> between seemingly irrational public <mark>statements to</mark> influence domestic and international <mark>audiences and</mark> actual <mark>policy. If the</mark> principle <mark>goal</mark> for Kim and other authoritarians <mark>is to preserve</mark> their <mark>rule</mark>, whether or family or Party, the most <mark>dangerous threats are domestic</mark>. Authoritarians <mark>rely on</mark> heavy doses of nationalism to sustain <mark>public support</mark>. This nationalism requires an external foe (usually the US), and there is a risk that nationalist sentiment could swell and escape the ability of a regime to control it. The risk is not irrational decisions by mad dictators but uncontrollable internal political forces where regime survival requires starting a war.
REGIME SURVIVAL outweighs
Post ‘21 [Daniel; January 29th; International Relations PhD candidate at Brown University, 20th year of active-duty naval service and is assigned to the Permanent Military Professor Program at the U.S. Naval War College, War On The Rocks, “Deterring North Korea” https://warontherocks.com/2021/01/deterring-north-korea/]
Last, both <mark>Kim and</mark> leaders in the <mark>U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates <mark>and</mark> its <mark>allies appear</mark> to remain <mark>rational</mark> actors despite recent bombastic behavior and inflammatory rhetoric (which may be plausibly attributed to clumsy signaling attempts). Kim may be a cold and brutal oppressor, but his behavior should be seen as quite rational if you make the very supportable assumption that, like most political leaders, <mark>his</mark> primary <mark>goal is keeping</mark> <mark>his regime alive and</mark> keeping <mark>himself in power</mark>. As others have noted, “Kim is a tyrant, but I don’t think he is suicidal.” Kim continues to build and enhance his nuclear weapons capability in reaction to real and proximate threats to his very survival. The United States frequently conducts exercises with South Korea and Japan, and North Korea frequently decries these exercises as hostile and reckless. Kim sees these exercises as practice events for an eventual attack on North Korea. The United States has also stationed missile defense capabilities in South Korea and Japan, as well as on ships in the region. Kim’s continued pursuit of enhanced nuclear capabilities in response is as rational as it is for the U.S. to want to mitigate its own vulnerability. Frequent military deployments to the region, and overflights of U.S. strategic (nuclear capable) bombers also serve to enhance the perception of threat on behalf of Kim. These security dilemma dynamics have certainly contributed to Kim’s rational pursuit of an enhanced nuclear weapons capability. On top of these very visible military measures, recent dramatic increases in hostile rhetoric from former President Donald Trump, such as his “fire and fury” remarks, have only served to solidify Kim’s perceived need for a nuclear deterrent to potential U.S.-backed regime change. Of course, deterrence requires clear communication and credibility, which includes demonstrating capability. <mark>Kim is</mark> well <mark>aware</mark> that if he were to engage in <mark>any</mark> sort of large scale <mark>aggressive</mark> military <mark>behavior</mark> against his neighbors, this <mark>could</mark> spell the <mark>end</mark> of <mark>his regime. He</mark> also <mark>has no reason to doubt</mark> the <mark>U.S. capability</mark> and opportunity <mark>to respond to threats</mark> from North Korea. Assuming, as I do above, that <mark>Kim</mark> desires to remain the leader of his country and to preserve his regime, he <mark>has little incentive to test the</mark> U.S. nuclear <mark>deterrent</mark>. Whether his eccentric and brutal behavior leads to some other inadvertent escalation is a different question. As far as U.S. leaders are concerned, assuming Kim is rational enough to know what he wants and to recognize how he can lose it seems to be a safe bet.
