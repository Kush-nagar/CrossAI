For Atchison and Panetta, “<mark>the ballot</mark>” a judge casts at the conclusion of a debate <mark>should signify nothing more</mark> or less <mark>than that person’s decision “to vote for the team that does the best debating.”</mark> This understanding encourages judges to limit their analysis of a debate to the arguments presented within each team’s allotted times to speak. It would exclude decisions focused on resolving external abuses such as: determining the appropriateness of statements or events between a team or program that occurred outside of the immediate debate; challenging a school’s success at “recruiting minority participants”; criticizing the civil rights legacy of participants’ academic institutions; or increasing the presence of underrepresented bodies in elimination debates. By contrast, <mark>some non-traditional teams</mark> interested in challenging the marginalizing effects of policy debate formats <mark>have begun to advocate</mark> what I call a “<mark>ballot as currency</mark>” model for judges to evaluate debates. While the specific terminology is not universally employed, the “ballot as currency” approach establishes that <mark>a judge’s ballot signifies what bodies and practices </mark>she <mark>[they] deem</mark>s <mark>appropriate</mark> for policy debate. Within this model, a non-traditional team’s ability to accumulate wins is a referendum on the perceived acceptableness of their bodies for academic spaces. Beyond the structural factors that limit the visibility of any individual debate, Atchison and Panetta identify <mark>two problems</mark> with the “ballot as currency” method for evaluating debates. <mark>First,</mark> the “<mark>ballot as currency</mark>” approach presents the dilemma of “<mark>ask</mark>ing <mark>a judge to vote to solve a community problem” with very “few participants”</mark> (generally the other people in the room) <mark>allowed to take a stake</mark> in the process. This places the course of community change on the shoulders of those who judge debates between traditional and non-traditional teams and excludes those “coaches and directors who are not preferred judges and, therefore, do not have access to many debates.” <mark>Furthermore, it excludes those “who might want to contribute to community conversation,</mark> but are <mark>not directly involved in competition</mark>.” Prioritizing the “ballot as currency” approach fails to recognize that “<mark>debate</mark> community <mark>is broader than the individual participants</mark>” of a given debate and risks the creation of “an insulated community that has all the answers” without ever engaging those concerned individuals who do not attend every competition. The result is that <mark>a very narrow set of judges,</mark> usually those that often judge Framework debates, <mark>are granted the authority to determine the outcome of communal change.</mark>

Elsewhere, Eve (Tuck, 2009, 2010) has argued that <mark>education</mark>al research and much of social science research <mark>has been concerned with documenting</mark> damage, or empirically substantiating <mark>the oppression and pain of</mark> Native communities, urban communities, and other <mark>disenfranchised communities</mark>. Damage-centered <mark>researchers</mark> may <mark>operate</mark>, even benevolently, <mark>within a theory</mark> of change <mark>in which harm must be recorded</mark> or proven in <mark>order to convince an outside adjudicator that reparations are deserved</mark>. These reparations presumably take the form of additional resources, settlements, affirmative actions, and other material, political, and sovereign adjustments. Eve has described <mark>this theory</mark> of change1 as both colonial and flawed, because it <mark>relies upon Western notions of power as scarce</mark> and concentrated, <mark>and</mark> because it <mark>requires disenfranchised communities to position themselves as</mark> both singularly defective and <mark>powerless</mark> to make change (2010). Finally, Eve has observed that “<mark>won” reparations rarely become reality</mark>, and that in many cases, <mark>communities are left with a narrative that tells them that they are broken</mark>. Similarly, at the center of the analysis in this chapter is a concern with the fixation social science research has exhibited in eliciting pain stories from communities that are not White, not wealthy, and not straight. Academe’s demonstrated fascination with telling and retelling narratives of pain is troubling, both for its voyeurism and for its consumptive implacability. Imagining “itself to be a voice, and in some disciplinary iterations, the voice of the colonised” (Simpson, 2007, p. 67, emphasis in the original) is not just a rare historical occurrence in anthropology and related fields. We observe that much of <mark>the work of the academy is to reproduce stories of oppression in its own voice</mark>. At first, this may read as an intolerant condemnation of the academy, one that refuses to forgive past blunders and see how things have changed in recent decades. However, it is our view that while many individual scholars have chosen to pursue other lines of inquiry than the pain narratives typical of their disciplines, novice researchers emerge from doctoral programs eager to launch pain-based inquiry projects because they believe that such approaches embody what it means to do social science. The collection of pain narratives and the theories of change that champion the value of such narratives are so prevalent in the social sciences that one might surmise that they are indeed what the academy is about.
