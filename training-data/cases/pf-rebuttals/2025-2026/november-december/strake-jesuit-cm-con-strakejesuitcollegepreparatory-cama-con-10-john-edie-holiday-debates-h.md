Maintaining a Non-Escalatory Posture
Since approximately 2010, China has been increasingly assertive, especially in foreign affairs. It often flexes its muscles, elbowing out competing territorial claimants in the East China Sea and South China Sea and expanding its territorial interests. Although some scholarly works argue that China’s assertiveness has been limited to non-military measures, 15 most agree that under President Xi’s leadership, China has shown a greater willingness to take riskier actions to advance its strategic interests.16 In a speech at a ceremony marking the 95th anniversary of the founding of the CPC, Xi emphasized that “we do not make trouble, but we are not afraid of it when trouble comes our way.”17 Xi also reiterated the necessity of preparing for the worst-case scenario and upholding the fighting spirit (坚持底线 思维、发扬斗争精神) to defend China’s national interests in sovereignty, security and development.
In response to Tokyo’s decision to nationalize the islands in 2012, Beijing adopted a confrontational diplomatic approach and increased the presence of administrative vessels in the disputed waters. Despite the United States reiterating its security commitment to support Japan’s claim over the islands, as well as Japan’s status as an important trade partner and source of foreign direct investment for China, these actions demonstrated China’s adherence to President Xi’s new diplomatic doctrines. Even so, a close examination of the official discourse and strategic behaviors suggests that China pursued a limited objective, characterized by high-profile demonstrative contestation short of forcible reclamation. <mark>Beijing’s routine navigation</mark> in disputed waters <mark>can be seen as</mark> a deliberate strategy to challenge Japan’s effective control over the islands while <mark>maintaining a predictable and manageable approach</mark>. <mark>China appears to be satisfied with the</mark> “<mark>new status quo</mark>” it <mark>has</mark> created through these actions, showing <mark>no signs of escalating</mark> so far.
China’s Limited Aims
What China wants in the Diaoyu/Senkaku dispute is Japan’s acknowledgment of the existence of the dispute and its adherence to China’s policy of “shelving the dispute and pursuing joint development.”18 While Japan claims that “the Senkaku Islands are indisputably an inherent part of the territory of Japan in light of historical facts and based upon international law,” China firmly believes that a tacit consensus was reached between the two nations to set aside the dispute in 1972, when the two nations decided to “leave the territorial dispute for future resolution” during the normalization of their diplomatic relations.19 To support this position, People’s Daily also cited declassified British archives, which include a mention of tacit consensus on the issue by Japanese Premier Suzuki Zenkō in his meeting with British counterpart Margaret Thatcher in 1982.20
Furthermore, China asserts that its resolute countermeasures were in reaction to Japan’s moves to alter the status quo by nationalizing the disputed islands. In other words, Beijing argues that China is not challenging the status quo, but trying to restore it. At the time, the Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs (CMFA) stated that “it is Japan that changes the status quo of shelving the dispute, makes proactive moves, and repeatedly offends China’s sovereignty integrity.”21 Liu Jiangyong, a senior diplomat and Tsinghua professor specializing in China-Japan relations, argued, “Since Japan had renounced the policy of ‘shelving the dispute’ … China has to fight back tit for tat.”22 Understanding these prevailing attitudes among Chinese leaders and decision-makers can help explain why China’s approach to the Daioyu/Senkaku dispute has been so measured—even as they ramp up escalatory rhetoric in other areas of foreign policy.
A “New Status Quo”
Although Japan has not accommodated China’s demands to publicly acknowledging shelving the dispute, Chinese leadership claimed victory anyway. In August 2013, People’s Daily stated that “We [China] handled complicated and sensitive issues such as North Korea’s pursuit of nuclear weapons, Diaoyu island dispute, South China Sea disputes, etc., <mark>successfully defending</mark> our <mark>sovereignty and</mark> maritime rights, <mark>maintaining</mark> regional <mark>stability</mark>, and creating friendly surrounding environments necessary for our social and economy development.”23 China’s Ocean Development Report 2013 highlighted that Beijing’s fight to protect its maritime interests in 2012 had achieved a great victory, specifically mentioning China’s navigation in the Diaoyu/Senkaku waters as an example. The report added, “The close cooperation between China Coast Guard and Fishery Administration adopted agile and flexible tactics and achieved initial success in our special law-enforcement operations in Huangyan Islands (Scarborough Shoal) and Diaoyu Islands waters.”24 Through this rhetoric, China manages to <mark>appease</mark> domestic <mark>nationalist sentiments</mark> and signal its status as a rising great power in the international community <mark>while avoiding a military response</mark> from Japan or the United States <mark>that could jeopardize</mark> its <mark>economic relations</mark> with the West.
Moreover, Beijing attributes its success to President Xi’s leadership and new practices of major-country diplomacy. According to an editorial in People’s Daily in 2017, “Since the Party’s 18th Congress, China has clarified its positions, drawn the bottom lines, and carried on the fighting spirits, and achieved great success in defending China’s interests.” The editorial specially mentioned the Diaoyu/Senkaku disputes, stating that “on the Diaoyu Islands problems, we did not only foil the conspirators’ intrigue but also took the initiative in the East China Sea simultaneously (挫败对手战略图谋的同时，牢牢把握了东海战略 博弈的主动权).”25 In Chinese language, “taking the initiative” often emphasizes setting the agenda and controlling escalating risks, rather than necessarily escalating. The edited volume The Xi Jinping Ear by James Hsiung, portions of which have been reprinted in multiple Chinese official media outlets, argues that the establishment of China’s Air Defence Identification Zone in November 2013 —encompassing the disputed waters surrounding the Diaoyu/Senkaku Islands —was intended to compel Japan to acknowledge the existence of a territorial dispute. This move is interpreted as consistent with Xi Jinping’s assertive and hardline leadership style.26 Another piece highlighted President Xi’s involvement, stating that “Xi commanded these major military operations, such as navigation operations in Diaoyu Islands waters … effectively safeguarding China’s interests in sovereignty, security, and development, increasing the prestige of the nation and the army, and building up the national confidence and pride.”27
Prioritizing Economic Development
Diversionary war theory posits that incompetent leaders tend to invoke foreign policy threats to divert public attention from their domestic turmoil.28 Some China analysts express concerns that during times of economic difficulties, China may engage in war-prone diplomatic assertiveness as a means to distract from domestic turmoil and rally popular support. As foreign policy experts Michael Beckley and Hal Brands write, “Rising powers become most aggressive when their fortunes fade, their difficulties multiply, and they realize they must achieve their ambitions now or miss the chance to do so forever. China has already started down this path.”29 However, upon closer examination, these claims may not hold true in the case of China, which has consistently reacted to domestic turmoil by prioritizing cooperative economic development rather than nationalist territorial expansion.
Echoes of History
Since the 1950s, according to MIT expert M. Taylor Fravel, Chinese leaders “have encountered strong domestic incentives to engage in diversionary crises or wars but almost never do so.”30 On territorial disputes, he argues, domestic insecurity creates stronger incentives for leaders to compromise with competing territorial claimants so that Beijing can secure the borders and concentrate resources to address internal challenges to its regime security.31 Remarkably, during Mao’s era, China successfully resolved most of its border disputes with neighboring countries.
In the late 1970s, when the Chinese economy was on the brink of collapse, Deng Xiaoping introduced a strategic approach aimed at resolving territorial disputes in the South China Sea and East China Sea. He proposed shelving these disputes and fostering friendly relations with Southeast Asian countries and Japan. Additionally, Deng also advocated for the innovative principle of “One Country, Two Systems,” which allowed Hong Kong and Macau to retain their capitalist economies and way of life, initially facilitating their smooth transition back to Chinese sovereignty. Deng and his successor, Jiang Zemin, continued the pursuit of friendly and transactional relations with Taiwan under the framework of the “One China, Different Interpretations,” often referred to as the 1992 Consensus. This consensus acknowledges that there is only one “China,” but recognizes the differing interpretations of what “China” represents across the Taiwan Strait (i.e., the Republic of China vs. the People’s Republic of China). The Chinese leadership’s efforts to resolve or shelve these disputes were driven by a well-organized objective: creating a friendly international environment that is essential for domestic development.
China’s Diaoyu/<mark>Senkaku</mark> policy is indeed a case that <mark>illustrates</mark> its <mark>prioritization of econ</mark>omic development over accommodating nationalism in territorial disputes. Accordingly, Beijing has played down several crises caused by Chinese and Japanese nationalists’ moves to assert sovereignty claims. For instance, in September 1996, David Chan, a Hong Kong (HK) Baodiao activist, drowned while attempting to swim to the islands, which led to a large-scale Baodiao movement in HK. HK citizens and politicians pressured Beijing to stand firm against Japan’s “new invasion” of Chinese territories. Beijing had strong incentives to accommodate, as HK’s collective expressions of patriotism were unprecedented, and a stable transfer of HK sovereignty back to China, scheduled in 1997, might have been jeopardized if Beijing failed to address HK citizens’ concerns. However, Beijing’s response was limited to diplomatic protests and a naval exercise allegedly to warn Japan.32
The major rationale behind Chinese behaviors during this period was China’s need for Japanese aid and foreign direct investment (FDI) to boost its industrial development. In 1978, Deng and his colleagues recognized the importance of introducing foreign equipment and technologies to enhance China’s industrial productivity. However, China’s foreign reserves, which stood at a mere $2 billion USD at the time, were insufficient to meet the substantial financial requirements of importing new equipment. Contracts signed with Western countries for equipment imports in 1978 amounted to $6.36 billion USD, creating a significant funding gap. To address this gap, Deng turned to Japan for both private investment and official development assistance (ODA), a decision that proved to be wise. Japan provided substantial loans amounting to ¥303 billion Japanese Yen in 1979 to finance China’s major projects. From 1979 to 2005, Japanese ODA accounted for a remarkable 67.2 percent of all foreign government aid received by China. Notably, Japanese ODA played a critical role in financing China’s basic infrastructure investments, particularly in the 1990s, reaching its peak in 1994 at approximately 27 percent of China’s total budgetary investment on basic infrastructure.33
China’s strategy of diversionary peace is rooted in the understanding that <mark>econ</mark>omic development <mark>holds greater significance for</mark> the CPC’s <mark>legitimacy</mark> than pursuing assertive foreign policies to stoke domestic nationalism. Economic considerations outweigh the appeal of fervent nationalism, especially during periods of economic challenges. According to Professor Yan Xuetong, China has no intention of antagonizing the West, as “these exports [to the developed economies] are the lifeblood of the Chinese economy: they ensure a consistent trade surplus, and the jobs they create are a vital engine of domestic social stability.”34
China’s Diplomatic Reorientation
Over the past decade, Chinese diplomacy has undergone dramatic transformations, with the rise and subsequent recalibration of “wolf-warrior” diplomacy standing out as a defining phenomenon. Characterized by confrontational rhetoric, public rebukes of foreign governments, and frequent violations of established diplomatic protocol, this assertive style was embraced by a number of Chinese diplomats who sought to project national strength and defend China’s image with unprecedented bluntness. However, this approach drew widespread international criticism and increasingly strained China’s relations with the West. Recognizing the potential economic and geopolitical costs of such combative diplomacy, Chinese leadership has gradually moved to moderate its diplomatic tone. In June 2021, Xi Jinping emphasized the importance of “adopting a narrative tone that reflects openness and confidence, yet conveys modesty and humility, in a bid to shape a reliable, admirable and respectable image of China.”35 The Wall Street Journal reported that this shift in approach has been driven by Beijing’s apprehension that its confrontational “wolf-warrior” diplomacy may exacerbate anti-China sentiment and potentially isolate the Chinese economy.36
Notably, outspoken and proactive diplomats known for their assertive wolfwarrior style, such as former deputy spokesperson of the Chinese Foreign Ministry Zhao Lijian and former ambassador to Sweden Gui Congyou, have been retired or reassigned. Since 2022, China has been actively seeking to resume official and unofficial exchanges and trying to fix its broken relations with Europe, Canada, Japan, Australia, and India, and maintain stable working relations with the United States amid strategic competition. Since November 2023, China has unilaterally granted visa-free entry to citizens of forty-three countries including Germany, France, Italy, Australia, and Japan.37 This reflects a strategic effort to boost people-to-people exchanges, revitalize post-COVID economic engagement, and improve its global image amid rising geopolitical tensions.
China’s new charm offensive is targeting Japan as well. Xi has repeatedly stated his will to enhance China’s relations with Japan and met with Prime Minister Shinzo Abe ten times from 2014 to 2019. At the 2019 Buenos Aires APEC Summit, Xi told then-prime minister Abe that “China continues to welcome Japan to be part of China’s reform and opening up, sharing new opportunities of China’s development.”38 As mentioned, China and Japan have engaged in intensive minister-level meetings and social exchanges. Xi and then-prime minister Kishida’s meeting in November 2023 reaffirmed their commitment to “the comprehensive promotion of a mutually beneficial relationship based on common strategic interests.”39
A major impetus is both states’ willingness to improve their economic ties.40 Kishida and Xi agreed to establish a dialogue framework on trade, and their ministries in charge discussed several key issues such as the stability of the supply chain, export control of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, China’s entry into the Comprehensive and Progressive Agreement for Trans-Pacific Partnership (CPTPP), and the acceptance of new members into the RCEP (Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership).41 In January 2024, Chinese Premier Li Qiang met with Japanese business leaders, underscoring the significant role of economic and trade cooperation as “the ballast and propeller of bilateral relations,” two words echoing the previous language used to describe China-US relations in China’s official discourse prior to the two countries’ trade war.42 Additionally, China is active in promoting East Asia integration, partly to offset the economic disengagement between China and the United States. China, Japan, and Korea agreed to accelerate free trade negotiations in March 2025—which had been on pause since 2019 and were resumed in 2024—amid the growing concerns over renewed tariff conflicts triggered by a second Trump administration.
Unlike diversionary war theory, domestic challenges have actually generated pacifying effects over China’s foreign behavior. Conversely, Chinese leadership’s perceptions of favorable distribution of power in the international system—particularly since the 2008 financial crises—have reinforced a more assertive, albeit cautious, posture in the East China Sea and South China Sea.43 As pointed out by Aaron Friedberg, “When Beijing feels stronger it acts more assertively; when it feels weaker or more vulnerable it acts with greater caution and restraint.”44
Is the Diaoyu/Senkaku Dispute Still a Flashpoint?
The three factors discussed in the above sections play important roles in pacifying the Diaoyu/Senkaku dispute from China’s perspective, creating incentives for Beijing to engage in strategic communication and crisis management with Japan. These strategic choices—restraint of popular nationalist activism, limited regional aims, and pursuit of economic development to quell domestic turmoil—all suggest that China has no intention to further alter the status quo. Additionally, China’s routine navigation in disputed waters is relatively transparent and predictable, mitigating the risks of misunderstandings and miscalculations. Adding the increasing diplomatic engagements between Beijing and Tokyo, the Diaoyu/Senkaku dispute will not be a flashpoint in the foreseeable future.
However, <mark>Chinese</mark> Coast Guard vessels’ <mark>presence</mark> in Diaoyu/Senkaku surrounding waters <mark>has been steadily increasing since 2012</mark>, challenging Japan’s effective control over these islands. In November 2023, during his visit to the Command Office for the East China Sea area of the China Coast Guard based in Shanghai, President Xi allegedly emphasized the need to “constantly strengthen” China’s efforts to safeguard the islands’ sovereignty.45 If China and Japan change course and engage in escalatory behaviors, hostilities could be reignited—with far-reaching implications for East Asian security. The following sections outline two potential paths to destabilization.
Japanese Landings on the Islands
The first scenario refers to Japanese citizens or officials landing on the islands in any name, which will probably recur in the foreseeable future. The Chinese government has closely monitored Japanese activists’ attempts to enter the 12-nautical-mile territorial waters surrounding the Diaoyu/Senkaku Islands, including efforts to fly both manned and unmanned vehicles over the area and to land on the disputed islands.46 In 2023, the Chinese Coast Guard stated that it had taken necessary measures to warn Japanese fishing boats and patrol vessels that it deemed to have “intruded” into the waters around the islands in 2023. In January 2024, China further issued warnings to Japanese Air Self-Defense Forces to leave airspace around and over the islands.47 Beijing urged Tokyo to constrain the “right-wing activists’” proactive actions in the dispute, while Tokyo protested Chinese harassing Japanese ships and urged its civilian actors to exercise caution and avoid actions that could escalate tensions with China over the islands.48
While the risks of miscalculation are certainly concerning, it is important to note that China’s <mark>response will be</mark> tit-for-tat and <mark>limited to non-military means</mark>. What China wants is a “no-entry zone” around the islands for both sides, a new status quo perceived in China’s thinking since 2012. Should Japanese ships appear in the surrounding waters, China’s response will be flexible and proportional. It <mark>aims to signal</mark> its <mark>dissatisfaction</mark> and anger <mark>without</mark> resorting to overtly aggressive actions that could provoke a <mark>military response</mark> from Japan or possibly the United States.
Militarization of the Islands
Another highly dangerous scenario that could exacerbate tensions in the Diaoyu/ Senkaku dispute is the potential militarization of the islands by either Japan or the United States. Some analysts argue that China has a strong interest in controlling the disputed islands due to their strategic location for facilitating People’s Liberation Army (PLA) advancement into the Pacific to prevent American military interference if China were to force unification of Taiwan, and align with President Xi’s vision of China’s “great rejuvenation.”49 Although this interpretation misunderstands the strategic value of the disputed islands for the CPC and even President Xi Jinping himself, China’s assertiveness in the surrounding waters is seen as a sign of escalation aimed at ultimately restoring China’s control over the islands. In these instances, Japan and the United States’ Diaoyu/Senkaku policies may escalate by the logic of military deterrence. Military deployment on or near disputed waters could significantly increase to deter <mark>possible invasions</mark> from the PLA, particularly since the Japanese Self-Defense Forces (SDF) and the Pentagon perceive its military advantages would be significantly challenged by an increasingly modernized PLA in the first island chain.
However, the portrayal of this scenario <mark>is</mark> often <mark>misleading</mark>, <mark>based on a false premise</mark> which suggests <mark>that China would opportunistically</mark> attempt to <mark>occupy</mark> the islands at lower costs, even hoping to create a fait accompli without an armed conflict before Tokyo and Washington could respond. However, <mark>China is well aware of</mark> the surveillance and intelligence <mark>capabilities of Japan and the U</mark>nited <mark>S</mark>tates, as well as their determination to defend the islands.50 China learned a valuable lesson from the West’s response to Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and understands that the costs of military actions can often exceed initial expectations.51 Therefore, <mark>a surprise attack</mark> by China on the Diaoyu/Senkaku islands <mark>is highly unlikely</mark>, making the militarization of the islands unnecessary.

The Erosion of Extended Deterrence
Extended deterrence has long been considered one of the fundamental tenets of the U.S.-Japan alliance.53 The assurance that Japan was included under the U.S. “nuclear umbrella” (the security guarantee that the United States could respond to an attack on allies with nuclear weapons) for decades provided Tokyo with a belief that it was safe from attack under the U.S. commitment. As the world’s sole superpower, it was relatively easy for the United States to make this promise. But <mark>the capabilities of</mark> potential <mark>adversaries</mark> in the region <mark>have grown exponentially</mark>, and <mark>U.S. leadership is now</mark> more <mark>contested</mark> in the region, calling the commitment into question. Extended deterrence dialogues at the deputy assistant secretary level—the last held in June 202554—have done little to arrest this doubt. <mark>No</mark>rth <mark>Ko</mark>rea <mark>has become</mark> a de facto <mark>nuclear</mark> state, developing what many experts think is a credible ability to hit the U.S. homeland with a nuclear-armed intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM).55 A <mark>deepening partnership among</mark> <mark>North Korea</mark>, <mark>China</mark>, <mark>and Russia</mark>—all with nuclear arsenals—further <mark>alarms</mark> U.S. <mark>allies</mark>. Extended <mark>deterrence has</mark> always <mark>been a hard sell</mark>; would the United States really risk a nuclear strike on an American city to protect Japan?
The increasingly <mark>questionable credibility</mark> of extended deterrence <mark>has loosened the</mark> long-held <mark>taboo of</mark> mentioning <mark>Japan’s development of its own</mark> <mark>nuc</mark>lear weapon<mark>s</mark> program. These <mark>discussions are</mark> still <mark>quiet</mark>, <mark>but</mark> they are <mark>growing</mark>. Nuclear Proliferation
In an influential book, Graham Allison argues that “no new nuclear weapons states” should be a prime foreign policy principle, and analyst Joseph Cirincione very much agrees, insisting that nonproliferation should be “our number one national‐​security priority.”5
<mark>There are</mark> good <mark>reasons to avoid alarmism</mark> in this area, however. First, the pace of nuclear <mark>prolif</mark>eration <mark>has been</mark> far <mark>slow</mark>er than has been commonly predicted primarily because the <mark>weapons convey little advantage</mark> to their possessor. Second, the consequences of such <mark>prolif</mark>eration that <mark>has</mark> taken place have <mark>been</mark> substantially <mark>benign</mark>: those who have acquired the weapons have “used” them simply to stoke their egos or to deter real or imagined threats.6
And thirdly, the costs of anti‐​proliferation policy have been very substantial: the number of people who have died as a consequence of dedicated efforts to contain nuclear proliferation runs well into six figures.
Pace
Alarmists have been wrong for decades about the pace of nuclear proliferation. <mark>Dozens of</mark> technologically <mark>capable countries have considered</mark> obtaining nuclear <mark>arsenals, but very few have done so</mark>. Indeed, as Jacques Hymans has pointed out, even supposedly optimistic forecasts about nuclear dispersion have proved to be too pessimistic.7 Thus, in 1958, the National Planning Association predicted “a rapid rise in the number of atomic powers … by the mid‐​1960s.”8 A few years later, C. P. Snow sagely predicted, “Within, at the most, six years, China and several other states [will] have a stock of nuclear bombs,” and John Kennedy observed that there might be “ten, fifteen, twenty” countries with a nuclear capacity by 1964.9
As part of that forecasting, it has generally been assumed that nuclear weapons would be important status — or virility — symbols; therefore, all advanced countries would want to have them in order to show how “powerful” they were. Thus, France’s de Gaulle opined in the 1960s, “No country without an atom bomb could properly consider itself independent,” and Robert Gilpin concluded that “the possession of nuclear weapons largely determines a nation’s rank in the hierarchy of international prestige.”10 In Gilpinian tradition, some analysts who describe themselves as “realists” have insisted for years that Germany and Japan must soon come to their senses and quest after nuclear weapons.11 Such punditry has gone astray in part because the pundits insist on extrapolating from the wrong cases. A more pertinent prototype would have been Canada, a country that could easily have had nuclear weapons by the 1960s but declined to make the effort.12 In fact, over the decades, <mark>a huge number</mark> of countries capable of developing nuclear weapons have <mark>neglected even to consider the opportunity</mark> — for example, Canada, Italy, and Norway — even as Argentina, Brazil, Libya, South Korea, and Taiwan have backed away from or reversed nuclear weapons programs, and Belarus, Kazakhstan, South Africa, and Ukraine have actually surrendered or dismantled an existing nuclear arsenal.13 Some of that reduction is no doubt due to the hostility of the nuclear nations, but even without that, the Canadian case seems to have proved to have rather general relevance.
To begin with, as Stephen Meyer has shown, there is no “technological imperative” for countries to obtain nuclear weapons once they have achieved the technical capacity to do so.14 Moreover, like military prowess in general, the weapons have not proved to be crucial status symbols. As Robert Jervis has observed, “India, China, and Israel may have decreased the chance of direct attack by developing nuclear weapons, but it is hard to argue that they have increased their general prestige or influence.”15 How much more status would Japan have if it possessed nuclear weapons? Would anybody pay a great deal more attention to Britain or France if their arsenals held 5,000 nuclear weapons, or would anybody pay much less if they had none? Did China need nuclear weapons to impress the world with its economic growth? Or with its Olympics? As Jennifer Mackby and Walter Slocombe observe, “Germany, like its erstwhile Axis ally, <mark>Japan</mark>, has become powerful because of its economic might rather than its military might, and its <mark>renunciation of nuc</mark>lear weapon<mark>s</mark> may even have <mark>reinforced</mark> its <mark>prestige</mark>.”16
<mark>Decades of</mark> alarmist <mark>predictions about</mark> proliferation chains, <mark>cascades</mark>, dominoes, waves, avalanches, epidemics, and points of no return <mark>have proved </mark>to be <mark>faulty</mark>. The <mark>proli</mark>feration of nuclear weapons <mark>has been</mark> far <mark>slow</mark>er than routinely expected because, insofar as most <mark>leaders</mark> of most countries (even rogue ones) have considered acquiring the weapons, they have come to <mark>appreciate</mark> several defects: the <mark>weapons are dangerous</mark>, distasteful, <mark>costly, and</mark> likely to <mark>rile the neighbors</mark>. Moreover, as Jacques Hymans has demonstrated, the <mark>weapons have</mark> also <mark>been</mark> <mark>exceedingly difficult to obtain</mark> for administratively dysfunctional countries like Iran.17
Consequences
Although we have now suffered through two‐​thirds of a century during which there has been great hysteria about the disasters inherent in nuclear proliferation, the consequences of the proliferation that has occurred have been substantially benign. The few <mark>countries</mark> to <mark>which</mark> the weapons <mark>have proliferated have quietly kept them in storage</mark> and haven’t even found much benefit in rattling them from time to time. And even the deterrence value of the weapons has been questionable — the major Cold War participants, for example, scarcely needed visions of mushroom clouds to conclude that any replication of World War II, with or without nuclear weapons, was a decidedly bad idea.18
Moreover, <mark>there has never been a</mark> militarily <mark>compelling</mark> — or even minimally sensible — <mark>reason</mark> <mark>to use the weapons</mark>, particularly because of an inability to identify suitable targets or ones that could not be attacked about as effectively by conventional munitions. And it is difficult to see how nuclear weapons benefited their possessors in specific military ventures. Israel’s presumed nuclear weapons did not restrain the Arabs from attacking in 1973, nor did Britain’s prevent Argentina’s seizure of the Falklands in 1982. Similarly, the tens of thousands of nuclear weapons in the arsenals of the enveloping allied forces did not cause Saddam Hussein to order his occupying forces out of Kuwait in 1990. Nor did possession of the bomb benefit America in Korea, Vietnam, Iraq, or Afghanistan; France in Algeria; or the Soviet Union in Afghanistan.
