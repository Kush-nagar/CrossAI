### Round-distinctive execution traits

1. **A K round where the AFF's *own case* is the strongest link — and both
   sides know it.** Unlike a policy round where links are contested evidence,
   here the link is the AFF's plain-text advantages (build the Navy, fix the
   nukes). NEG never has to *prove* a link; it just reads the AFF's contentions
   back. That collapses the usual K link-debate and moves the entire round up to
   framework, which is why the ontology/consequences clash carries everything.
   The lesson for a pro-nuclear AFF against this K: a *softer* case (baseload
   decarbonization only, no militarization advantage) denies the K its free
   links — the hawkish case maximized offense at the cost of maximal K exposure.

2. **The decisive layer is litigated hardest where it can't be flowed.** The
   ontology-vs-consequences question gets its fullest airing in the **grand
   crossfire** ([52:57]–[54:57]) — "why is it ontologically bad to prevent
   children from dying in wars?" — a genuinely sharp exchange that lives in a
   speech the judge doesn't flow. Both final focuses then re-run it cold. When
   the round's hinge is a framework question, cross is where it's *understood*
   and the last two speeches are where it must be *won*; this round shows the gap
   between the two.

3. **Consequentialism answering an epistemology K — the structural trap this
   round illustrates.** AFF's best beat ("ontology needs consequences") and its
   whole weighing close are consequentialist, which is precisely what a
   prior-question K exists to filter. AFF partially escapes by recasting its
   impacts as *moral* (child soldiers, indigenous harm) rather than magnitude
   (it "purposely did not read extinction" [32:07]) — a real attempt to compete
   on the K's terrain. Whether that escapes the filter or performs the
   contradiction is the unresolved question; either way it is the model example
   of the postfiat/prefiat and performative-contradiction rows in a live K round.

4. **First-speaking-neg K forces an off-balance AFF and a mirror-image speech
   order.** NEG electing to speak first and open with the K meant AFF answered
   the K in its *rebuttal* while also frontlining a case it read *second* — and
   NEG got the last rebuttal-side extension (1st FF) as the first-speaking team.
   The AFF's baseline-knowledge gap shows early ("Can you explain this K card by
   card?" [16:42]), and its cleanest theoretical answer (hypo-testing owes a
   competitive world) never gets built. Against a prepared K, speaking second is
   not the luxury it is in a policy round.
