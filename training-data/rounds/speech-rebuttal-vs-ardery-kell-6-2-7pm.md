One immediate legal hurdle: Can the government categorically prohibit an entire class of legal entities from participating in the real estate market?
Entin says that the U.S. <mark>Supreme Court</mark> has <mark>recognized business</mark> entities <mark>as “legal persons</mark>” since the 1880s.
“The government would need to have a pretty powerful justification for saying that some type of business entity cannot buy property,” Entin said in an interview.
While the court’s 2010 Citizens United decision specifically addressed corporate free speech rights in political campaigns under the First Amendment, the broader legal principle of corporate personhood extends back much further and touches property rights as well, Entin says.
The <mark>Fifth Amendment</mark>, which applies to the federal government, and the Fourteenth Amendment, which applies to states, both <mark>declare that gov</mark>ernment <mark>cannot “deprive any person</mark> <mark>of</mark> life, liberty <mark>or property without due process</mark> of law.” Courts have long interpreted “person” to include business entities.

<mark>A blanket prohibition</mark> on all institutional investors, by contrast, <mark>provides no</mark> such individualized <mark>process,</mark> says Entin.

What about sellers’ rights?
Trump’s proposal also raises questions about property owners’ rights to sell to the buyer of their choice.
While federal Fair Housing Act restrictions prevent discrimination based on race, religion, and nationality, those laws were enacted to combat systematic exclusion of protected classes—particularly African Americans who were locked out of entire neighborhoods through discriminatory practices.
Extending similar restrictions to corporate buyers presents different legal issues, says Entin.

A high-profile proposal to ban investors from buying single-family homes and other residential properties has stalled in Congress, underscoring how much harder such a policy is to implement than it may sound in theory. Lawmakers from <mark>both</mark> <mark>sides</mark> of the aisle <mark>are wary of sweeping restrictions</mark> <mark>that</mark> could <mark>unsettle capital flows, distort markets</mark>, and invite a raft of legal and practical challenges while doing little to address the underlying supply shortage that contributes to affordability problems.
The idea behind the proposal was to reduce competition for entry-level homes, giving first-time buyers a clearer path to ownership without competing against institutional or individual investors with deeper pockets. But translating that goal into enforceable policy has proven tricky. Questions about how to define an “investor,” how to treat entities like pension funds or small landlords, and how to enforce such a ban across private transactions with minimal federal oversight have bogged down discussions. Real estate markets are decentralized and governed by a patchwork of state and local laws, making a one-size-fits-all federal rule difficult to craft and enforce.
Critics also warn of unintended consequences. <mark>Restricting investor purchases</mark> could shrink the overall pool of buyers, dampen demand in certain markets, or <mark>push capital into less transparent corners</mark> of the market. Institutional buyers often provide liquidity and help stabilize pricing cycles; <mark>removing</mark> <mark>them</mark> abruptly could <mark>have ripple effects on home values</mark>, construction financing <mark>and</mark> broader <mark>market confidence</mark>. Moreover, defining exceptions for small landlords or retirement funds further complicates enforcement, illustrating that good policy intentions can quickly run into real-world complexity when markets are large and diverse.
