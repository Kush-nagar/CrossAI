# PF Debate Q&A — Beginner

## Second rebuttal vs. first rebuttal — what changes

Second rebuttal carries an added obligation: **frontlining** — defending your own case — which first rebuttal doesn't have. Budget roughly 1.5–2 minutes for it, and do it *before* attacking your opponent's case: your own case offense is the strongest offense you have in the round, so protect it first, then spend remaining time on turns/no-links/other responses to their side.

## PF speech times, order, and the coin flip

Constructive: 4 min. Rebuttal: 4 min. Summary: 3 min. Final Focus: 2 min. Each team gets 3 minutes total prep, drawn down between speeches. Three crossfires (3 min each): after constructives, after rebuttals, and grand crossfire after summaries.

Coin flip: winner picks side *or* speaking order, not both; loser gets whichever's left. Most teams take speaking order over side — a well-prepped team should be comfortable on both sides, and speaking second carries a small prep advantage (first summary has to collapse first, letting second summary react to it).

## How to flow

Two sheets, but divided **by flow, not by side**. Columns move left to right, alternating between the two teams' speeches, so each response sits directly beside the argument it answers — pro rebuttal next to con constructive, first summary next to con rebuttal (since you're frontlining against specific con responses), etc.

## Cut cards vs. paraphrasing

Paraphrasing is allowed, but only if the underlying card exists and can be produced on request — keep a properly cut copy on hand. Reading in cut format is usually better in tech rounds (paraphrasing exposes you to theory); paraphrasing is fine in lay rounds as long as you have the card to back it up.

## What "frontlining" means and where it happens

Frontlining = defending your own case, the first layer of defense against rebuttal attacks. It happens in two speeches: **second rebuttal** (answers first rebuttal's case attacks before attacking the first-speaking team's case) and **first summary** (answers second rebuttal's case attacks before extending responses on the opponent's case).

## PF vs. LD vs. Policy

PF vs. LD: PF is consequence-based rather than centered on moral obligation (LD's traditional focus), and PF is 2-person vs. LD's 1-on-1. PF vs. Policy: mostly a depth difference — Policy's longer speech times and full-year single-topic prep let debates develop in far more detail.

PF isn't simply "easier" — the current meta is trending toward LD/Policy elements (theory, critiques, philosophical arguments), which arguably makes PF *harder* in some ways since its speech times are fixed and short, without room to develop complex arguments the way Policy/LD can. PF is easier to learn at the beginner level and a strong foundation for later LD/Policy, but not as easy as advertised at the highest level.

## Topic snapshot: data center moratorium (Sept/Oct 2026 NSDA topic — time-bound, will go stale once the topic rotates)

**Resolution:** The United States federal government should enact a moratorium on hyperscale data center construction.

Pro core arguments: (1) AGI risk — compute/data throughput from hyperscale centers enables dangerous misaligned-AGI development; a moratorium caps the scale. (2) Innovation/sustainability — a moratorium conditioned on safety/sustainability standards improves what eventually gets built, rather than just blocking it; this framing also answers a lot of con offense. (3) Grid strain — centers draw heavily on local power grids, with impacts from community harm up to large-scale grid failure. (4) Warming — construction is highly polluting and water-intensive, worsening drought/water insecurity where centers are sited.

Con core arguments: (1) Economy — AI is in a bubble; an abrupt construction halt shocks investors and triggers broader market confidence collapse. (2) Tech race — a unilateral US pause lets China gain ground, risking both Chinese emboldenment and a US overreaction to lost primacy.

# PF Debate Q&A — Intermediate

## How much of your case to defend in second rebuttal

Working benchmark: 1.5–2 minutes frontlining. If that only covers one contention, kick the other — that's correct, not a failure. Avoid spending 3 minutes frontlining; past that point you will drop something on their case. If you kick cleanly (concede defense, get out from under turns, answer every piece of offense generated off your case — turns, impact turns, straight turns), frontlining one contention and kicking the other is perfectly fine. Cap frontlining at roughly half your rebuttal so you retain time to pressure their case and their first summary.

## Is defense "sticky" in summary?

Judge-dependent and genuinely contested — check the paradigm. Most judges lean *against* sticky defense as interventionist (the judge shouldn't recover defense the team itself failed to extend), but the variance is real enough that this isn't safe to assume either way.

## What real comparative weighing sounds like

Magnitude alone is weak — nearly everyone reads an extinction impact, so the differential is small, and restating your impact scenario isn't weighing at all. Real weighing explains *why* your impact wins on a metric relative to theirs — not "we're fast," but "we're fast **because** theirs is slow, for this reason." Every weighing claim needs an explicit reason the opposing impact is slower/smaller/less severe/less probable. Weighing should start in **first summary**, not later.

## Handling a dropped turn on your own case

Answer a turn immediately in the next speech — a dropped, well-developed turn becomes conceded offense and is very hard to recover from. But conceding a turn doesn't end the round: offense can be outweighed. Argue your offense outweighs the (now-true) turn using your weighing mechanisms — smaller/slower/less probable than your offense. This work belongs in summary onward, since those speeches can't generate new frontlines against an already-conceded turn — weighing is what's left.

## Adapting between lay and tech judges

What changes: speed, jargon, how extrapolated/unconventional your arguments can be. What stays constant: persuasion and warranting, in both settings — don't treat a tech judge as license to be blippy (asserting without explaining), and don't treat a lay judge as pure presentation. Lay rounds should be more narrative-driven — group small technical defense into one catch-all response and prioritize being argumentative, persuasive, and consistent across speeches.

## What crossfire is actually for

Balance asking questions (clarify your position, extract concessions for later) against being asked (you get more response time than they spent asking — a chance to re-explain something unclear from your last speech). For opponents talking over you: use nonverbal cues first (facial expression/body language signal incoherence to the judge); if they run long, be insistent — note the time elapsed, take your question, hold the line. If they still won't yield, that reflects on them. If they're aggressive, staying visibly composed makes them look less credible by contrast.

## Building a prep file for a tournament

Cases finalized and drilled well before the tournament, with frontlines to every anticipated response, timed, strategy discussed with partner/coaches in advance. For blocks: **depth over breadth** — a large volume of weak blocks won't survive contact with a real opponent, and re-cutting mid-tournament is inefficient. Target the most common contentions, and specifically prep clean, evidence-specific indicts against the handful of teams whose cases get widely copied on the circuit. Keep blocks generic enough to stay flexible, robust enough to collapse on. Don't cut cards just to have them — minute/squirrely/irrelevant arguments aren't worth the prep time trade-off.

## Handling a miscut or misrepresented card

Read the indict **in speech**, not to the judge outside a speech. Point out the flaw explicitly, tell the judge what to do with it (e.g. "read the card after the round, it doesn't say X"). Delivery/conviction matters as much as substance for winning an evidence indict.

# PF Debate Q&A — Advanced

## When theory is actually strategic

Don't read theory in front of a lay judge. Theory is generally becoming less prevalent as debaters get better at answering it. Absent a genuinely egregious violation, most theory-shell problems could've been resolved before the round — reach out and ask a team to disclose, ask for a paraphrase correction, request a trigger warning as an accommodation if you're genuinely affected. Handling it pre-round is usually the better path than reading the shell.

## Meta-weighing (weighing between weighing mechanisms)

- Magnitude/severity/scope are typically treated as the same metric — responses to one usually apply to all three.
- Magnitude > timeframe: severity precedes urgency (we can survive a catastrophe, not an existential risk).
- Magnitude > probability: risk = magnitude × probability; if magnitude is effectively infinite (extinction), any non-zero probability produces unbounded expected harm.
- Timeframe > probability: a faster impact is less likely to be corrected by intervening actors.
- Probability > timeframe: speed is irrelevant if the impact never triggers at all.
- Probability > magnitude: hypothetical size doesn't matter absent a trigger; also, an unbounded set of non-zero existential risks exists — prioritizing all of them makes policymaking impossible.

## Are Kritiks viable in PF at bid tournaments?

Increasingly viable, largely because teams collapse to framework rather than the alternative — a team that only has to win a link, a theory of power, and framework functions much like a theory shell/independent voter, cutting the time trade-off. The PF meta has also built up a solid body of reasons the impacts underlying K framework outweigh. It genuinely wins at the highest level (the 2026 TOC champion read a K) — if investing in this, learn to debate framework properly; framework/procedurals matter more to master than case literature here.

## Turns + defense vs. pure collapse in final focus

Turns plus defense is generally the A-strategy, unless you're winning the weighing debate outright. Defense reduces the risk/probability of their impact; holding a clean turn plus defense usually breaks the round your way, since doubt remains about whether/how their argument triggers while there's no comparable doubt about yours. Turns and defense often pair directly — impact defense + impact turn shows their impact doesn't trigger *and* their advocacy forecloses a good outcome; no-link + link turn shows their link doesn't solve *and* actively worsens it. Collapsing on both together is usually right.

## Deciding what to collapse from rebuttal into summary

**On your own case:** go for what they under-covered (or conceded outright); go for what you can actually defend against the responses they made; weight frontlining heavily since summary has little room for new case defense; most importantly, collapse on whatever interacts most cleanly with their offense — even a conceded argument loses if they win the weighing debate.

**On their case:** sometimes kicking your own case entirely and collapsing on offense generated off theirs is stronger (e.g. if they badly mishandled a turn) — judges tend to give more latitude extrapolating a turn in the back half since it's usually under-developed in rebuttal. What to prioritize also depends on their burden: **against pro** (who needs uniqueness + impact for exigence), prefer collapsing on non-uniques and impact defense — solvency deficits/no-links are escapable via try-or-die framing. **Against con** (who generates offense off link + impact), a no-link is more persuasive than a non-unique. Terminal impact defense is underused and should be a default when available — if their impact doesn't exist, uniqueness and link being conceded doesn't matter.

## Topic: answering circumvention on the data center moratorium (time-bound — Sept/Oct 2026 topic)

Best pro response to "firms will just build offshore/states will preempt": firms lack the incentive to relocate — less bureaucratic friction domestically, and a domestic tech ecosystem far more conducive to data center investment than most alternatives; those incentives persist through a moratorium. Fight this on **solvency**, not uniqueness — circumvention is an argument about why the advocacy can't solve, so "the harm already exists" doesn't engage the warrant. Stronger version: the moratorium itself drives better-designed data centers that firms build once it lifts.

## Indicting a card that's accurately cut but methodologically weak

Do both, not one or the other: argue your offense outweighs theirs, **and** use the methodological flaw as an added differentiator (your research is sound, theirs isn't). Judges dislike shallow evidence-quality fights, but a substantively explained methodological indict (not just asserted) is fine.

## Choosing tournaments for a bid season

Geography is rarely the binding constraint — most regions have strong bid tournaments. Earlier-awarding bid tournaments (octas/quarters) are generally easier to bid at than later ones (semis/finals) — larger/stronger fields, but more margin for error since fewer elim rounds are needed. Budget-limited teams can lean on online octas-bid tournaments, which are common.

## What makes a good RFD

Give targeted feedback that says what a team could/should have done differently — not just a log of what went wrong. That's what actually lets debaters build on the criticism in future rounds. Be honest, don't sugarcoat — no need for rudeness, but a diplomatic non-answer helps a debater far less than plainly naming what went wrong and why.
