# Round Vision — General Framework

Round vision is meta-vision within the round: foreseeing how the round will
unfold and making strategic decisions based on that — like calculating moves
ahead in chess. It covers collapse choices, which arguments to go for, time
allocation, and predicting the opponent's strategy. Framed for tech PF but the
principles generalize across formats; apply them as *general heuristics* fit
to the specific round in front of you, not as a script.

The single most common failure is not lack of understanding — these ideas are
intuitive — but **failing to remember to apply them under in-round pressure**.
Coaching and drilling should therefore reward *recognizing and executing* the
obvious-but-forgotten move, not just knowing it exists.

## Core principles (apply in every speech)

1. **Path of least resistance.** Always pick the easiest route to the ballot.
   A dropped argument with clean weighing beats a contested argument you're
   winning on the merits. Before every back-half speech, ask: what is the
   cheapest thing I can win that is sufficient to win the round?
2. **Positive time trades.** Spend less time to force the opponent to spend
   more (the "elixir trade"). Weighing that filters out three of their
   arguments at once beats three line-by-line responses. Turns force
   frontlines. Efficient trades bank time — and extra time is margin for
   error.
3. **Keep multiple paths open early; collapse late and decisively.** Maximize
   offensive options in the front half (read turns, don't over-kick) so the
   opponent can't block your one path. Then collapse hard in the back half —
   indecision between two collapse targets, or a muddled half-collapse, loses
   more rounds than picking the second-best target cleanly.
4. **Close the door.** Don't just win your path — shut off theirs. Visualize
   their best final speech (they will usually telegraph it via time
   allocation), then preempt it: "even if they win X, here's Y that still
   blocks their ballot." Preempts that get conceded are among the most
   persuasive things a judge can be shown.
5. **Never underestimate weighing.** If they win any risk of offense and the
   weighing, terminal-sounding defense doesn't save you. If the weighing
   debate is live, invest in it; at worst it converts to "our case + the
   weighing."

## Front half (constructive + rebuttal): a game of time allocation

- **Round vision starts before the round.** Case construction should predict
  the common opposing arguments *and* the common responses to your own case
  (spikes/preempts). Scout: disclosed cases, past rounds, people who've hit
  the opponent or had the judge.
- **Triage by threat, not by order.** Distinguish time-sucks (thin, generic
  contentions they won't collapse to) from real threats (fleshed-out,
  multi-link contentions they plan to go for). Read minimal, turn-heavy
  coverage on time-sucks; dump diverse, hard-to-group responses on the
  arguments they actually want — and scrutinize that evidence for indicts.
- **Second-speaker rebuttal split.** Frontline your own case first, then
  attack; every frontline is one less response on their flow, so set the
  split (~2:00/2:00 in tech rounds) by which of their responses are most
  damning and which of your arguments you want in the back half.
- **Clever concessions beat brute force.** Kick arguments whose responses
  contradict the opponent's own case; concede a piece of defense that also
  kicks a turn. Know what to cut on the fly — debaters chronically
  overestimate how much they can cover.
- **Watch their time allocation.** How the opponent splits their rebuttal
  telegraphs their collapse. Tailor the next speech to that prediction —
  attacking the argument they invested in (or pre-covering the one they
  neglected) wrecks their planned strategy before they give it.

## Back half (summary + final speeches): where most round vision lives

- **Collapse selection criteria**, in order of importance: (1) most weighable
  offense with the best interaction against their weighing; (2) least
  mitigated — the flow with the least ink to answer; (3) the biggest
  skill/handling disparity — what they mishandled, you capitalize on.
- **First speaker gives the second speaker safety nets.** A summary that
  hands final focus one frontline, one weighing mechanism, and one response
  is fragile. Give multiple weighing mechanisms (timeframe, try-or-die,
  prerequisites, meta-weighing as a fallback) and a breadth of defense across
  different parts of their argument (uniqueness, link, impact) so the final
  speech picks whichever survived.
- **Final speech mirrors the summary.** Abandoning the first speaker's
  investment is almost never right. Deliver it as the judge's reason for
  decision — lines the judge can copy into the ballot. Defense gets a small
  slice; weighing and your own offense get the rest.
- **Salvage protocol (when behind going into the final speech):** collapse to
  ONE piece of offense and win the weighing overwhelmingly against everything
  they've extended. It doesn't matter how many pieces of offense they have —
  or how many turns went conceded — if your one argument outweighs it all.
  Little to no defense; near-new implications on extended material are
  tolerated in a salvage.
- **Preempt the predicted final speech.** If you speak first in the back
  half, name their likely closing move (from their profile and time
  allocation) and answer it before they give it — e.g., meta-weigh timeframe
  before they run try-or-die.

## Progressive rounds (K/theory): same principles, different surface

- The core ideas — path of least resistance, time trades, predicting their
  strategy — apply identically; don't miss the forest for the unfamiliar
  trees.
- **Find the highest layer** of the debate and win it; use it to filter out
  lower-layer arguments without line-by-lining them (the layered equivalent
  of weighing-as-filter).
- **You don't have to engage head-on.** If they overinvest in one layer
  (framework, standards), a strategic concession plus offense elsewhere
  (conceding framing to kick turns; a counter-interp instead of standards
  line-by-line; meta-theory that up-layers the shell) often wins the time
  trade outright.
- **IVIs (independent voting issues)** are usually blippy time-sucks — answer
  them cheaply, never zero-cover them (conceded, they can be terminal).

## Coaching and drilling implications

- The highest-value drill is exactly this: freeze a round right before a
  back-half speech, have the debater give their own version, then compare
  against what strong play looked like and why. Grade the *decision* (what
  they collapsed to, what they preempted, what they cut) at least as heavily
  as the execution.
- When grading, check the speech against the core principles by name: did it
  take the cheapest sufficient path? did it trade time efficiently or
  brute-force line-by-line? did it collapse decisively? did it close the door
  on the opponent's predicted play? did it leave the final speech safety
  nets (or, if the final speech, mirror the summary)? did it respect the
  weighing debate?
