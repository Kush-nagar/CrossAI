# Elim-Round RFD Lessons

Distilled from actual judge RFDs in two observed elimination rounds between
**other teams** (archived in `rounds/`, tag `bao-rounds`) — unlike the TOC 2026
R1 lessons (coach analysis), these are the judges' own written decisions, so
they are direct evidence of what judges credit and when:

- **Octas:** Westwood SP (AFF) vs. Seven Lakes EM (NEG), housing topic ("ban
  corporate acquisition of single-family residences"). Policy-style round;
  judge Cale McCrary voted **NEG** on the dollar DA with the advantage zeroed.
- **Quarters:** Harker LL (NEG) vs. Westwood SP (AFF), war-powers topic
  ("eliminate the President's authority to deploy military forces abroad
  without Congressional approval"). AFF read spark (extinction good) + animal
  wipeout; theory/IVI layer on top. **NEG won 3-0** (RFDs from Goyal and Ave,
  plus a panel-consensus overview).

Each lesson gives the RFD moment, the general principle, and how Cross applies
it in drill grading, chat coaching, and stress tests. The closing section maps
judge expectations onto each speech.

## Evidence comparison and speech timing

### 1. A clash-breaking evidence comparison has a deadline: the speech before final focus

**RFD moment (Octas):** The NEG rebuttal made a meta evidence comparison — its
Forbes evidence "cites experts holistically and is inclusive of 1AC housing
claims." McCrary lists the answers he'd have accepted (yours also cites
experts, a dates comparison) and then rules: "absent any of this occurring in a
speech prior to final I am treating this as conceded, clash breaking uniqueness
ev comparison." That single unanswered comparison zeroed the entire advantage.

**Principle:** When the opponent compares evidence quality on the round's
central uniqueness question, any comparison back — even a cheap one — must land
by the summary. Judges will not credit a first-time answer in final focus, and
an unanswered ev comparison doesn't just weaken the argument, it resolves the
whole clash against you.

**How Cross applies it:** Treat an opponent's ev comparison as a live-fuse
argument with a per-speech deadline. In drills and stress tests, flag any
summary that leaves one unanswered; in feedback, supply the cheap acceptable
answers (source parity, recency) the judge said would have sufficed.

### 2. "We explained this on case" is not a summary answer

**RFD moment (Octas):** The AFF's entire 1st-summary push on the dollar DA was
"I explained this on case." McCrary calls this the decisive error: because the
cross-application was never re-executed on the DA flow, the NEG's conceded
top-shelf push stood, and the judge filtered the whole uniqueness debate
through it.

**Principle:** A cross-application must be re-executed where the judge needs
it, in the speech where the comparison matters. Pointing at another flow leaves
the target flow conceded in the judge's eyes — especially fatal when the
opponent has an overview framing how that flow should be evaluated.

**How Cross applies it:** In summary drills, treat "see our case" /
"we answered this earlier" as a non-answer whenever the referenced analysis
isn't actually restated and applied. Require the comparison to be done on the
flow being decided.

### 3. A conceded top-shelf filter becomes the judge's lens

**RFD moment (Octas):** The NEG's 2nd-rebuttal top-shelf push — "only sudden
shocks to the dollar trigger the scenario," repeated as a summary overview —
was conceded throughout. McCrary explicitly used it "to filter the uniqueness
debate," which is why the AFF's Peter-evidence turn could not get charity in
1st final.

**Principle:** Overviews and top-shelf framing that go unanswered stop being
arguments and become the judge's evaluative lens. Every later push gets read
through them. Answer filters when they're read, not when they bite.

**How Cross applies it:** Track opponent overviews/filters as their own line on
the flow with the same extension discipline as offense. Flag any back-half
speech that leaves a live filter conceded, and in stress tests, have the
opposing side deploy exactly this kind of filter.

### 4. A turn sprung late gets no charity without its setup

**RFD moment (Octas):** 1st final revealed a "hidden straight turn" — investors
are already pulling out, so only the AFF solves. McCrary: "This comparison HAS
to be done in 1st summary for me to be as charitable to the turn as 1st final
wants me to be." He also refused to resolve the turn's contingency himself —
if the housing market is stabilizing (the NEG's won uniqueness), why does the
Marohn bailout scenario trigger at all?

**Principle:** A turn collapsed to in final speeches needs its uniqueness and
evidence-preference work done in summary, and it must survive in the opponent's
best world ("even if housing is stable, the turn still triggers because…").
Judges won't build the bridge for you, and surprise value doesn't substitute
for setup.

**How Cross applies it:** When a debater plans a late-round turn collapse,
check the setup chain: was the ev-preference established in summary? Does the
turn work under the opponent's uniqueness? Grade "hidden" turns without setup
as low-probability moves, not clever ones.

## Theory and layered debate

### 5. Judges default to drop-the-argument — DTD needs a warrant that survives "DTA solves"

**RFD moment (Quarters):** All three judges resolved the IVI debate the same
way. Goyal: whether the voting issue matters "comes down to whether DTA
solves"; the AFF's lexicality warrant was "not really warranted," and dropping
the NEG's IVI "resolves the abuse claims" — including the infinite-abuse claim,
since the offending team is still penalized. Ave, in four words: "Nonres theory
is drop the argument."

**Principle:** The judge's default penalty for a theory violation is dropping
the argument, not the debater. A DTD push must specifically warrant why DTA
cannot rectify the skew (and "they'd do it infinitely" loses to "they still get
penalized each time"). Absent that, time spent on DTD is time donated.

**How Cross applies it:** In theory drills and stress tests, evaluate DTD
collapses against the "DTA solves" test first. Coach debaters facing IVIs that
conceding a DTA-level penalty and winning the substance underneath is usually
cheaper than winning DTD.

### 6. A prefiat objection doesn't falsify a postfiat claim

**RFD moment (Quarters, panel consensus):** Westwood answered the agency
framework with an ableism indict. The panel: that is "a prefiat reason to
reject it but not a postfiat reason why it's factually wrong — so wipeout must
also be wrong." The factual claim (animals lack agency; agency is a prereq to
ethics) stood conceded, and the wipeout page fell with it.

**Principle:** Repugnance-style responses (this argument is ableist/racist/
exclusionary) operate on the prefiat layer: they can get an argument rejected
or penalized, but they do not make it false. If the claim also needs to be
factually wrong for your offense to survive, you need a separate postfiat
answer, or you lose the substance even while winning the indict.

**How Cross applies it:** When grading answers to framework/exclusion
arguments, classify each response as prefiat or postfiat and check the postfiat
box is actually covered. Flag collapses where a team's surviving offense
depends on a claim they only ever attacked prefiat.

### 7. Impact framing needs an articulated warrant, not an ethical appeal

**RFD moment (Quarters):** Both RFDs fault the AFF for never warranting why
pain and pleasure matter. Ave: the appeal to "sexism/racism by drawing lines"
is a "generic ethical appeal" that "doesn't warrant or contest need for
consciousness to be considered an agent... even at full strength I still buy
humans logically come first."

**Principle:** Analogy to an uncontroversial wrong is not a warrant for your
criterion, and it doesn't contest the opponent's. Win the criterion debate
directly — why does suffering confer moral status? why isn't agency required? —
or the judge resolves the layer on the opponent's conceded standard even while
granting your analogy "at full strength."

**How Cross applies it:** In framework-heavy drills, require an explicit
criterion warrant and an explicit contestation of the opponent's criterion.
Grade analogy-only pushes as unwarranted even when emotionally strong.

### 8. "Dropped" doesn't rescue an implication that arrives in final focus

**RFD moment (Quarters):** The AFF's "drawing lines bad" argument was dropped,
but its implication (it's a voting issue; drop the argument) appeared for the
first time in 2nd final focus. Goyal: "far too new and not really impacted
out" — so agency still mattered "above all else" despite the drop.

**Principle:** What sticks from a dropped argument is only what was implicated
when it was live. New implications, new penalty framing, or new weighing
attached in the last speech get discounted even on conceded material.

**How Cross applies it:** Reinforces the TOC lesson that conceded ≠ won: when a
debater banks on a drop, check the implication was articulated before the final
speech. Flag final-focus-only implications as new arguments, not extensions.

### 9. Mishandled layers are a time sink — judges notice where you should have collapsed

**RFD moment (Quarters):** Ave calls the AFF's final-focus theory push "a waste
of time... [they] should have collapsed elsewhere," then votes "cleanly on
case." The whole NEG case page also sat untouched all round — nobody's final
speeches used it.

**Principle:** Judges see time allocation as a strategic decision and will say
so in the RFD. Continuing to invest in a layer you're mishandling — instead of
collapsing to the page where you're actually ahead — reads as a decision error,
not just an execution error.

**How Cross applies it:** In round-vision terms: re-evaluate layer selection at
every back-half speech. When grading, ask "given how the last speech went,
was this still the right page?" and penalize sunk-cost investment in a losing
layer when a cleaner page existed.

## What judges expect, speech by speech

RFD-grounded expectations Cross should apply when grading or stress-testing a
specific speech (these compound with the TOC 2026 R1 lessons and the
cross-judge consensus reference):

- **Rebuttal (2nd especially):** the cheapest high-leverage moves are meta
  evidence comparison ("our ev is inclusive of theirs") and a top-shelf
  filter/overview on your best flow — judges carry both through the round if
  conceded. Frontlines here set what you're allowed to be charitable about
  later.
- **1st Summary:** the deadline speech. Ev comparisons must be answered,
  cross-applications re-executed on the flow that matters, opponent filters
  contested, and any turn you plan to collapse to must get its uniqueness/
  ev-preference setup here. Judges quote this speech's gaps as the RFD.
- **2nd Summary:** last safe place for new implications or penalty framing on
  live arguments; anything after is "too new." Extend filters and comparisons
  explicitly — "extended" alone keeps them alive only if the other side stays
  silent.
- **Final foci:** crystallize only. Judges refuse to (a) credit first-time
  answers to ev comparisons, (b) extend charity to turns whose setup wasn't
  done, (c) accept new implications on dropped arguments, or (d) resolve your
  contingencies for you. The judge's charity in finals is exactly what the
  summary purchased.
- **Theory layers, any speech:** expect DTA as the default remedy; expect
  prefiat and postfiat to be scored separately; expect the panel to call the
  layer a "wash" and look for who wins the cheapest substance underneath it.
