# Emerald AG RFD Lessons

Distilled from the judges' own written decisions across the 41 technical
circuit PF rounds in `rounds/emerald-ag/` (10 tournaments, 2025-26, including
TOC and multiple bid-tournament elim panels). Unlike single-round analyses,
these patterns recur across **many independent judges**, which makes them the
strongest evidence in this corpus of how the national-circuit pool actually
decides. Each lesson gives the recurring RFD pattern, the principle, and how
Cross applies it.

## Evidence integrity — the pool's most distinctive habit

### 1. Judges read your cards — a tag the card doesn't support zeroes the argument

**RFD pattern:** This happened round after round. A Sunvite semis judge pulled
up the full article behind a link card and ruled: "The Klare evidence does not
say the word 'dump' ONCE IN THE ENTIRE ARTICLE." A Sunvite octos judge zeroed
a terminal impact because the card "is not about human extinction... it
pertains to species extinction instead which puts the ! at 0." A Berkeley
judge: "I look at the Wolf ev and it doesn't say that — so I know it's urgent
but don't know when it will occur," and decided the timeframe debate against
that card. UKSO judges re-read gridlock and dollar-heg cards the same way.

**Principle:** In this pool, the card text is the ceiling on the argument. A
tag the evidence doesn't actually say isn't a weaker argument — it's zero, and
judges will verify it themselves in elims. Miscut, mis-contextualized, or
over-extrapolated evidence loses rounds that the flow says were winnable.

**How Cross applies it:** When a drill or round description includes evidence
whose card text is known, check tag-to-card fidelity before scoring the
argument's strength. In feedback, treat "your card doesn't say this" as a
round-losing risk, not a style note. In stress tests, have opponents recut
central link evidence.

### 2. Evidence indicts must be executed — judges won't do the recut for you

**RFD pattern:** A Sunvite finals judge, on a half-made indict: "I don't think
the evidence indict frontline is fleshed out enough for me to do that work for
you." A Sunvite octos judge coached the fix directly: "pull a quote... about
what their evidence actually says instead" of one-liner analytics.

**Principle:** Saying "their evidence is miscut" is an assertion. The indict
only scores when the actual text is quoted and the gap between tag and card is
shown. Judges refuse to open the card and finish the argument themselves.

**How Cross applies it:** Grade unexecuted indicts near zero. When coaching,
require the quote-and-compare form: read their line, state what the tag
claims, state what the card says.

### 3. Cards break ties against analytics

**RFD pattern:** A Blake octos judge, on an otherwise-even clash: "trigger
warnings bad is also carded, so I prefer it to break the tie." A TOC judge
discounted a load-bearing claim because it "was never carded" while the
opposing link had evidence.

**Principle:** When two positions are comparably warranted and extended, the
carded one wins. Analytics can beat cards only with visibly better warrants.

**How Cross applies it:** In close-call scoring, tip toward the carded side
and say that's why. Coach debaters to card the claims their collapse depends
on.

## Weighing

### 4. Timeframe belongs to the whole link chain, not the first link

**RFD pattern:** Sunvite finals: "a stock market crash might happen instantly,
but I don't think that means every part of the link chain happens
immediately." TOC doubles: "I don't get warrants for why I should prefer the
timeframe of a specific part of a link chain from either team, so I just
evaluate on which argument I think happens first." TOC R3 sharpened it: why
prevent "extinction in 2 months only for extinction to happen in a year?"

**Principle:** Immediacy claims are evaluated end-to-end. A fast trigger with
an unwarranted escalation chain loses timeframe to a slower scenario whose
full chain is warranted. Judges also expect timeframe comparisons to say which
*part* of the chain the claim attaches to.

**How Cross applies it:** When scoring timeframe weighing, ask whether the
speed claim covers trigger AND escalation. Flag first-link-only immediacy as
incomplete weighing, not won weighing.

### 5. Weighing that never interacts is a wash — and washes get decided elsewhere

**RFD pattern:** Yale doubles: aff says "more probable," neg "just repeats
their argument... I call this debate a wash," and the round was decided on a
dropped try-or-die framing instead. Sunvite finals (Raja): "if it was
comparatively weighed by the neg or you delinked this, I would have probably
not based my decision off of probability."

**Principle:** Two sides repeating their own weighing mechanism without
engaging the other's produces no winner on that issue; the judge moves to
whatever IS resolved — usually clean extended offense or an unanswered framing.
One real comparison ("even at their fastest timeframe, our probability
controls because...") captures the layer.

**How Cross applies it:** Score repeated-but-uncompared weighing as a wash
that forfeits control of the decision, and check what the judge falls back to
— that fallback is the real ballot story.

### 6. Try-or-die framing: dropped it's round-winning, new in finals it's nothing

**RFD pattern:** Yale doubles: "Aff goes for try or die, which is dropped" —
both judges made it the decision, one adding it retroactively answered the
neg's timeframe push. Emory R1, the flip side: "I don't think I can give aff
try or die given I think it was new in 1st Final and the articulation there
wasnt great."

**Principle:** Risk framings (try-or-die, "any risk of extinction outweighs")
are among the highest-leverage back-half arguments when introduced early and
left unanswered — and get zero credit introduced in finals. The pool treats
even weighing-flavored framings as too new at that point when thinly
articulated.

**How Cross applies it:** Track try-or-die as offense with extension
discipline. Reward early, unanswered deployment heavily; discount
finals-debut versions to zero and say why.

## Framework and layers

### 7. An agreed framing turns the round into a link race — win the comparative link

**RFD pattern:** Berkeley R5: "both teams agree that fairness is the most
important in-round issue — Emerald wins the accessibility weighing that
controls the internal link to fairness," and the ballot followed. Emory R2:
"everyone seems to agree fairness is the most important argument — the aff is
the only side that makes analysis as to why they have the strongest link."
Bronx R3, substantive version: "Extinction weighing is pretty much conceded,
so whoever gives me the first link into extinction gets my ballot."

**Principle:** Once a framework, weighing mechanism, or terminal impact is
mutually agreed or conceded, judges stop evaluating it and race the links into
it. The winning move is explicit comparative link analysis ("we have the
strongest/fastest/only link into the thing we all agree matters") — teams that
keep debating the framing itself, or that link in without comparing, lose the
race silently.

**How Cross applies it:** Identify the round's agreed layer first in any
back-half evaluation, then score whether the speech does *comparative* link
work into it. Coach the phrase-level move: name the agreement, then claim the
link race.

### 8. Conceded layer-ordering decides where the judge starts — and often ends

**RFD pattern:** Blake R4: "T uplayers killjoy, so I eval T first and only
look to the K debate if the neg does not win T." Blake octos: "theory uplayers
the K" (and the whole panel started there). TOC R7 named the error: "the neg
makes a mistake conceding the ROTB and going for the alt without articulating
why theory does not come first." Sunvite R4: the shell that's "a prior
question" got evaluated first because nobody contested the ordering.

**Principle:** Layer-ordering claims (theory first, ROTB, prior question) that
go unanswered are treated as settled procedure — the judge evaluates the
winning layer first and frequently never reaches yours. Investing the back
half in a layer you've conceded is subordinate donates the round.

**How Cross applies it:** In progressive drills, check the ordering debate
before scoring any layer's substance. A team ahead on a subordinated layer is
behind. This extends the existing highest-layer round-vision factor with its
failure mode: conceding the ordering while going for the lower layer anyway.

### 9. Circular frontlines don't count

**RFD pattern:** Blake R4: "cross applying K links on the T page doesn't work
because the K doesn't matter at all if the neg wins T — using the K to
frontline T is kinda circular."

**Principle:** You cannot answer a procedural objection to running a position
by invoking the position itself. Theory answers must be independent of the
argument the theory indicts.

**How Cross applies it:** When grading theory answers, check dependency: if
the frontline's force presupposes the indicted position is legitimate, score
it as a non-answer.

### 10. Self-contradiction is fatal — judges police consistency across speeches, partners, and conduct

**RFD pattern:** Three flavors, all decisive. Blake octos: "your CI makes zero
sense to me. You get caught violating your own requested norms, and then try
to wiggle out of it." Emory R2: the we-meet "relies on the idea that I should
evaluate text>spirit, which is something your partner explicitly told me not
to do in the last speech" — the judge pulled the trigger there. Stanford R2:
"This is a perfcon, you read data" (running an anti-quantification K while
reading statistics); Stanford triples: kicking an extinction contention
"doesn't magically erase that the initial operation was in big-scope impact
framing while now claiming that kind of calculus is illegitimate."

**Principle:** Judges treat a team's positions, conduct, and both partners'
speeches as one advocacy. A counter-interp your own wiki violates, a we-meet
your partner's framing forbids, or a kritik your own case performs against —
each is rejected outright, usually with visible annoyance. Kicking the
contradicting material late doesn't clean it.

**How Cross applies it:** In case stress tests and drill grading, run a
consistency pass: does any position indict something this team is itself
doing, reading, or has already argued? Flag it as an opponent's cheapest
round-winning press.

## Ballot paths

### 11. Presumption is where judges land, never where you aim — offense is looked to first

**RFD pattern:** UKSO triples: "at the point at which the aff is reading
presumption warrants in final, I definitely look to the team with extended
offense first." Judges DO presume — Berkeley doubles ("no one has offense, I
presume neg") and a Stanford quarters panelist ("I negate on presumption")
both did — but only after independently zeroing everything, and visibly
unhappy about it.

**Principle:** Asking for presumption while the opponent still has cleanly
extended offense reads as conceding the offense race. Presumption pushes are
credible only alongside terminal defense that actually zeroes the opponent,
plus a warranted presumption direction.

**How Cross applies it:** Grade presumption collapses against the opponent's
live offense first: if any clean extension survives, the presumption path
scores as a losing move regardless of its internal logic.

### 12. Stacked non-uniques gift the opponent a kick-out

**RFD pattern:** Bronx R3, as direct coaching in the RFD: "Probably not smart
to read so many non-uniques, because they can concede to those to kick out of
the turns."

**Principle:** Non-unique responses and turns are in tension on the same flow:
the opponent can concede your non-uniques to kick the contention and escape
your turns. Rebuttal strategy has to pick which one the flow is being set up
for.

**How Cross applies it:** In rebuttal drills, check response-mix coherence on
each flow — turns paired with heavy non-unique stacks get flagged as
self-defeating setup, with the kick-out named as the opponent's likely move
(this is also a round-vision prediction).

## Judge-pool profile (for judge-fit scoring)

Cross-cutting traits of this pool, for calibrating judge-adaptation factors
when no individual paradigm is given:

- **Panels split on lenses, not usually on the flow.** Elim panels (Berkeley
  doubles, Emory doubles/octos, Sunvite finals, Stanford quarters) often
  split 2-1 where the dissenter used a different weighing lens (probability
  vs. timeframe) or was the self-identified lay judge. Safety nets across
  multiple weighing mechanisms are what win panels — one judge on a panel
  voting on your second mechanism is the margin.
- **Concessions are absolute:** "functionally conceded," "extended through
  ink," and "100% risk" language recurs — this pool gives full weight to
  clean concessions and zero charity to late repair.
- **They coach in RFDs:** most decisions name the specific cheaper move that
  would have flipped the ballot (kick the case, contest the ordering, quote
  the card). Cross should surface moves at that same specificity.
- **Conduct is noticed:** at least one panelist explicitly weighed
  condescension in crossfire against an otherwise-technical team. Rudeness
  is a real cost with lay panelists on bubble/elim panels.
