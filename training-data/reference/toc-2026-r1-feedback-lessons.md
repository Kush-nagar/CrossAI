# TOC 2026 Round 1 Feedback Lessons

Coach analysis of two TOC 2026 Round 1 flights between **other teams** — these are
observed rounds studied as teaching material, not rounds the debater being coached
was in. Full feedback docs and flows are archived in `rounds/` (tag `toc-2026`).

Both flights were debated on the **April 2026 Public Forum topic** (presidential
war powers).

- **R1F1:** Acton-Boxborough ES (NEG) vs. Fairmont Prep CS (AFF) — policy-style
  disad debate (PTX, deterrence, Estonia, time-travel contention).
  **AFF (Fairmont) won.**
- **R1F2:** Milpitas XS (AFF) vs. Lawrenceville CM (NEG) — Kant framework debate
  (constitutivism, omnilateral will vs. utilitarianism). **NEG (Lawrenceville) won.**

Each lesson below gives the round moment, the general principle, and how Cross
should apply it in feedback, stress tests, and drill grading.

## Weighing

### 1. Weighing must be warranted, and must describe the impact you're collapsing to

**Round moment (R1F1 NEG):** The NEG's weighing push was diverse but asserted —
claims like "our timeframe is immediate" with no warranting. Fiat makes the *link*
immediate; it says nothing about the *impact's* timeframe. If the impact is what
you're weighing, a link-property dressed up as impact weighing is a mismatch.

**Principle:** A weighing claim needs a mechanism, and the property being weighed
must actually belong to the terminal impact in the collapse. Check: "does this
weighing sentence describe the thing I'm asking the judge to vote on?"

**How Cross applies it:** In drill grading and feedback, flag weighing that is
asserted without a warrant, and separately flag weighing whose property
(timeframe/probability/magnitude) attaches to the link chain rather than the
collapsed impact. Both make the weighing hollow even if "conceded."

### 2. Don't defend your weighing with a lone metaweighing claim

**Round moment (R1F1 NEG):** The NEG made one metaweighing claim while conceding
the AFF's individual weighing claims outright. Metaweighing is efficient — it can
filter out whole pieces of offense — but pinning everything on it is fragile,
especially for the team speaking **first** in final focus: the 2nd final focus got
to extend all its conceded weighing and then spend abundant time on the single
metaweighing claim, ending the round vastly ahead.

**Principle:** Protect the earlier final speech by contesting the opponent's
individual weighing claims, *even with blippy responses*. Contestation forces the
later speech to do work everywhere; concession lets it consolidate.

**How Cross applies it:** Treat "metaweighing only + conceded individual weighing"
as a significant back-half error for whichever team lacks the last word. Blippy
contestation of individual weighing claims is better than none.

### 3. Weigh between pieces of offense, not just between frameworks

**Round moment (R1F2 AFF):** In a Kant vs. util round where the NEG went for
offense on two layers, the AFF weighed frameworks but never weighed between pieces
of offense under those frameworks. The NEG's many routes to the ballot were all
underdeveloped — offense-level weighing was the cheapest way to avoid being spread
thin.

**Principle:** When the opponent has multiple ballot routes (across or within
layers), weighing between *pieces of offense* is a filter that shrinks your
back-half burden. Cover all bases: framework weighing AND offense weighing.

**How Cross applies it:** In progressive/layered rounds, ask whether the debater
weighed within layers, not just between them. Recommend offense-level weighing as
the anti-spread-thin tool.

## Answering arguments

### 4. Resolve the evidence question — don't pivot around it

**Round moment (R1F1 NEG):** The only credible AFF defense on a NEG contention was
"your link has no evidence — the cited website is broken." The implication of that
response is "treat the link as an analytic." The winning answer: *even if the link
is analytical, it is warranted and has no defense against it, so the concession
makes it true.* Instead the NEG answered "time travel is fiated" (i.e., no other
way for the resolution to occur), which the AFF could answer with "no evidence it's
possible" — the evidence question stayed unresolved, and it was the whole dispute.

**Principle:** Identify what a response actually implicates. If the dispute
reduces to an evidence-credibility question, your answer must resolve *that
question* — usually via an even-if reframe ("even if analytic, it's warranted and
undefended") — not shift to a different consideration that leaves the core dispute
standing.

**How Cross applies it:** When grading answers to evidence indicts, check whether
the answer resolves the credibility question or merely changes the subject.
Credit even-if reframes that neutralize the indict; penalize collapsing to an
argument whose central dispute was left unresolved.

### 5. Distribute rebuttal coverage by threat, not by flow order

**Round moment (R1F1 AFF):** Rebuttal overcovered NEG contentions 1–2, slightly
undercovered the 3rd, and barely covered the 4th — which was the squirrelly one
(time travel) and therefore the biggest threat if under-answered. Fully
neutralizing two of four arguments looks appealing, but undercovering the tail
makes it trivial for the opponent to collapse there and win the time tradeoff.

**Principle:** Squirrelly arguments are major threats precisely because standard
answers don't apply cleanly. Consider going bottom-up on the opponent's case, or
watch the timer to split time more evenly across arguments. Even coverage beats
overkill on the front half of the flow.

**How Cross applies it:** In rebuttal drills, evaluate the *distribution* of
coverage against the threat level of each argument, not just total coverage.
Overcoverage of already-dead arguments plus a barely-touched squirrelly contention
is a flagged pattern.

### 6. Frontline your own offense line-by-line — buzzwords don't answer responses

**Round moment (R1F2 AFF):** The AFF was truth-level ahead on Kant but frontlined
its offense with buzzwords, hand-waving away NEG responses that "seemed silly."
Specific responses went unanswered — "Congress isn't a reflection of the will of
the people," "500 members of Congress aren't distinct from 1 President."

**Principle:** Extending framework can be thematic; **frontlining your own offense
must be line-by-line.** Truth-level advantage doesn't excuse skipping specific
responses — under tech judging, an unanswered response is won by the opponent no
matter how silly it seems.

**How Cross applies it:** When grading frontlines, list the opponent's specific
responses and check each got an actual answer. Buzzword-density in place of LBL on
one's own offense is a distinct error from general vagueness.

## Back-half discipline

### 7. Conceded ≠ worth extending: filter responses through win conditions

**Round moment (R1F1 AFF):** 2nd final focus spent time extending a technically
conceded indict of the NEG's Brazil empiric — a response that contested neither
the NEG's link nor their weighing, and therefore contributed nothing to any win
condition.

**Principle:** When deciding what to extend in the back half, start from your win
conditions and ask whether each response advances one. "It was conceded" is not a
reason to go for a response; it's only a reason the response would stick *if* it
mattered.

**How Cross applies it:** For every back-half extension, trace it to a win
condition. Extensions with no path to the ballot are wasted time even when
conceded — grade them as errors, not neutral filler.

### 8. Every back-half response needs a ballot implication — and know defense from offense

**Round moment (R1F2 NEG):** Confused by the framework debate, the NEG threw
everything at the wall in rebuttal — a fine strategy there (conserves prep,
stretches the summary thin). But the back half never slowed down to ask what each
response *means for the ballot*. Example: the "turn" that Kant collapses to
consequences looks like a turn because it shows a contradiction, but its actual
implication is just defense — "their framework collapses to ours."

**Principle:** Throw-everything is a rebuttal-only license. From summary onward,
if you can't articulate why winning a response wins you the debate, don't go for
it — even if you're winning it, it won't matter in the evaluation. And label
implications honestly: a contradiction is usually defense, not offense.

**How Cross applies it:** In back-half drills, require an implication for each
extended response ("this wins because…"). Flag mislabeled turns that are actually
defense, and flag time spent on winning-but-irrelevant responses.

## Cross-examination

### 9. When confused, use cross for clarification — not gotchas

**Round moment (R1F2 NEG):** Lacking baseline Kant knowledge, the NEG spent cross
trying to catch the AFF on technicalities. That can buy ethos, but in a technical
debate it doesn't move the evaluation — and when the opponent knows the material
better, they're more likely to be right than you are.

**Principle:** Cross is a resource. If you don't understand the intricacies of the
opponent's position, spend cross asking as many clarification questions as
possible — that's what makes your back-half responses land. Gotcha-hunting in a
debate you don't fully understand is ethos-only value.

**How Cross applies it:** When evaluating cross strategy (or advising before a
technical round), recommend clarification-first cross whenever the debater is
behind on baseline knowledge. Treat gotcha-focused cross in that situation as a
strategic error, not a win.
