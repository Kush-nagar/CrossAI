# Core Debate Concepts

## Why Fairness Outweighs

There are four reasons fairness outweighs other impacts:

1. **The Paradox.** Every argument requires fair evaluation, so discounting fairness is paradoxical — you can't use the ballot to devalue fairness without relying on the ballot being a fair mechanism in the first place.
2. **Ballot Proximity.** The ballot can vote against unfair arguments, which rectifies abuse in this round — but one ballot can't shape community norms or solve out-of-round racism. This is a double-edged standard: it's why fairness violations are ballot-solvable, but also why accessibility/out-of-round impacts often are *not*.
3. **The Game.** Opponents subscribe to the game too, whether they admit it or not — evidenced by them going for dropped arguments, spreading instead of discussing, and preffing judges specifically to fairly evaluate arguments. Their in-round behavior concedes that fairness matters.
4. **Access.** Procedural fairness breeds empathy and inclusion for minority debaters; the inverse (unpredictable, unfair rounds) creates cynicism and burnout when debaters can never win against unfair arguments. This means fairness and access aren't opposed — fairness access-turns accessibility arguments.

## What is a Kritik?

A K is a type of progressive argument that criticizes the epistemological or philosophical underpinnings of an argument rather than its material implications. Kritiks are typically composed of five parts: **Theory of Power, Link, Alt, Impact, and Framework.**

## Best Responses to Accessibility Arguments

- **Fairness turns accessibility.** Procedural fairness breeds empathy and inclusion for minority debaters; the inverse creates cynicism and burnout when debaters can never win against unfair arguments.
- **Not ballot proximal.** Racism happens out of rounds; structural inequities are alt causes a single ballot can't solve.
- **Judge/Tab should evaluate on ethical truths, even if Access outweighs.** Anything else justifies voting for racist-but-technically-strategic arguments and causes false accusations to proliferate in the debate community, desensitizing it to racial violence — both turn their own offense.
- **Guilt Assuasion.** Attempting to resolve accessibility in-round does nothing but act as a racial alibi for judges outside of round, breeding ignorance that turns their spillover claim.
- **Double Down.** Debate's competitive incentive prompts debaters to double down on the problematic assumption for the sake of the ballot.
- **Community Backlash.** Identity/ethics-based arguments get antagonized by debaters on the circuit, victim-blaming minority debaters outside of round — which outweighs on scope.
- **False Positives.** Time constraints conflate technical concessions with ethical mistakes, meaning out-of-round discussion solves better — presume every claim about the team's assumptions false absent proof.

## Responding to a Constructive Argument You Know They Won't Go For

Punish subpar case construction: pair straight-turning/impact-turning their offense with No Links and Impact Defense respectively — this makes it impossible for them to kick out of, forcing them to rely on inadequate case offense against your fleshed-out case turns. Two scenarios follow:

- **They go for their throwaway contention anyway.** You win because they have to overextrapolate bad evidence or a poorly constructed scenario. Extend a couple of pieces of solid, terminal defense read in rebuttal. In rebuttal, prioritize *quality* over *quantity* of responses on these — you want them stuck on the worse argument, beatable with one high-quality response, freeing up the rest of your rebuttal to dissuade them from pivoting to their stronger contention.
- **They frontline the throwaway and go for their stronger contention.** This spreads them thin across two contentions. Either go for an undercovered turn on the throwaway, or — since they under-covered their stronger contention — extend a conceded or mishandled piece of defense there instead.

## What is a Link-In?

A link-in (sometimes used interchangeably with "turns case") is a weighing mechanism that lets you garner offense off part of your opponent's contention — explaining why your argument either accesses theirs, or has its own terminal impact reachable through theirs.

Example (climate change vs. nuclear war): a debater reading an internal link to climate change links in by arguing climate change magnifies resource conflicts, diversionary wars, and great power competition, which escalates nuclearly. A debater reading nuclear war links in by arguing wars in general worsen warming, since industrialization increases during conflict.

## Weighing Mechanisms

- **Magnitude** — how large the impact is.
- **Severity** — how severe a threat the scenario is (e.g., nuclear war vs. povery/starvation; nuclear states vs. non-nuclear states — nuclear conflicts are more severe because they're more likely to cause more deaths, pain, and escalation).
- **Scope** — how many actors/geographic areas the impact encompasses.
- **Timeframe** — how quickly the argument triggers.
- **Probability** — how likely the argument is to trigger.

Disclaimer: magnitude, severity, and scope are often used interchangeably, though technically distinct — magnitude is roughly "how many people," scope is geographic scale, severity is threat level.

## Pre-Round Preparation

1. **Manage nerves first.** High nerves obscure judgment. Drink water, eat a snack, talk with friends, use the restroom, or listen to music before a stressful round.
2. **Prep for the specific round.** Read judge paradigms (circuit friends, judging record, past experience). Scan opponents' Caselist Wiki for broken positions and estimate likely arguments based on the judging panel and their prior strategy.
   - Prep out threatening teams the night *before* elims, when most debaters have already broken most positions on the wiki — this frees more pre-round time for in-round strategy discussion.
3. **Talk through the back half with your partner/coach.** This is the most important step. Review opponents' round reports for their favored arguments, and think through how your constructive arguments interact with theirs. Build a decision tree: if they go for argument A in the back half, what contention do we go for or what response do we extend? If they respond a certain way, should we still go for this contention? Useful both pre-tournament and refined further in each pre-round based on the judge panel/newly broken arguments.

## What Makes Evidence Good

- **Specificity.** Delineated warrants and emphasized specific metrics let you leverage precise lines of evidence against responses.
- **Qualifications.** Institutional credibility matters (e.g., Brookings Institute over a random blog post).
- **Quality.** Meta-analyses, longitudinal studies, and aggregate polls (large data sets) generally beat narrowly measured quantitative data. Evidence with explicit lines explaining its own uniqueness (e.g., "traditional studies don't assume X") is strategic leverage in evidence comparison/tiebreaks.

## Choosing a Contention's Scenario

- **Specificity** avoids biting generic defense — a specific impact scenario (e.g., resource wars off warming, not warming generally) dodges broad impact defense. A unique metric, explained as the most indicative of a trend, also answers uniqueness responses. "This defense isn't about our specific scenario" can work outright if the contention is specific enough.
- **Inherent weighing intuition.** Some scenarios are inherently easier to weigh than others (e.g., an Economy DA relying on fast investor pullout off AFF perception vs. a Warming contention). Cut scenarios with a strong weighing-literature base so weighing can be hidden in the case and blown up/flagged in summary.
- **Opposite-side interaction.** Notice common assumptions/trends across the other side's contentions (shared solvency mechanism, shared internal-link scenario) and choose a scenario that top-shelf answers the majority of them.
- **Avoid pure stock-chasing.** Outlandish scenarios have sparse literature and often lose to common-sense analytics or a semi-competent prep-out. Prefer scenarios central to the topic's core controversy — yes, there may be more top-shelf responses, but they can be preempted/frontlined in advance for a stable "A" strategy. Contestation is the point of debate.

## Effective Cross-Fire Questions

Two main types:

- **Clarification.** Pin down an opponent's narrative (making it harder to shift out of later) or genuinely understand a confusing argument. Use answers to ask follow-ups — if an argument's justification doesn't hold up on request, that's often revealing.
- **Concession.** Force a specific concession ("Is the alternative conditional?" "Can we agree extinction outweighs?") that either removes some of their offense or sets up your back-half decision.

# Calibrated Example Answers (prep/practice framing — never in-round coaching)

These examples show the target tone, specificity, and structure for chatbot answers. Note the framing: all of these are **pre-round prep or standalone drilling questions**, never "help me respond to what my opponent just read mid-round" — that framing is out of scope regardless of how a question is phrased.

**Topic context: November/December — Resolved: The United States federal government should require technology companies to provide lawful access to encrypted communications.**

### "I'm building my 1AC on this topic — what's a strong Kritik I should prep answers for, and what's the strongest generic 2AR block against it?"

A strong Kritik on this topic is the Security K — it indicates that lawful access of criminals securitizes specific racial groups, which is racist. The strongest generic 2AR block is Framework plus the Perm, assuming they go for the alternative. "Clash outweighs and turns the K" is one of the best framework extensions: 3rd and 4th level testing teaches advocacy skills that decolonize the real world. A few K-framework responses are also strong here (guilt assuasion, judge choice in-round racism, etc.).

### "Help me pre-write a link-in frontline for my DA in case the AFF reads a case that could turn it."

Only we get link-ins: if we win the timeframe (TF) question, our case turns theirs before they can turn ours — and it makes their impacts inevitable, which non-uniques their turns-case argument.

### "This judge's paradigm says they prefer weighing in the constructive — how should I restructure my 1AC template to front-load that?"

Use specific brink-uniqueness evidence that articulates recent trends — this sets up the TF/Try-or-Die question well for the back half. Lines in your evidence delineating a specific date your scenario occurs also set up weighing. Find a quick internal-link scenario to extinction, since TF debates usually come down to the gap between the disad's link and the AFF's uniqueness. Go further and compare internal links directly: scenarios like preemptive strikes, use-it-or-lose-it, and quick paths to nuclear conflict beat slower internal links like economic decline or warming in TF debates. Scope also matters as a weighing mechanism beyond TF — an internal-link scenario touching a variety of actors (a global conflict vs. small proxy conflicts) is typically more severe and magnifies the risk of your internal link. Tagging matters as much as evidence choice: tags like "Now is the brink" or "a litany of actors escalate" help the judge flag your arguments as weighable early.

### "I want to drill against Accessibility arguments — write me a frontline that also handles a fairness concession, so I can practice delivering it."

Fairness outweighs — the ballot-proximity standard is terminal defense to accessibility: your ballot can only rectify in-round procedural skews, but one round fails to establish community norms and can't rectify out-of-round racism. Independently, judge choice solves in-round racism better even if accessibility outweighs — it preserves debater autonomy while ensuring genuine racists get punished, avoiding both voting for racist debaters who can outtech opponents, and false accusations that desensitize the community to violence.
