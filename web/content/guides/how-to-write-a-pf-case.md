title: How to write a Public Forum case
description: The four things every strong PF case does, how to pick cards that do double duty, and how to build a case you can actually collapse on.
order: 1
---

A case isn't four minutes of your best cards. It's the setup for the back half of the round. Build it so that, in summary, you could go for **any** piece of it and have the weighing, the defense and the evidence quality already sitting on your flow.

Good cases do four things.

## 1. Embed the weighing

The best time to win weighing is before your opponents know you're weighing. Pick cards whose warrants can be spun into a weighing mechanism later:

- A **uniqueness card that shows a brink** ("the system is at its breaking point *now*") becomes your timeframe and probability weighing in summary.
- A **fast link** ("the shift happens within months of the policy") wins timeframe against a slow disadvantage.
- A **specific impact scenario that still reaches a big number** gives you magnitude without sounding generic.

The weighing doesn't need to be in the tag. A card counts if its warrant can be *extrapolated* into weighing. Read your own case and mark which card is secretly your brink card, so you remember to extend it.

Standalone weighing cards are fine too. A card whose only job is to say "this impact is irreversible" or "their turn can't be unique" earns its seconds.

## 2. Hide spikes

Spikes are cards that answer the other side before they speak.

- **Defensive spikes** preempt the responses you know are coming. If every team will say "the actor just shifts to another platform," put the card that says they can't inside your contention.
- **Offensive spikes** interact with the other side's likely case: a link that also turns their most common contention.
- **Weighing spikes**, as above.

To find them, list the five responses you'd make against your own case and the three most common positions on the other side. Then look for evidence that answers them *and* still fits your story.

## 3. Win on evidence quality

When warrants clash, the judge looks at who said it. Prefer recent evidence, real qualifications and large data sets. Watch for **thumpers**: if your impact card is from four years ago, the other team can argue the scenario already happened without the impact.

Keep cards in their original paragraphs, cut them honestly, and tag them in the words you'll use to extend them. See [how to cut cards](/guides/how-to-cut-cards).

## 4. Leave yourself several ways to collapse

Give the back half options: more than one link into your impact, more than one impact, or a scenario that works off uniqueness alone. Stay close to the **stock ground** of the resolution. Stock arguments are easier to defend because the evidence exists on both sides and you've seen every answer.

## A quick self-check

Before you lock the case, ask:

1. Which card is my brink, my timeframe, my magnitude?
2. What are the three responses I'll hear most, and where are they answered?
3. If I collapse to only one link in summary, does it still stand?
4. Is any card old enough to get thumped?

If you can answer all four, the case is ready for [frontlining](/guides/frontlining) and a real round. Want to see what top teams ran on a topic? Every [PF topic page](/topics) lists the most common arguments by side.
